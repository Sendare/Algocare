Ectopic Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Ectopic Pregnancy

An ectopic pregnancy occurs when a fertilized ovum implants outside the normal endometrial cavity of the uterus. The vast majority of these implantations happen within the Fallopian tubes, specifically the ampulla region. Rarer sites include the cervix, ovary, and the abdominal cavity. Because these alternative tissues lack the stretching capacity and rich decidual support of the uterus, the growing embryo eventually causes the tissue to rupture, leading to severe internal hemorrhage. This condition remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and first-trimester maternal mortality in Nigerian healthcare facilities if not promptly recognized and treated.

Pathophysiology and Common Anatomical Sites

Normal transport of a fertilized egg depends on ciliary action within the Fallopian tube and smooth muscle peristalsis. Any condition that delays or obstructs this journey allows the blastocyst to implant prematurely while still inside the tube.

  • Ampulla: The widest section of the tube and the site of over 70% of ectopic implantations.
  • Isthmus: The narrow muscular part; implantations here tend to rupture early and violently.
  • Fimbriae: The funnel-shaped fringe end near the ovary, representing a less common tubal site.
  • Interstitial portion: The segment embedded within the uterine muscle wall, carrying a high risk of massive, catastrophic hemorrhage upon rupture.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Anything that damages the internal lining or muscular architecture of the Fallopian tubes increases the likelihood of an ectopic pregnancy. A major culprit in many clinical settings is pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), often caused by untreated sexually transmitted infections like Chlamydia trachomatis, which leaves behind scar tissue and adhesions.

  • Previous ectopic pregnancy: Substantially raises the recurrence rate in subsequent conceptions.
  • Tubal surgery: Prior sterilizations, reversals, or reconstructive procedures disrupt normal luminal pathways.
  • Assisted reproductive technology: Treatments like in-vitro fertilization carry an increased baseline risk.
  • Contraceptive failure: Intrauterine devices prevent intrauterine implantation effectively, but if pregnancy occurs, a higher proportion are ectopic.

Clinical Presentation and Symptoms

The classic triad of an unruptured ectopic pregnancy includes amenorrhea followed by abnormal vaginal bleeding and lower abdominal pain. The pain is often unilateral, sharp, or cramping, reflecting the distension of the affected tube. Once rupture occurs, the clinical picture changes dramatically. Blood spilling into the peritoneal cavity irritates the diaphragm, causing referred shoulder pain. Patients may present with generalized abdominal rigidity, dizziness, fainting, and signs of hypovolemic shock. A common clinical pitfall is mistaking these symptoms for a normal threatened miscarriage or acute appendicitis.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Rapid assessment is vital to prevent catastrophic collapse. The nurse must immediately evaluate hemodynamic stability by checking blood pressure, pulse rate, and peripheral perfusion.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management depends on the patient's hemodynamic stability, the size of the ectopic mass, and whether rupture has occurred. For early, unruptured cases in stable patients, medical management using systemic methotrexate is common. Methotrexate is a folic acid antagonist that stops the rapid cell division of the trophoblast.

Surgical intervention becomes necessary if the patient is unstable, if the ectopic mass is large, or if medical therapy fails. Procedures include a salpingostomy (removing the pregnancy while saving the tube) or a total salpingectomy (removing the affected tube entirely). Laparoscopy is the preferred surgical approach whenever the patient's condition permits.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Perioperative and medical nursing care centers on pain management, emotional support, and careful monitoring for complications. Nurses must administer prescribed analgesics and monitor vital signs closely for silent internal bleeding.

For patients receiving methotrexate, teaching must include avoiding alcohol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and folic acid supplements, as these interfere with the drug's action or mask pain symptoms. Emotional care is equally critical; patients grieving pregnancy loss require compassionate, non-judgmental support. Nurses must also verify Rh factor status and administer anti-D immunoglobulin to Rh-negative mothers to prevent isoimmunization in future pregnancies.

Complications and Emergency Response

The most feared complication of an ectopic pregnancy is tubal rupture resulting in massive hemoperitoneum and hypovolemic shock. Nurses must recognize early indicators of deterioration such as tachycardia, tachypnea, falling blood pressure, pallor, and restlessness. Emergency response protocols require immediate insertion of large-bore intravenous lines, drawing blood for cross-matching and rapid fluid resuscitation with crystalloids or blood products. Time wasted can lead rapidly to maternal cardiac arrest. Quick coordination with the surgical team for emergency laparotomy is life-saving.
