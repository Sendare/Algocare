Cancer of the Uterus

Introduction and Overview of Uterine Cancer

Uterine cancer primarily develops in the inner lining of the uterus, known as the endometrium. It stands as one of the most common gynecological malignancies globally and affects women predominantly after menopause. Early detection usually leads to a favorable prognosis because the disease often produces symptoms before spreading beyond the pelvic cavity. Nursing professionals play a critical role in recognizing these early warning signs and guiding patients through diagnostic pathways.

Types and Classifications

  • Endometrial Adenocarcinoma: Accounts for the vast majority of cases, originating in the glandular cells of the endometrium.
  • Uterine Sarcoma: A rare and aggressive form that develops in the muscles or supporting connective tissues of the uterus.
  • Type I Tumors: Generally estrogen-dependent, low-grade, and carry a more favorable prognosis.
  • Type II Tumors: Estrogen-independent, high-grade, include serous and clear cell carcinomas, and carry a higher risk of recurrence.

Risk Factors and Etiology

The development of uterine cancer is strongly linked to prolonged exposure to estrogen without the balancing effect of progesterone. Unopposed estrogen stimulation can arise from endogenous sources like obesity or exogenous sources like hormone replacement therapy containing estrogen alone. Other established risk factors include early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, tamoxifen use, and genetic syndromes such as Lynch syndrome. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus also increase vulnerability, frequently intersecting with obesity in metabolic syndrome.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Abnormal uterine bleeding serves as the hallmark sign of uterine cancer. In postmenopausal women, any spotting or bleeding, no matter how minor, warrants immediate investigation. Pre-menopausal women may experience heavy menstrual bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, or prolonged periods. As the disease advances, patients might report pelvic pain, painful urination, dyspareunia, or a palpable pelvic mass.

Diagnostic Investigations

  1. Endometrial Biopsy: The primary office-based procedure used to obtain tissue samples for histological confirmation.
  2. Transvaginal Ultrasound: Measures endometrial thickness; a thin lining typically rules out cancer, while a thickened lining prompts biopsy.
  3. Hysteroscopy: Allows direct visualization of the uterine cavity and targeted tissue sampling.
  4. CT and MRI Scans: Utilized for preoperative staging to assess local tumor invasion and distant metastasis.

Medical and Surgical Management

Surgical intervention forms the cornerstone of treatment for uterine cancer. Total hysterectomy combined with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is standard practice, often accompanied by lymph node sampling or sentinel node mapping. Adjuvant therapies, including external beam radiation therapy, brachytherapy, and chemotherapy, are tailored based on surgical staging, tumor grade, and histological type. Hormone therapy using progestins may be considered for patients with advanced disease or those desiring fertility preservation under strict specialist oversight.

Nursing Care and Complications

Nursing management encompasses preoperative preparation, postoperative monitoring, and supportive rehabilitation. Nurses monitor vital signs, assess surgical wound sites, manage pain, and watch for hemorrhage or deep vein thrombosis. Complications from surgery and radiation can include lymphedema, urinary incontinence, bowel obstruction, and sexual dysfunction. Providing empathetic counseling and facilitating support groups helps patients navigate body image alterations and emotional distress.
