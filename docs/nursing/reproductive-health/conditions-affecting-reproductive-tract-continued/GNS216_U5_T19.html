Uterine Abnormalities

Introduction and Congenital Uterine Malformations

Uterine abnormalities can be present from birth, stemming from abnormal development during embryonic life, or they can develop later due to various pathological processes. Congenital malformations arise from the incomplete fusion, improper development, or failed resorption of the Müllerian ducts (paramesonephric ducts) during female reproductive tract formation.

  • Uterine Didelphys: Complete non-fusion of both Müllerian ducts, resulting in a double uterus and often a double cervix and vagina.
  • Bicornuate Uterus: Partial fusion of the upper uterine cavity, creating a heart-shaped uterus with two distinct horns.
  • Septate Uterus: Normal outer uterine contour with an internal muscular or fibrous septum dividing the cavity.
  • Unicornuate Uterus: Development of only one Müllerian duct, leading to a solitary banana-shaped uterine horn.
  • Arcuate Uterus: A mild variation featuring a small indentation at the uterine fundus internally.

Misconception: A septate uterus and a bicornuate uterus look similar on a superficial scan, but their external contours differ significantly, which dictates whether hysteroscopic or abdominal surgery is required for correction.

Acquired Structural Abnormalities

Unlike congenital malformations, acquired structural abnormalities of the uterus develop during a woman's lifetime. These lesions significantly alter the uterine cavity and myometrium, often causing abnormal uterine bleeding and pelvic pain.

  • Leiomyomas (Fibroids): Benign monoclonal tumors arising from smooth muscle cells of the myometrium, categorized by location as submucosal, intramural, or subserosal.
  • Endometrial Polyps: Hyperplastic overgrowths of endometrial glands and stroma projecting into the uterine cavity.
  • Adenomyosis: The presence of ectopic endometrial glands and stroma deep within the myometrium, causing a uniformly enlarged, boggy, and tender uterus.
  • Intrauterine Adhesions (Asherman Syndrome): Fibrous scar tissue obliterating the uterine cavity, typically resulting from previous surgical trauma or infection like postpartum curettage.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Uterine abnormalities disrupt normal physiology through various mechanisms. Submucosal fibroids and polyps increase the surface area of the endometrium and interfere with the normal vascular compression needed for hemostasis during menstruation, leading to heavy or prolonged bleeding.

Structural distortions can impair sperm transport, prevent adequate embryo implantation, or restrict the space required for a growing fetus. Women frequently report dysmenorrhea, chronic pelvic pressure, and dyspareunia. Adenomyosis causes painful menstruation because trapped endometrial tissue bleeds inside the myometrium during menses, triggering localized inflammation and painful uterine contractions.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging Techniques

Accurate diagnosis of uterine abnormalities relies on a combination of clinical history and advanced imaging modalities. Transvaginal ultrasonography serves as the primary screening tool due to its accessibility and high resolution for assessing myometrial and endometrial architecture.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment strategies for uterine abnormalities depend on symptom severity, patient age, and reproductive desires. Asymptomatic lesions often require only surveillance.

Medical management focuses on controlling bleeding and pain using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, combined hormonal contraceptives, or gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists to shrink fibroids temporarily. Surgical interventions range from minimally invasive hysteroscopic resection of polyps and submucosal fibroids to abdominal myomectomy or hysterectomy for definitive care. Uterine artery embolization provides a non-surgical alternative by blocking the blood supply to fibroids.

Impact on Fertility and Pregnancy Outcomes

Uterine abnormalities can profoundly affect reproductive success. Distortion of the uterine cavity by submucosal fibroids, polyps, or intrauterine adhesions prevents successful embryo implantation and increases the risk of early pregnancy loss.

Congenital anomalies like a septate uterus are strongly associated with recurrent first-trimester miscarriages. Conversely, a bicornuate or unicornuate uterus reduces the available myometrial expansion space, predisposing the patient to preterm labor, fetal malpresentation, and intrauterine growth restriction. Cervical incompetence, often linked to structural defects, leads to painless second-trimester dilation and pregnancy loss.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nurses play a critical role in managing patients with uterine abnormalities through continuous assessment, emotional support, and structured education. Monitoring vital signs and tracking quantitative blood loss are vital during acute bleeding episodes. Nurses prepare patients physically and psychologically for diagnostic procedures and surgeries, ensuring informed consent is understood.

Education focuses on explaining the specific diagnosis, clarifying prescribed medication regimens, and discussing potential impacts on fertility. Nurses counsel patients on recognizing warning signs like severe pelvic pain, high fever, or hemorrhage that require immediate medical attention.
