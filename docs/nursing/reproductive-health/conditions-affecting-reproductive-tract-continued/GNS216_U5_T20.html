Endometritis

Introduction and Definition of Endometritis

Endometritis is the inflammation of the endometrium, which is the inner mucous membrane lining of the uterus. This condition most commonly arises from ascending bacterial infections from the lower genital tract. In clinical practice across Nigerian healthcare facilities, postpartum endometritis remains one of the leading causes of maternal morbidity following delivery, particularly after prolonged rupture of membranes or caesarean sections. While it frequently presents acutely after childbirth or miscarriage, it can also manifest as a chronic condition linked to pelvic inflammatory disease or retained foreign bodies like intrauterine devices.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The development of endometritis depends heavily on microbial invasion and patient susceptibility. The normal vaginal flora can become pathogenic when introduced into the uterine cavity under specific favorable conditions.

  • Caesarean section: The single highest risk factor, especially when performed after the onset of labor or membrane rupture.
  • Prolonged rupture of membranes: Breaks the natural protective barrier, allowing bacteria to ascend from the vagina into the sterile uterine environment.
  • Frequent digital vaginal examinations: Increases the introduction of lower genital tract organisms into the upper reproductive tract during labor.
  • Manual removal of the placenta: Introduces external pathogens and causes localized trauma to the uterine lining.
  • Intrauterine contraceptive devices: Associated with chronic endometritis due to continuous foreign body presence and low-grade bacterial colonization.

Pathophysiology and Classification (Acute vs. Chronic)

Endometritis is broadly categorized into acute and chronic forms based on the duration of the inflammatory process and the type of immune cells involved.

FeatureAcute EndometritisChronic Endometritis
OnsetRapid, usually postpartum or postabortionInsidious, lingering over weeks or months
Primary CellsNeutrophils infiltrating the endometrial stromaPlasma cells scattered within the stroma
Common CausesBacterial pathogens like *Streptococcus* or *Staphylococcus*Persistent infections like *Chlamydia* or retained products
SymptomsHigh fever, severe lower abdominal pain, purulent lochiaAbnormal uterine bleeding, mild pelvic discomfort, discharge

In acute cases, bacteria breach the mucosal barrier, causing intense neutrophilic infiltration, edema, and microabscess formation. Chronic forms feature a persistent lymphocytic and plasma cell infiltrate, often altering the normal cyclic regeneration of the endometrial tissue.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Patients presenting with endometritis exhibit a constellation of signs that reflect localized pelvic infection and systemic inflammatory response. Nurses must recognize these clinical cues promptly during postpartum ward rounds.

  • Fever and chills: Often the earliest systemic indicators of bacterial invasion and bacteremia.
  • Lower abdominal or pelvic pain: Ranging from dull cramping to severe tenderness upon uterine palpation.
  • Foul-smelling lochia: Vaginal discharge that turns purulent, dark, and exceptionally malodorous in postpartum cases.
  • Uterine subinvolution: Delayed reduction of the uterus back to its non-pregnant size, noted during abdominal palpation as a soft, boggy fundus.
  • Prolonged or irregular bleeding: Particularly prominent in chronic cases where the inflamed lining sheds irregularly.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Diagnosing endometritis combines thorough clinical assessment with laboratory investigations. Nurses play a crucial role in gathering baseline data and monitoring the patient's response to therapy.

A complete blood count typically reveals leukocytosis with a left shift, indicating an active bacterial infection. Endometrial biopsy is reserved for chronic cases to identify plasma cells under microscopy, while high vaginal or intrauterine swabs help isolate the specific causative organism for targeted antibiotic therapy. Pelvic ultrasonography is frequently ordered to rule out retained products of conception, which often mimic or perpetuate the infection.

During assessment, nurses systematically check vital signs every four hours, measure fundal height and consistency, and inspect the character, color, and odor of lochia or vaginal discharge.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Prompt antimicrobial therapy forms the cornerstone of endometritis treatment to prevent systemic complications like sepsis. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are initiated immediately while awaiting culture results. The classic combination regimen for postpartum endometritis includes clindamycin and gentamicin, which effectively cover both aerobic and anaerobic organisms commonly found in the female genital tract. Alternatively, broad-spectrum cephalosporins or extended-penicillins may be used.

In cases where retained products of conception are confirmed via ultrasound, surgical evacuation through vacuum aspiration or gentle curettage is performed alongside antibiotic coverage to clear the nidus of infection.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on monitoring recovery, promoting comfort, and educating the patient on self-care and prevention.

  • Administer prescribed medications: Ensure timely delivery of intravenous and oral antibiotics and analgesics.
  • Monitor vital signs: Track temperature trends closely to evaluate the effectiveness of antimicrobial therapy.
  • Promote comfort and positioning: Encourage early ambulation and semi-Fowler's position to facilitate the drainage of lochia and pelvic fluids.
  • Encourage adequate hydration and nutrition: Support immune function and replace fluids lost through fever.
  • Educate on hygiene: Instruct the patient on proper perineal care, handwashing, and frequent sanitary pad changes to prevent reinfection.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

When diagnosed and treated promptly, the prognosis for endometritis is generally excellent, with complete resolution of symptoms and preservation of reproductive health. However, delayed or inadequate treatment can lead to severe, life-threatening complications.

  • Pelvic peritonitis: Spread of infection from the uterine wall into the peritoneal cavity.
  • Parametritis or pelvic cellulitis: Extension of infection into the broad ligament and surrounding pelvic connective tissues.
  • Septic pelvic thrombophlebitis: Infection and thrombosis of the pelvic veins, which can lead to septic emboli.
  • Tubal factor infertility: Scarring and adhesion formation within the fallopian tubes, particularly following chronic infections or pelvic inflammatory disease.
  • Septic shock: A critical systemic response to bacteremia requiring intensive care management.
