Anxiety Disorders

Introduction to Anxiety Disorders and Normal vs. Abnormal Anxiety

Anxiety is a universal human emotion characterized by apprehension, tension, and sympathetic nervous system arousal in response to a perceived threat. While normal anxiety serves a protective, adaptive function—motivating preparation and survival—abnormal anxiety is disproportionate to the actual stimulus, persists long after the trigger is removed, and significantly impairs daily functioning. In clinical practice, distinguishing between everyday worry and a clinical disorder depends on intensity, duration, and the degree of functional disruption in social, occupational, or academic spheres.

Neurobiological Factors and Pathophysiology

The biological basis of anxiety involves complex interactions within the central nervous system, particularly the limbic system, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex. The amygdala acts as the brain's emotional alarm center, processing perceived threats and signaling the hypothalamus. Dysregulation of key neurotransmitters underpins these pathological states:

  • Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA): The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter; low levels or reduced receptor sensitivity lead to unimpeded neuronal excitation.
  • Norepinephrine: Often hyperactive in anxiety states, driving the physical fight-or-flight symptoms.
  • Serotonin: Modulates mood, sleep, and anxiety pathways; deficits are closely linked to persistent anxious distress.

Classification and Common Types of Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders encompass several distinct diagnostic categories, each presenting with unique behavioral patterns and triggers. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) involves chronic, excessive worry about multiple life events. Panic Disorder is marked by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks accompanied by intense fear of future episodes. Specific Phobias involve marked, persistent fear of clearly discernible objects or situations. Social Anxiety Disorder centers on intense fear of negative evaluation in social or performance situations.

Etiology and Predisposing Risk Factors

The development of anxiety disorders stems from a multifactorial interplay of genetic, environmental, and psychological vulnerabilities. A family history of anxiety significantly elevates personal risk, pointing to heritable neurochemical traits. Environmental stressors, such as childhood trauma, chronic adversity, or severe loss, can sensitize the central nervous system. Additionally, cognitive styles characterized by a tendency to catastrophize or perceive ambiguous situations as threatening contribute heavily to symptom onset.

Clinical Manifestations and Behavioral Assessment

Anxiety manifests through a combination of psychological, cognitive, and somatic symptoms. Psychological signs include feelings of dread, restlessness, and irritability. Cognitive features involve racing thoughts, poor concentration, and hypervigilance. Somatic indicators stem from autonomic nervous system overactivity, featuring tachycardia, shortness of breath, muscle tension, diaphoresis, and gastrointestinal distress. During behavioral assessment, nurses observe motor restlessness, trembling, avoidance behaviors, and speech patterns.

Psychiatric Nursing Assessment and Mental Status Examination

A comprehensive psychiatric nursing assessment for anxiety involves evaluating mood, affect, thought processes, and perceptual disturbances. During the Mental Status Examination (MSE), the nurse observes that thought processes may become tangential or blocked if anxiety reaches severe levels. Insight and judgment can be temporarily impaired when distress peaks. It is essential to screen for comorbid conditions, particularly depression and substance use, which frequently accompany chronic anxiety.

Pharmacological and Psychotherapeutic Management

Effective management of anxiety disorders combines pharmacotherapy and evidence-based psychotherapy. Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) serve as first-line pharmacological agents due to their efficacy and safety profile. Benzodiazepines provide rapid relief for acute severe anxiety but carry risks of dependence and sedation, making them suitable only for short-term use. Psychotherapeutically, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is the gold standard, helping patients identify and restructure distorted thought patterns and engage in gradual exposure.

Nursing Interventions and Crisis De-escalation Strategies

When a patient experiences severe or panic-level anxiety, nursing interventions must shift toward immediate stabilization. The nurse should maintain a calm, quiet demeanor, use clear and concise sentences, and reduce environmental stimuli by moving the patient to a safe, low-lit space. Never leave a highly panicked patient alone. Grounding techniques—such as deep breathing exercises or asking the patient to identify objects in the room—help redirect focus away from internal panic and back to the external present.

Complications, Co-occurring Conditions, and Education

Chronic, unmanaged anxiety leads to severe functional impairment, social isolation, major depressive disorder, and increased risk of substance abuse. Physical complications include stress-related gastrointestinal issues, cardiovascular strain, and compromised immune function. Patient education focuses on teaching recognition of early anxiety triggers, emphasizing adherence to medication regimens, and promoting healthy lifestyle modifications like regular physical exercise, adequate sleep, and caffeine reduction.
