Changing Concepts of Mental Health Care in Nigeria

Historical Perspectives and Traditional Beliefs

Long before orthodox medicine arrived in Nigeria, mental illness was viewed largely through spiritual and cultural lenses. Communities often attributed psychiatric symptoms to demonic possession, witchcraft, or ancestral displeasure. Consequently, care-seeking behavior directed families toward traditional healers, spiritual homes, and Islamic or Christian prayer camps. While these indigenous systems offered communal support, patients frequently faced human rights violations, including being chained, beaten, or starved in attempts to expel evil forces. Orthodox psychiatric care only began in the mid-20th century, starting with the Arochukwu asylum established in 1907, which later transformed into Aro Village Mental Health System, integrating the community into treatment.

The Era of Asylum-Based Custodial Care

The introduction of Western psychiatric care in Nigeria initially mirrored the British colonial model, focusing heavily on large, isolated asylums. Yaba Lunatic Asylum, built in Lagos in 1907, epitomized this custodial approach. These institutions were designed primarily to protect the public rather than rehabilitate the patient. Wards were overcrowded, understaffed, and heavily reliant on physical restraints and sedatives. Nurses in this era functioned mostly as guards, maintaining institutional order rather than engaging in therapeutic interventions.

Shift Towards Community Mental Health

Recognizing the limitations of isolated asylums, Nigerian mental health care began shifting toward community psychiatry in the late 20th century. Pioneers like Professor T. Adeoye Lambo championed the Aro Village System, which integrated psychiatric treatment into ordinary village life. Patients lived with local host families while receiving care, drastically reducing the stigma and social isolation associated with institutional walls. This shift redefined psychiatric nursing from mere surveillance to community engagement, home visits, and psychoeducation.

Legislative Reform and Policy Challenges

For decades, Nigerian mental health services operated under the outdated Lunacy Act of 1958, a colonial piece of legislation that criminalized mental illness and focused on detention rather than treatment. Advocacy from mental health professionals and civil society organizations eventually led to the signing of the National Mental Health Bill in 2023. This modern legal framework guarantees human rights for people with mental health conditions, establishes a department of mental health services, and prohibits discrimination. However, implementation remains slow due to poor funding, bureaucratic bottlenecks, and a lack of infrastructural enforcement across states.

Current Trends and Psychiatric Nursing Practice

Modern psychiatric nursing in Nigeria extends far beyond hospital wards into primary health care centers, schools, and workplaces. Nurses now utilize evidence-based interventions, therapeutic communication, and psychopharmacology management. Multidisciplinary teamwork involving psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, social workers, and clinical psychologists has become the standard of care. Despite these advancements, significant barriers persist, including a severe brain drain of nursing personnel, widespread societal stigma, and catastrophic out-of-pocket health spending for patients and their families.
