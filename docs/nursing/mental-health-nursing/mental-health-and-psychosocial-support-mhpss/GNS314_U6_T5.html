Intervention Pyramid for MHPSS

Introduction and Overview of the MHPSS Pyramid

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) encompasses any type of local or outside support that protects or promotes mental well-being and treats mental disorder. In humanitarian crises and complex public health emergencies, populations experience profound psychological distress alongside physical devastation. The Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) developed a tiered intervention pyramid to structure and coordinate these multi-layered responses. This framework ensures that limited resources are deployed effectively and that the diverse needs of affected communities are met without medicalizing normal distress. The pyramid shape dictates that foundational supports serve the majority of the population, while specialized interventions cater to a smaller percentage with severe conditions.

Tier 1: Basic Services and Security

The foundational layer of the MHPSS pyramid focuses on ensuring that basic physical needs, safety, and security are met. Psychological well-being is impossible when individuals lack food, shelter, clean water, and physical protection from harm. Interventions at this level are largely non-mental-health specific, delivered by humanitarian workers, administrators, and security personnel. Activities include securing human rights, ensuring safe aid distribution, and restoring essential community services. Nurses working in primary health clinics contribute by advocating for safe living conditions and treating physical injuries that trigger acute distress.

Tier 2: Community and Family Support

Tier 2 involves strengthening family and community networks to buffer against psychological distress. Disasters and conflicts often fracture traditional support systems, leaving vulnerable groups isolated. Interventions at this level reactivate social networks, support traditional communal coping mechanisms, and establish safe communal spaces. Activities include setting up child-friendly spaces, organizing community dialogues, and disseminating accurate information about available resources. Nurses often collaborate with local leaders and youth groups to identify distressed individuals who require reconnection with social supports.

Tier 3: Focused, Non-Specialized Supports

Tier 3 provides targeted support for the subset of the population whose needs cannot be met by basic services or community networks alone. These individuals experience persistent distress, grief, or mild-to-moderate mental disorders. Care is delivered by health workers, social workers, or trained lay counselors who have received specialized task-shifting training. Psychological First Aid (PFA), basic counseling, and structured psychological interventions like Problem Management Plus (PM+) operate at this level. Healthcare personnel in primary care clinics frequently manage patients at this tier through basic mental health integration.

Tier 4: Specialized Services

The peak of the pyramid represents specialized services for the small percentage of the population whose suffering impairs their daily functioning despite lower-tier interventions. Conditions managed at this level include severe depression, psychosis, severe post-traumatic stress disorder, and acute suicidal ideation. Care must be provided by mental health specialists, including psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, clinical psychologists, and psychiatric social workers. Interventions combine specialized psychological therapies with psychotropic medications. Resource constraints in many regions make access to this tier challenging, necessitating strong referral pathways from lower tiers.

Nursing Roles and Integration in Nigerian Healthcare

Nurses play a vital role across all tiers of the MHPSS pyramid, particularly within resource-constrained Nigerian healthcare settings. In primary health centers, nurses frequently spot early signs of psychological distress during routine consultations. Integrating mental health into primary care helps overcome cultural stigma associated with visiting specialized psychiatric facilities. Challenges in Nigeria include a severe shortage of mental health professionals, inadequate funding, and prevailing cultural beliefs that attribute mental illness to spiritual causes. Addressing these barriers requires task-shifting, community sensitization, and robust collaboration between orthodox healthcare providers and traditional institutions.
