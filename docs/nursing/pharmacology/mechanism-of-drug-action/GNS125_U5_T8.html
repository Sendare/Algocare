Adverse Drug Reactions

Introduction and Classification of Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is any noxious, unintended, and undesired response to a medicine that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy. Unlike side effects, which are often predictable and may even be utilized for therapeutic advantage, ADRs are typically harmful and require intervention. The Rawlins and Thompson classification system categorizes these reactions into two main types based on their predictability and dose-dependence.

  • Type A (Augmented): These reactions are dose-dependent, predictable, and related to the known pharmacological action of the drug. They occur frequently and usually have a low mortality rate. Examples include bleeding while on warfarin or hypoglycemia from insulin.
  • Type B (Bizarre): These are dose-independent, unpredictable, and unrelated to the drug's primary pharmacology. They often involve immunologic or genetic idiosyncrasies, carry a high mortality rate, and occur in susceptible individuals. Examples include severe anaphylaxis to penicillin or drug-induced rashes.

Extended Classification: Types C, D, E, and F

While Types A and B cover the most common presentations, pharmacovigilance frameworks expand the classification to capture chronic and delayed drug behaviors.

  • Type C (Chronic): Associated with long-term continuous use of a drug, such as analgesic nephropathy from chronic paracetamol abuse.
  • Type D (Delayed): Manifests long after the treatment has stopped, including teratogenicity like phocomelia from thalidomide.
  • Type E (End-of-use): Occurs when a drug is abruptly stopped, such as withdrawal seizures after sudden cessation of antiepileptic therapy.
  • Type F (Failure): Unexpected failure of therapy, often caused by drug interactions or resistance patterns.

Clinical Manifestations and Common Presentation

Adverse drug reactions can mimic common disease states, making diagnosis challenging. Nurses must look out for sudden changes in organ function. Gastrointestinal upsets like nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea are frequent, while dermatological signs range from mild maculopapular rashes to life-threatening conditions like Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Hematological abnormalities such as unexplained bruising, bleeding, or fatigue point toward bone marrow suppression. Neuropsychiatric symptoms, including confusion, tremors, and lethargy, frequently affect older adults on multiple medications. Recognizing these patterns early prevents misdiagnosis and stops inappropriate escalation of drug therapy.

Assessment, Risk Factors, and Vulnerable Populations

Certain individuals face a higher statistical likelihood of experiencing adverse drug reactions. Assessing patient history is the cornerstone of safe nursing care. Key risk factors include:

Risk FactorMechanism / Reason
Extreme AgeNeonates and older adults have altered drug clearance and metabolic capacities.
PolypharmacyTaking multiple drugs increases the mathematical probability of drug-drug interactions.
Organ ImpairmentRenal or hepatic disease delays excretion, causing drug accumulation and toxicity.
GeneticsVariations in hepatic enzymes alter the speed of drug metabolism.

Management, Treatment Strategies, and Emergency Response

When an adverse reaction occurs, immediate action determines patient safety. The first step is stopping the offending medication unless the reaction is trivial and benefits outweigh the risks. Supportive care follows, tailored to the specific symptom presentation. In severe anaphylactic reactions, immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine is lifesaving, alongside airway maintenance and intravenous fluid resuscitation. Antidotes may be administered if specific toxicity is confirmed, such as naloxone for opioid overdose or phytomenadione (vitamin K) for warfarin-induced bleeding. Documenting the event clearly in the patient chart prevents future re-exposure.

Prevention and Pharmacovigilance in Nigeria

Preventing ADRs requires meticulous clinical practice, patient education, and active reporting systems. Nurses must perform thorough drug histories, check for known allergies, and calculate dosages carefully, especially for weight-based pediatric regimens. In Nigeria, reporting suspected adverse drug reactions is coordinated through the National Pharmacovigilance Centre (NPC) under the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC). Using the NAFDAC spontaneous reporting yellow form allows healthcare workers to contribute to national safety data, helping identify rare or delayed signals that clinical trials missed.
