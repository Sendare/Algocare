General and Specific Antidotes

Introduction to Poisoning and Overdose Management

Poisoning management in Nigerian healthcare facilities requires swift identification of the toxic agent and immediate supportive care. Toxins enter the body through ingestion, inhalation, injection, or absorption. The primary goal of emergency care is to preserve vital functions—airway, breathing, and circulation—before administering any reversal agent. Healthcare providers must quickly gather history from relatives, witnesses, or look for empty containers brought to the clinic.

Definition and Mechanisms of Action of Antidotes

An antidote is a substance that counteracts, neutralizes, or reverses the toxic effects of a poison. They achieve therapeutic effects through several distinct pharmacological mechanisms:

  • Chemical antagonism: Binds directly with the toxin in the bloodstream to form an inactive complex.
  • Receptor antagonism: Competes with the toxic agent for specific cellular receptor sites.
  • Physiological antagonism: Produces an opposite systemic effect through different physiological pathways.
  • Metabolic inhibition: Prevents the body from converting a less toxic substance into a highly lethal metabolite.

General Antidotes and Supportive Decontamination

General antidotes do not target a specific chemical structure but instead limit absorption or enhance elimination of multiple types of poisons.

  • Activated Charcoal: Adsorbs ingested toxins in the gastrointestinal tract, preventing systemic absorption. It is ineffective for heavy metals, alcohols, and corrosives.
  • Intravenous Fluids: Enhance renal clearance of water-soluble toxins through forced diuresis.
  • Whole Bowel Irrigation: Flushes the entire gastrointestinal tract using polyethylene glycol solution, useful for ingested packets of illicit drugs.

Specific Antidotes and Their Target Toxins

Specific antidotes bind to or neutralize particular toxins, halting specific biochemical pathways. Nurses must recognize the correct pairing between poisons and their specific reversal agents:

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring in Toxicity

Continuous monitoring is vital because a patient's condition can deteriorate rapidly following an overdose. Nurses must perform focused neurological assessments using the Glasgow Coma Scale, monitor cardiorespiratory parameters every fifteen minutes until stable, and accurately measure fluid intake and output. Laboratory investigations, including electrolyte panels, arterial blood gases, and specific drug levels, guide ongoing therapeutic adjustments.

Administration Guidelines and Safety Precautions

Administering antidotes requires strict adherence to drug calculations, correct dilution, and appropriate infusion rates. Rapid intravenous push of certain reversal agents, such as naloxone, can precipitate severe withdrawal symptoms or acute hypertension. Nurses must verify the expiry date, check compatibility with intravenous maintenance fluids, and ensure emergency resuscitation equipment remains immediately available at the bedside.

Complications and Nursing Interventions in Antidote Therapy

Antidote therapy can occasionally trigger adverse reactions or secondary complications. For instance, atropine administration frequently causes severe dry mouth, blurred vision, and urinary retention. Nurses must manage these predictable side effects proactively, maintain a quiet environment for agitated patients, and watch for hypersensitivity reactions, especially when administering animal-derived antivenoms.
