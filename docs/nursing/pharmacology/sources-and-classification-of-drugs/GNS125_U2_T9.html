Drug Overdose

Introduction and Definition of Drug Overdose

A drug overdose occurs when an individual consumes a substance—prescription, over-the-counter, illicit, or traditional herbal preparation—in quantities far exceeding the recommended or safe therapeutic dose. This overexposure overwhelms the body's normal metabolic pathways, leading to acute systemic toxicity. In Nigerian clinical settings, overdose cases frequently involve accidental pediatric ingestion of household medications, intentional self-harm among adolescents and young adults, and accidental toxicity from unregulated herbal mixtures or self-medication practices. Recognizing an overdose early requires understanding that toxicity is a relative concept dependent on patient age, weight, liver and kidney function, and concurrent substance use. Emergency care begins the moment the patient arrives at the health facility, shifting rapidly from assessment to life-saving interventions.

Common Classes of Drugs Involved in Overdose

Different pharmacological classes induce distinct toxic syndromes, known as toxidromes, which guide initial bedside identification. The most frequently implicated agents in acute overdose include:

  • Opioids: Agents like tramadol, codeine, and pentazocine depress the central nervous system and respiratory drive.
  • Sedative-Hypnotics: Benzodiazepines and alcohol slow brain activity, causing profound drowsiness and respiratory depression.
  • Analgesics: Paracetamol is widely available in Nigeria and causes severe, delayed hepatotoxicity in high doses.
  • Psychotropics: Tricyclic antidepressants and antipsychotics disrupt cardiac conduction and lower seizure thresholds.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Toxicity

Toxicity occurs when drug molecules saturate receptors, block vital enzymatic processes, or generate reactive metabolites that destroy cellular structures. For instance, paracetamol is normally metabolized into a harmless compound, but an overdose forces excess conversion into NAPQI, a toxic metabolite that depletes cellular glutathione and destroys hepatocytes. Opioids bind heavily to mu-opioid receptors in the central nervous system, directly inhibiting the respiratory center's response to carbon dioxide buildup. Understanding these mechanisms clarifies why certain antidotes work at the molecular level to halt tissue destruction and restore normal physiology.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Assessment of an overdose patient requires a systematic approach focused on identifying specific toxidromes. Nurses must evaluate vital signs, neurological status, and pupil size immediately upon arrival.

Initial Emergency Management and Resuscitation

Resuscitation follows the classic ABCDE protocol. Airway patency is the absolute priority, especially if the patient has a depressed level of consciousness and risks aspiration. Nurses must prepare suction equipment and assist with endotracheal intubation if spontaneous breathing is inadequate. Breathing is supported with high-flow oxygen and bag-valve-mask ventilation when respiratory rate drops. Circulation checks include monitoring pulse, blood pressure, and establishing intravenous access for fluid resuscitation and emergency medications. Blood glucose should be tested immediately using a glucometer, as hypoglycemia can mimic or worsen neurological toxidromes.

Pharmacological Antidotes and Decontamination Strategies

When specific agents are identified, targeted antidotes can reverse toxicity rapidly. Naloxone is administered for opioid-induced respiratory depression, reversing sedation and restoring breathing. Activated charcoal may be given orally or via nasogastric tube within one hour of ingestion to adsorb toxins in the gastrointestinal tract and prevent systemic absorption. However, charcoal is contraindicated in patients with impaired airways or those who ingested corrosive substances. Whole bowel irrigation or gastric lavage is rarely used today and reserved only for life-threatening ingestions seen very early after exposure.

Nursing Care and Patient Monitoring

Continuous monitoring is vital because clinical conditions can deteriorate abruptly following an overdose. Nurses must record vital signs every 15 minutes during the acute phase, paying close attention to respiratory rate and oxygen saturation. Neurological assessments must track any changes in orientation, reflex activity, or level of consciousness. Intake and output charts are essential, especially when forced diuresis or renal clearance mechanisms are relied upon. Cardiac telemetry should be maintained for overdoses involving tricyclic antidepressants or cardiotoxic drugs to catch lethal arrhythmias early.

Complications and Preventative Education in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Severe drug overdoses can lead to lasting complications such as hypoxic brain injury, acute kidney injury, rhabdomyolysis, and permanent hepatic necrosis. Once the patient is medically stabilized, psychiatric evaluation is mandatory if intentional self-harm was the cause. Preventative education must be integrated into discharge planning. Nurses should educate families on safe medication storage out of children's reach, proper disposal of unused drugs, and the dangers of sharing prescription drugs or patronizing unregulated informal medicine vendors. Community outreach plays a vital role in reducing the incidence of accidental poisonings across Nigeria.
