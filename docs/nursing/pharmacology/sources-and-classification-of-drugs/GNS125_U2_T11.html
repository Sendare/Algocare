Drug Abuse

Defining Drug Abuse and Related Terms

Understanding the spectrum of substance consumption requires clear definitions. The terminology ranges from safe therapeutic application to chronic relapsing brain disease.

  • Drug Use: Consuming a medication strictly according to prescribed medical guidelines for a legitimate health condition.
  • Drug Misuse: Using a drug for a purpose other than intended, or in incorrect doses, such as sharing painkillers or doubling a dose without medical advice.
  • Drug Abuse: Intentional, excessive self-administration of a substance for non-medical reasons, typically to alter mood, perception, or consciousness, resulting in social, legal, or physical harm.
  • Addiction (Dependence): A chronic, relapsing brain disorder characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite harmful consequences, involving physical and psychological tolerance and withdrawal symptoms.

Commonly Abused Substances in Nigeria

Clinical practice in Nigeria reveals distinct patterns of substance use influenced by availability, socioeconomic pressures, and cultural acceptance. Cannabis remains the most widely cultivated and consumed illicit substance across urban and rural demographics.

Pharmaceutical opioids, particularly tramadol and codeine-containing cough syrups, have seen widespread abuse, prompting regulatory crackdowns. Alcohol is a socially sanctioned yet heavily abused psychoactive substance contributing significantly to trauma admissions in emergency departments. Stimulants such as methamphetamine (locally known as 'mkpuru mmiri') and prescription amphetamines are increasingly prevalent among youths and commercial vehicle drivers. Solvents and inhalants, including glue and petrol fumes, are frequently abused by street children due to their low cost and easy accessibility.

Neurobiology of Addiction

Addiction alters the structural and functional integrity of the central nervous system. Psychoactive substances target the brain's reward pathway, primarily the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, which originates in the ventral tegmental area and projects to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex.

When a person ingests an abused substance, it causes a massive surge of dopamine, far exceeding natural rewards like food or social interaction. Over time, the brain attempts to maintain homeostasis by reducing dopamine receptor availability and decreasing natural dopamine production. This neurochemical adaptation causes tolerance, requiring larger amounts of the substance just to achieve baseline pleasure. When use stops, the depleted dopamine signaling results in anhedonia, severe craving, and classic withdrawal symptoms.

Risk Factors and Vulnerability

Susceptibility to drug abuse involves an intricate interplay of biological, psychological, and environmental variables. Genetic predispositions account for approximately half of an individual's risk for addiction, influencing how enzymes metabolize substances and how receptors respond.

Environmental triggers include peer pressure, family history of substance abuse, childhood trauma, and socioeconomic instability. Unemployment, poverty, and community violence common in certain regions increase psychological distress, pushing vulnerable individuals toward self-medication. Mental health comorbidities, such as depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, frequently co-occur with substance use disorders, creating a bidirectional cycle where one condition worsens the other.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Detecting substance abuse requires sharp clinical observation, as patients often conceal their habits. Physical signs vary by drug class but commonly include pupillary changes (miosis with opioids, mydriasis with stimulants), unsteadiness, slurred speech, tremors, and poor personal hygiene.

Behavioral indicators involve sudden mood swings, withdrawal from family activities, unexplained financial difficulties, and chronic absenteeism from school or work. Nurses must conduct a thorough, non-judgmental nursing history using validated screening tools like the CAGE questionnaire or DAST (Drug Abuse Screening Test). Assessing vital signs, neurological status, and last time of use helps identify acute intoxication or impending withdrawal delirium.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Care for patients experiencing substance abuse or withdrawal demands a structured, compassionate, and evidence-based approach. The initial priority in acute presentations is stabilizing the airway, breathing, and circulation, alongside managing severe withdrawal symptoms to prevent life-threatening complications like seizures or delirium tremens.

Nurses administer ordered pharmacotherapy, such as benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal or replacement therapy for opioid dependence, while closely monitoring vital signs and neurological scores. Beyond acute stabilization, interventions focus on building therapeutic rapport, educating the patient on coping mechanisms, and encouraging participation in support groups or psychological counseling like cognitive behavioral therapy.

Complications and Public Health Impact

Drug abuse triggers cascading physical, psychological, and societal consequences that burden healthcare infrastructure. Chronic intravenous drug use drastically elevates the transmission rates of blood-borne infections such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C due to unsterile needle sharing.

Organ damage is frequent; long-term alcohol abuse leads to hepatic cirrhosis, while heavy stimulant use causes cardiovascular strain, hypertension, and stroke. Psychologically, substance abuse precipitates depression, psychosis, and cognitive impairment. On a public health level, drug abuse strains emergency services, increases interpersonal violence, breaks down family structures, and diminishes national productivity.

Prevention, Rehabilitation, and Policy in Nigeria

Addressing drug abuse effectively requires a multi-sectoral strategy combining law enforcement, community education, and accessible rehabilitation services. Regulatory bodies like the National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) spearhead efforts to interdict illicit drug trafficking, arrest offenders, and seize contraband.

Primary prevention focuses on school-based education campaigns, youth mentorship programs, and mass media awareness highlighting the dangers of substance use. Rehabilitation centers provide medical detoxification, psychological counseling, and social reintegration programs to help recovering individuals maintain sobriety. Nurses play a vital role in these facilities by advocating for policy reforms that treat drug addiction as a treatable health disorder rather than solely a criminal offense.
