Drug Administration Abbreviations

Introduction to Drug Administration Abbreviations

Prescription writing relies heavily on shorthand derived mostly from Latin terms to save space and time on medication charts. While these short forms streamline clinical documentation, they also present serious safety hazards in busy Nigerian hospital wards. Nurses must interpret every order accurately before administration. Misreading a single letter can change how often a drug is given, leading to severe underdosing or accidental toxicity.

Common Frequency and Timing Abbreviations

Frequency abbreviations dictate how often and when a patient receives a specific medication dose. Confusing similar-looking prefixes or numbers can easily result in missed doses or dangerous overdosing.

  • BD (or BID): Bis in die, meaning twice daily.
  • TDS (or TID): Ter in die, meaning three times daily.
  • QDS (or QID): Quater in die, meaning four times daily.
  • OD (or daily): Omni die, meaning once daily.
  • PRN: Pro re nata, meaning as needed for specific symptoms.
  • Stat: Immediately, meaning a single dose given right away.

Route of Administration Abbreviations

The route indicates how the medicinal product enters the body. Using the correct route ensures proper drug absorption and therapeutic effect while preventing catastrophic administration errors, such as giving an oral suspension intravenously.

  • PO: Per os, meaning by mouth.
  • IV: Intravenous, meaning directly into a vein.
  • IM: Intramuscular, meaning deep into a muscle mass.
  • SC (or SubQ): Subcutaneous, meaning under the skin layers.
  • SL: Sublingual, meaning placed under the tongue.
  • Top: Topical, meaning applied to the skin surface.

Dosage Form and Measurement Abbreviations

Measurement units and preparation types define the exact quantity and physical form of the drug. A common clinical error involves misreading metric units, particularly confusing micrograms with milligrams due to poor handwriting.

  • mg: Milligram, one-thousandth of a gram.
  • mcg (or µg): Microgram, one-millionth of a gram.
  • mL: Milliliter, a metric unit of liquid volume.
  • gtt: Drop, used for ophthalmic or intravenous drip rates.
  • tab: Tablet, a solid oral dosage form.
  • cap: Capsule, a gelatin container enclosing medication.

Error Prevention and The Do Not Use List

Certain abbreviations are notoriously prone to misinterpretation and feature prominently on international and national 'Do Not Use' lists. For instance, writing 'u' for units can easily look like a zero or a four, turning a safe dose into a lethal overdose. Nurses must actively advocate for spelling out terms like 'unit' and 'daily' instead of relying on dangerous shortcuts.

Safe Transcription and Verification Practices in Nursing

Safe medication administration begins before the drug reaches the patient's bedside. When transcribing orders from a doctor's notebook to the main treatment chart, nurses must remain vigilant. Never guess an unclear abbreviation. Double-check calculations, verify patient allergies, and consult the hospital pharmacist when any doubt exists about a shorthand notation.
