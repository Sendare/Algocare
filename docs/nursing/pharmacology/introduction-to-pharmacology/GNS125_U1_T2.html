Pharmacology

Introduction to Pharmacology and Historical Context

Pharmacology is the comprehensive study of drugs and how they interact with living systems. Historically, early healers relied entirely on plants, minerals, and animal products to treat ailments. Traditional African medicine laid a rich foundation for local pharmacognosy long before synthetic drugs arrived. Modern pharmacology bridges traditional practices with scientific rigor, isolating active ingredients to create standardized treatments. Nurses must understand this evolution to appreciate why certain remedies persist in local healthcare settings while modern synthetic medications dominate clinical practice.

Core Definitions and Terminologies in Pharmacology

Mastering pharmacology requires familiarity with specific foundational terms that describe drug behavior and actions.

  • Drug: Any chemical substance that alters biological function when introduced into a living organism.
  • Pharmacology: The broad science dealing with all aspects of drug action and properties.
  • Pharmacy: The clinical science and art of preparing, dispensing, and reviewing medications.
  • Toxicology: The specific study of poisons and the adverse effects of chemicals on living systems.

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacology splits into two primary operational branches: what the body does to the drug, and what the drug does to the body.

BranchDescriptionKey Phases
PharmacokineticsWhat the body does to the drugAbsorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion (ADME)
PharmacodynamicsWhat the drug does to the bodyReceptor binding, signal transduction, physiological response

Pharmacokinetics dictates how long a drug stays active in the bloodstream. Pharmacodynamics explains therapeutic effects and unwanted side effects at the cellular level.

Drug Nomenclature: Chemical, Generic, and Brand Names

Every pharmaceutical agent can be identified by three distinct names, which often causes confusion during clinical handover.

  1. Chemical Name: Describes the exact atomic and molecular structure of the drug, used primarily by chemists and researchers.
  2. Generic Name: The nonproprietary, official name assigned to a drug, such as paracetamol, independent of any manufacturer.
  3. Brand Name: The proprietary, commercial name chosen by the pharmaceutical company that markets the drug, such as Panadol.

Nurses must prioritize generic names in clinical documentation to prevent medication errors caused by similar-sounding brand names.

Sources and Classification of Drugs

Medications originate from diverse natural and synthetic sources. Plant sources yield alkaloids and glycosides like digoxin. Animal sources provide insulin and heparin. Mineral sources supply magnesium and calcium compounds. Modern laboratories also synthesize countless synthetic and semi-synthetic molecules.

Drugs are classified according to their therapeutic use, such as antihypertensives or antibiotics, and by their pharmacological action, such as beta-blockers or diuretics. This dual classification helps nurses predict patient responses and monitor therapeutic outcomes effectively.

Routes of Drug Administration and Dosage Forms

The route chosen for medication administration directly dictates how fast and how completely a drug reaches the systemic circulation. Enteral routes involve the gastrointestinal tract, encompassing oral, sublingual, and rectal administration. Parenteral routes bypass the GI tract entirely, including intravenous, intramuscular, and subcutaneous injections. Topical routes deliver drugs directly to skin or mucous membranes.

Dosage forms vary from simple tablets and capsules to complex suspensions, inhalers, and transdermal patches designed to suit specific administration routes.

Adverse Drug Reactions and Pharmacovigilance

Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are noxious, unintended responses to medications occurring at normal therapeutic doses. Unlike predictable side effects, ADRs can be severe, life-threatening, or unexpected. Pharmacovigilance is the science dedicated to detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects or any other drug-related problems.

Nurses play a frontline role in pharmacovigilance by observing patients closely, identifying unusual symptoms early, and reporting adverse reactions to national regulatory bodies like NAFDAC in Nigeria.

The Role of the Nurse in Safe Medication Administration

Nurses are the ultimate safeguard between the medication order and the patient. Safe administration relies on adhering strictly to the recognized principles of drug administration. Clinical vigilance prevents errors during drug preparation, calculation, and delivery.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using two unique identifiers.
  • Right Drug: Check the label against the prescription order three times.
  • Right Dose: Calculate dosages accurately, double-checking high-risk drugs with a colleague.
  • Right Route: Confirm the delivery method matches the medical order.
  • Right Time: Administer medications within the prescribed schedule window.
  • Right Documentation: Record administration immediately after delivery, never before.
