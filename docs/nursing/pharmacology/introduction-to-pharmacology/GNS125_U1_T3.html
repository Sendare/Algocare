Drugs

Definition and Fundamental Concepts of Drugs

A drug is any substance, excluding food and water, that alters physiological or biochemical processes in a living organism. In nursing practice, drugs are utilized primarily for diagnosis, prevention, treatment, or relief of disease symptoms. Pharmacology serves as the overarching study of these chemical agents and their interactions with biological systems.

Key terminology in drug therapy includes:

  • Pharmacology: The comprehensive study of drugs and their properties.
  • Pharmacy: The science of preparing, dispensing, and compounding medications.
  • Therapeutics: The clinical application of drugs to prevent and treat diseases.
  • Toxicology: The study of poisonous substances and adverse drug effects.

Sources of Drugs: Natural and Synthetic

Medicinal substances originate from diverse environments and chemical laboratories. Natural sources have sustained human healing for millennia, while laboratory innovations have expanded treatment possibilities.

  • Plant Sources: Derived from leaves, roots, or bark, yielding agents like quinine from the cinchona tree or digoxin from digitalis.
  • Animal Sources: Extracted from animal tissues or fluids, historically including insulin from pancreas extracts.
  • Mineral Sources: Inorganic elements obtained from the earth, such as magnesium sulfate and iron supplements.
  • Synthetic Sources: Artificially manufactured in laboratories through chemical synthesis, creating reliable supplies of agents like paracetamol and most modern sulfonamides.

Every pharmaceutical agent possesses multiple names, which can cause confusion in clinical settings if nurses are not vigilant. Understanding these naming conventions prevents medication errors.

Classification Systems of Drugs

Drugs are grouped into classes based on shared characteristics, helping clinicians predict therapeutic outcomes and potential adverse effects. A single medication may belong to multiple categories depending on the classification perspective.

  • Therapeutic Classification: Categorized by what condition the drug treats (e.g., antihypertensives lower blood pressure).
  • Pharmacologic Classification: Categorized by how the drug acts at the molecular or tissue level (e.g., beta-blockers block adrenergic receptors).

Misconception alert: Nurses often assume a drug class guarantees an identical side effect profile for every agent within that group, ignoring subtle structural differences that alter individual patient tolerance.

Overview of Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacology relies on two core principles: what the body does to the drug, and what the drug does to the body.

Pharmacokinetics tracks the journey of a drug through four stages, often remembered by the acronym ADME:

  1. Absorption: Movement of the drug from its administration site into the bloodstream.
  2. Distribution: Transport of the drug from the bloodstream to body tissues and target sites.
  3. Metabolism: Biotransformation of the drug, primarily occurring in the liver, into active or inactive metabolites.
  4. Excretion: Removal of drug waste products from the body, chiefly via the kidneys.

Pharmacodynamics explores the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs on the body and their mechanisms of action, including how drugs bind to cellular receptors to trigger responses.

Drug Legislation and Safety in Nigeria

Regulatory bodies enforce strict laws to protect the public from counterfeit, substandard, and adulterated medications. In Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria (PCN) lead these efforts.

Nurses must be familiar with regulations regarding controlled substances, prescription-only medicines, and over-the-counter drugs. Unsafe dispensing practices and the proliferation of open drug markets pose severe public health threats that demand constant vigilance from healthcare professionals.

The Nurse's Role in Safe Medication Administration

Nurses act as the final safety barrier between a prescribed medication and the patient. Executing this responsibility requires rigorous adherence to safety protocols and clinical judgment.

Adhering to the standard rights of medication administration prevents most clinical errors:

  • Right patient
  • Right drug
  • Right dose
  • Right route
  • Right time
  • Right documentation
  • Right reason
  • Right response

Before administering any agent, the nurse must assess allergies, review current lab results, and monitor the patient post-administration for therapeutic efficacy or adverse reactions.
