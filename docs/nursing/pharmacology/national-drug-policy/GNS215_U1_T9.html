Multiple Prescriptions

Understanding Multiple Prescriptions and Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy refers to the concurrent use of multiple medications by a patient. While often necessary to manage complex or co-existing health conditions, it carries significant clinical implications. In many Nigerian healthcare settings, patients may acquire medications from multiple sources, including patent medicine vendors, herbalists, and orthodox hospitals, compounding the total daily pill burden.

  • Appropriate Polypharmacy: The prescribing of multiple drugs where each medicine is optimized for a specific evidence-based indication, and the balance of benefits outweighs the risks.
  • Problematic Polypharmacy: The use of multiple medications where the prescribing is disproportionate to the clinical need, or where duplications and inappropriate drug choices occur.

When managing patients across community clinics or tertiary hospitals, nurses must recognize that more drugs do not always equate to better care. The sheer volume of tablets can overwhelm vulnerable populations, particularly elderly patients or those with visual and cognitive impairments.

Causes and Contributing Factors in Clinical Practice

Multiple prescriptions rarely happen by accident. They stem from systemic issues in healthcare delivery and patient behaviors. A frequent driver is fragmented care, where a patient sees multiple specialists—such as a cardiologist, endocrinologist, and nephrologist—without a central primary care physician coordinating the overall therapeutic plan. Each specialist adds medications for their specific organ system.

Another major factor is the prescribing cascade. This occurs when a side effect of a primary drug is misinterpreted as a new medical condition, prompting the addition of a second drug to treat the side effect. Patient expectations also play a role; many individuals visiting Nigerian clinics believe a consultation is incomplete without receiving at least one injectable or several tablets to take home.

Risks, Adverse Drug Reactions, and Interactions

As the number of prescribed medications increases, the probability of adverse outcomes rises exponentially. Pharmacokinetic changes, such as altered hepatic metabolism and reduced renal clearance in older adults, make drug accumulation common.

  • Pharmacodynamic Interactions: Two drugs with similar or opposing pharmacological effects interact directly at receptor sites, leading to excessive sedation or severe hypotension.
  • Pharmacokinetic Interactions: One drug alters the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of another, frequently mediated through the cytochrome P450 enzyme system in the liver.

Beyond direct drug toxicity, multiple prescriptions severely impair adherence. A complex dosing schedule often leads to missed doses, accidental double-dosing, or premature discontinuation of vital therapies.

Assessment and Medication Reconciliation

Medication reconciliation is a formal, structured process where healthcare professionals partner with patients to create the most accurate list possible of all medications currently being taken. This includes prescription drugs, over-the-counter remedies, vitamins, and traditional herbal preparations.

  1. Verification: Collect a complete medication history by asking the patient to bring all their medication packs to the clinic or bedside.
  2. Clarification: Review the dosage, frequency, and route of each listed item to ensure accuracy and therapeutic alignment.
  3. Reconciliation: Compare the hospital admission orders with the verified home list to identify discrepancies, duplications, omissions, or potential interactions.

Nurses play a frontline role during admission, transfer, and discharge by performing thorough reconciliation. Catching a discrepancy early prevents medication errors from propagating across different care transitions.

Nursing Management and Patient Education

Managing a patient on multiple prescriptions demands robust organizational skills and clear communication. Nurses must assess the patient's functional ability to manage their regimen independently. Physical limitations like arthritis can make opening child-resistant pill containers nearly impossible, leading patients to leave medication bottles open or skip doses entirely.

Education must be tailored and practical. Instead of handing over a crowded schedule, the nurse should help consolidate dosing times where clinically permissible—such as shifting medications to once- or twice-daily dosing regimens. Visual aids, color-coded charts, or pill organizers can assist patients with low literacy levels. Emphasizing the purpose of each drug helps reinforce adherence, ensuring the patient understands why they take every single pill.

Regulatory Guidelines and Safe Practice in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the regulation of drug distribution and dispensing is overseen by agencies such as the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria (PCN). Despite these regulatory frameworks, the open drug market and widespread availability of over-the-counter antibiotics and analgesics contribute heavily to self-medication and uncontrolled multiple prescriptions.

Hospital protocols and national standard treatment guidelines exist to promote rational drug use. Nurses working in Nigerian health facilities must understand these frameworks to advocate for safe prescribing practices. Collaborative rounds involving doctors, pharmacists, and nurses remain a vital strategy to review medication appropriateness and protect patients from the harms of uncoordinated polypharmacy.
