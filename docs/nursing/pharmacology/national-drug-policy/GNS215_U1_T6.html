Role of the Nurse in Essential Drug Programme

Introduction and Objectives of the Essential Drug Programme

The Essential Drug Programme (EDP) aims to ensure that safe, effective, and affordable medicines are available at all times within the Nigerian healthcare delivery system. It addresses historical challenges of drug shortages, fake pharmaceuticals, and exorbitant costs that plague resource-limited settings. By focusing on a carefully chosen list of priority medications, the public health system maximizes scarce financial resources to meet the real health needs of the population. Nurses operate at the frontline of this policy, translating high-level supply chain frameworks into direct bedside care. Understanding the core objectives of the EDP allows nursing professionals to act as stewards of public health resources rather than passive observers of hospital logistics.

Selection and Quantification of Essential Medicines

  • Needs Assessment: Identifying prevalent diseases within the local community, such as malaria, infectious diarrhea, and vaccine-preventable illnesses.
  • Standard Treatment Guidelines: Utilizing approved national protocols to determine which specific pharmaceutical agents are required for various clinical presentations.
  • Quantification Methods: Calculating accurate consumption rates and morbidity data to prevent both severe stockouts and costly overstocking of expired items.
  • Nursing Input: Providing vital feedback from the ward level regarding actual drug utilization patterns versus projected estimates.

Procurement, Storage, and Inventory Management

Proper handling of pharmaceuticals protects their chemical integrity from the point of delivery to administration. Poor storage conditions, such as high ambient temperatures and excessive humidity, degrade active pharmaceutical ingredients, rendering treatments useless. Nurses frequently manage ward-level drug stores and must adhere strictly to stock rotation practices.

Dispensing, Administration, and Rational Use

Rational drug use ensures patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. Irrational use, such as the overprescription of antibiotics or unnecessary polypharmacy, contributes to antimicrobial resistance and adverse drug events. Before administering any medication, the nurse must execute safety checks, verifying the right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Clinical vigilance at this stage intercepts prescribing errors originating from earlier steps in the supply chain.

Patient Education and Adherence Support

Prescribing an essential medicine is meaningless if the patient fails to take it correctly at home. Patients often stop taking medications prematurely when their symptoms subside, or they skip doses due to confusing instructions or perceived side effects. Nurses bridge this communication gap by providing clear, jargon-free explanations regarding the purpose of the drug, expected duration of therapy, and potential side effects. Empowering patients with knowledge transforms them into active participants in their treatment plan, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes.

Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting

No pharmaceutical product is entirely free of unintended effects. Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. Nurses are uniquely positioned to observe early signs of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) during routine ward rounds and patient care. When an unexpected reaction occurs, the nurse must document the event, provide immediate supportive care, and report the occurrence to the national regulatory authority, such as NAFDAC in Nigeria, using yellow cards or digital reporting platforms.
