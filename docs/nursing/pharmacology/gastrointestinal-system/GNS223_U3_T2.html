Purgatives

Introduction to Purgatives and Definition of Terms

Purgatives are substances used to promote defecation and relieve constipation. Within clinical pharmacology, terms like laxatives, purgatives, and cathartics are often used interchangeably, though they differ slightly in potency. Laxatives produce a soft, formed stool with minimal discomfort. Purgatives result in a more fluid and prompt evacuation of the bowel. Cathartics produce complete evacuation of the intestinal tract, often yielding a watery discharge. Understanding these definitions helps nurses administer the correct preparation based on clinical needs, whether preparing a patient for diagnostic imaging or managing simple sluggish bowel movements.

Classification and Mechanisms of Action

Pharmacological agents used to clear the bowels are categorized by how they alter physiological processes in the gastrointestinal tract.

  • Bulk-forming agents: Absorb water in the intestine, swelling to form a gel-like mass that stretches the bowel wall and triggers peristalsis.
  • Osmotic agents: Draw water into the intestinal lumen through high osmolality, softening the stool and increasing luminal pressure.
  • Stimulant agents: Direct irritants to the enteric nervous system that increase intestinal motility and secretion.
  • Stool softeners (emollients): Lower surface tension, allowing water and lipids to penetrate and soften fecal matter.

Clinical Indications and Therapeutic Uses

Purgatives are indicated in several clinical scenarios where passing soft stool or emptying the colon is necessary. They prevent straining in patients with recent myocardial infarction, hernias, or severe hemorrhoids. They are also standard preparation protocols prior to diagnostic procedures like colonoscopy, barium enemas, or abdominal surgeries to ensure clear visualization. Additionally, certain poison ingestions require purgatives to speed up elimination of toxic substances from the gastrointestinal tract, provided the toxin is not corrosive.

Contraindications and Adverse Effects

Indiscriminate use of purgatives can lead to significant complications. They are strictly contraindicated in patients presenting with undiagnosed abdominal pain, bowel obstruction, appendicitis, or perforation, as increased peristalsis can cause rupture. Common adverse effects include abdominal cramping, electrolyte imbalances, and fluid depletion. Long-term reliance on stimulant purgatives can result in laxative dependence, where the colon loses its natural ability to contract independently.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education in Nigeria

Nurses play a critical role in managing patients on purgative therapy, especially in local Nigerian clinical contexts where herbal concoctions and over-the-counter purgatives are common. Nurses must assess the patient's baseline bowel habits, hydration status, and diet before administration. Patient education should focus on lifestyle modifications such as increasing dietary fiber from local foods like beans, vegetables, and tubers, alongside adequate water intake. Nurses must warn patients against the habitual use of harsh local concoctions that deplete electrolytes and damage the intestinal lining over time.
