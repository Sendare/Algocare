Anticholinergic Drugs

Introduction and Mechanism of Action

Anticholinergic drugs, sometimes called parasympatholytics, are agents that block the action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors in the parasympathetic nervous system. By stopping this neurotransmitter from binding to its target sites, these medications effectively reduce involuntary muscle movements, decrease various body secretions, and dilate the pupils. Understanding this foundational pharmacology helps nurses predict both the therapeutic benefits and the predictable side effects a patient will experience during therapy.

Classification and Common Drug Examples

  • Atropine: A classic natural belladonna alkaloid used primarily to treat symptomatic bradycardia and dry up secretions before surgery.
  • Scopolamine: Frequently administered via transdermal patches to prevent motion sickness and postoperative nausea.
  • Ipratropium: An inhaled quaternary ammonium compound utilized as a bronchodilator for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Oxybutynin: A synthetic tertiary amine prescribed widely to manage symptoms of an overactive bladder.

Therapeutic Uses and Clinical Indications

Clinicians prescribe anticholinergics across various medical specialties to counteract excessive parasympathetic activity. In respiratory care, they relax smooth muscles in the airways to relieve bronchospasm. Gastroenterologists use them to calm hyperactive bowel motility in conditions like irritable bowel syndrome. Additionally, neurologists employ central-acting agents to help control tremors and rigidity in early Parkinson's disease by restoring the balance between acetylcholine and dopamine in the brain.

Adverse Effects and Toxicity

The side effect profile of anticholinergic drugs is an extension of their pharmacological action, often remembered through the mnemonic: cant see, cant pee, cant spit, cant shit.

  • Xerostomia: Severe dry mouth caused by reduced salivary gland secretion.
  • Blurred vision: Resulting from paralysis of the ciliary muscle (cycloplegia) and pupillary dilation (mydriasis).
  • Urinary retention: Caused by relaxation of the detrusor muscle and constriction of the bladder sphincter.
  • Constipation: Due to slowed gastrointestinal transit time.
  • Tachycardia: Blockage of vagal tone on the heart rate.

Contraindications and Precautions

Because these medications slow down the body and trap fluids in certain organs, they can be dangerous for specific patient populations. They are strictly contraindicated in individuals with narrow-angle glaucoma, as mydriasis can block aqueous humor outflow and trigger an acute pressure spike. Patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia should avoid them to prevent complete urinary obstruction. Furthermore, anyone with a history of gastrointestinal obstruction or severe ulcerative colitis must not take them, as halting peristalsis can lead to toxic megacolon.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring patients receiving anticholinergic therapy to catch early signs of toxicity or intolerable side effects. Assessment must include tracking daily fluid intake and output, listening for bowel sounds, and checking for bladder distension. Nurses should teach clients to manage dry mouth by sucking on hard sugarless candies or sipping water frequently. Elderly patients require special vigilance, as they are exceptionally sensitive to central nervous system effects like confusion, hallucinations, and memory impairment.
