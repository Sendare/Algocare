Handling and Storage of Drugs

Introduction to Drug Handling and Storage

Proper drug handling and storage protect pharmaceutical integrity from the moment products arrive at a healthcare facility until administration to a patient. Improperly stored medications can lose their chemical potency, resulting in treatment failure, or degrade into toxic breakdown products that harm patients. Nurses play a frontline role in maintaining this safety chain across hospital wards, clinics, and community health centers throughout Nigeria.

Environmental Factors Affecting Drug Stability

Environmental conditions directly dictate the shelf-life and chemical stability of pharmaceutical preparations.

  • Temperature: Excessive heat accelerates chemical degradation, while freezing can crack glass ampoules and destroy protein structures in biologics.
  • Light: Photolensitive drugs undergo molecular changes when exposed to direct sunlight or fluorescent room lighting, requiring amber glass containers or opaque packaging.
  • Humidity: High moisture levels cause solid dosage forms like tablets and capsules to soften, crumble, or stick together.

Ward medication rooms must be monitored daily using calibrated thermometers and hygrometers to catch excursions before drugs are compromised.

Cold Chain Management and Vaccine Storage

The cold chain is an uninterrupted series of refrigerated storage and distribution activities that maintain vaccines and thermosensitive drugs within safe temperature ranges, typically 2°C to 8°C. A break in this chain permanently ruins biological products, rendering them useless even if they are later cooled back down. Dedicated vaccine refrigerators must be used—never domestic refrigerators, which experience extreme temperature fluctuations during defrost cycles. Temperature logs must be recorded twice daily to verify compliance.

Storage of Controlled Substances and High-Alert Medications

Certain medications carry high risks of abuse, severe adverse effects, or fatal medication errors, requiring strict regulatory oversight.

  • Controlled Substances: Opioids and sedatives must be stored in a double-locked metal safe affixed to the wall or floor. Access is restricted to authorized nursing staff, and every dose dispensed requires a physical signature in a narcotic register.
  • High-Alert Medications: Concentrated electrolytes, insulin, and anticoagulants must be clearly labeled and segregated in storage areas to prevent accidental misadministration during emergencies.

Inventory Control and Stock Rotation Principles

Efficient inventory management prevents both stockouts of essential medicines and the accumulation of expired stock. The fundamental rule for stock rotation is FEFO (First Expired, First Out), which ensures that items with the nearest expiration dates are dispensed before newer shipments. Regular stock audits help reconcile physical counts with supply records, identifying discrepancies, theft, or hidden waste across clinical units.

Prevention of Medication Contamination and Degradation

Maintaining sterility and chemical purity during storage requires adherence to basic hygiene and packaging rules. Multi-dose vials must be labeled with the exact date and time of first puncture to track microbial growth risks. Medications should remain in their original blister packs or containers until the moment of preparation, shielding them from ambient dust, moisture, and chemical cross-contamination on ward counters.

Disposal of Expired and Damaged Medications

Expired, compromised, or contaminated medications cannot be thrown into standard municipal trash or flushed down ward sinks, as active chemical residues enter local water tables and soil. Healthcare facilities must quarantine expired stock in a designated, labeled return bin away from active inventory. Disposal must follow institutional protocols and national environmental guidelines, often involving incineration or returned-goods reverse distribution to certified pharmaceutical waste handlers.
