Antiparasitic Drugs

Introduction to Parasitic Infections and Antiparasitic Therapy

Parasitic infections remain a significant public health challenge, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions like Nigeria. These infections are caused by organisms that live on or inside a host organism and get their food from or at the expense of that host. Antiparasitic drugs are pharmaceutical agents designed to kill or inhibit the growth of these parasites, which broadly include protozoa, helminths, and ectoparasites. Unlike bacteria, parasites are eukaryotic organisms, meaning their cellular structure closely resembles human cells. This biochemical similarity makes the development of selective toxicity challenging, as drugs that harm the parasite can also cause adverse effects in the human host.

Effective antiparasitic therapy relies on accurate identification of the causative organism and an understanding of its life cycle. Treatment goals include eliminating the parasite, relieving clinical symptoms, and preventing transmission within the community. Nurses play a crucial role in recognizing clinical manifestations of parasitic infestations, supporting diagnostic evaluations, and ensuring treatment compliance.

Anthelmintics: Treating Helminthic Infestations

Helminths are parasitic worms that infect various organs in the human body, commonly categorized into nematodes (roundworms), cestodes (tapeworms), and trematodes (flukes). Anthelmintics are medications used to expel or destroy these infesting worms.

  • Albendazole and Mebendazole: These benzimidazoles work by inhibiting microtubule synthesis in the parasite, blocking glucose uptake and depleting its energy stores until it dies.
  • Ivermectin: This agent paralyzes the parasite by intensifying inhibitory neurotransmission, leading to muscular paralysis and clearance by the host immune system.
  • Praziquantel: Highly effective against schistosomes and tapeworms, this drug increases cell membrane permeability to calcium, causing severe contractions and paralysis of the worm.

Antimalarial Agents: Management of Malaria

Malaria is a life-threatening vector-borne disease caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted by infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. Management requires prompt administration of effective antimalarial drugs tailored to the species and severity of infection.

  • Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapies (ACTs): Currently the gold standard for treating uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria, combining a fast-acting artemisinin derivative with a longer-acting partner drug.
  • Chloroquine: Historically used for sensitive strains, though widespread resistance has limited its use primarily to non-falciparum species like Plasmodium ovale and malariae.
  • Quinine and Artesunate: Intravenous formulations reserved for severe and complicated malaria cases, requiring careful cardiac monitoring during infusion.
  • Primaquine: Essential for eradicating the dormant liver stages (hypnozoites) of Plasmodium vivax and ovale to prevent relapse.

Antiprotozoal Drugs: Amebiasis, Giardiasis, and Trichomoniasis

Protozoal infections affect millions globally, often transmitted through contaminated food and water or sexual contact. Treatment often involves nitroimidazole derivatives and other targeted anti-infectives.

  • Metronidazole: The cornerstone therapy for anaerobic protozoal infections including amebiasis, giardiasis, and trichomoniasis. It enters the protozoal cell, gets reduced into reactive compounds, and damages DNA.
  • Tinidazole: A similar nitroimidazole with a longer half-life and often better gastrointestinal tolerability profile than metronidazole.
  • Nitazoxanide: A broad-spectrum antiprotozoal agent frequently used to treat cryptosporidiosis and giardiasis in both pediatric and adult populations.

Ectoparasiticides: Managing Scabies and Pediculosis

Ectoparasites reside on the external surface of the human body, specifically infesting the skin and hair. The two most common infestations managed in clinical practice are scabies (caused by Sarcoptes scabiei mites) and pediculosis (lice infestation).

  • Permethrin 5% Cream: The preferred topical treatment for scabies. It acts on the nerve cell membrane of parasites to disrupt sodium channels, causing delayed repolarization, paralysis, and death of the mite and its eggs.
  • Benzyl Benzoate: Another traditional topical scabicide used widely in resource-limited settings.
  • Ivermectin Oral: Used off-label or systematically for severe scabies or crusted scabies outbreaks, especially when topical applications fail.
  • Malathion Lotion: Applied topically for head lice (pediculosis capitis), inhibiting acetylcholinesterase in the parasite.

Adverse Effects and Nursing Care in Antiparasitic Therapy

Because antiparasitic agents target complex metabolic pathways, patients frequently experience adverse drug reactions. Gastrointestinal upset—including nausea, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea—is nearly universal across this drug class due to local irritation and the dying burden of parasites in the gut.

Central nervous system effects such as dizziness, headache, and peripheral neuropathy can occur with prolonged nitroimidazole use or high-dose antimalarials. Hepatotoxicity and bone marrow suppression are serious risks associated with prolonged benzimidazole courses, requiring baseline and periodic liver function and complete blood count monitoring. Nurses must monitor for signs of hypersensitivity, jaundice, or unusual bleeding, and educate patients on completing the full course of therapy despite mild side effects.
