Infusions and Transfusions

Introduction to Infusions and Transfusions in Nursing Practice

Intravenous infusions and blood transfusions are fundamental therapeutic interventions in Nigerian healthcare settings, ranging from busy tertiary teaching hospitals to rural primary health centres. An infusion involves the direct administration of fluids, electrolytes, or medications into the venous system. A transfusion specifically refers to the transfer of whole blood or specific blood components from a donor to a recipient. Both procedures aim to restore intravascular volume, correct electrolyte imbalances, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, and maintain acid-base balance. Nurses hold the ultimate bedside responsibility for verifying orders, inspecting products, monitoring the patient, and managing adverse reactions. A lack of vigilance during these procedures can lead to catastrophic morbidity or mortality, making rigorous adherence to safety protocols non-negotiable.

Types of Intravenous Infusions and Blood Products

Intravenous fluids are broadly categorized into crystalloids and colloids, each serving distinct hemodynamic goals. Blood products are similarly divided based on the specific hematological deficit identified in the patient.

  • Crystalloids: Aqueous solutions of mineral salts or other water-soluble molecules, such as Normal Saline and Ringer's Lactate.
  • Colloids: Solutions containing large insoluble molecules like starches or albumin that remain in the intravascular space longer.
  • Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs): Concentrated red cells used to increase oxygen-carrying capacity without adding excessive volume.
  • Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP): Contains clotting factors utilized to manage bleeding disorders and coagulopathies.
  • Platelets: Administered to prevent or treat bleeding in patients with severe thrombocytopenia.
  • Cryoprecipitate: Rich in fibrinogen and factor VIII, primarily used for specific clotting factor deficiencies.

Preparation and Safety Protocols

Safety protocols form the bedrock of safe transfusion and infusion practice. Before initiating any blood product, a mandatory dual-nurse bedside check must be performed. This involves verifying the patient's identity against the blood bank compatibility form, hospital identification band, and the blood bag label.

  1. Patient Identification: Confirm the patient's full name and hospital number verbally if conscious.
  2. Product Inspection: Check the blood bag for abnormal discoloration, clots, leaks, or expiration dates.
  3. Baseline Vitals: Measure and record temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure immediately before starting.
  4. Aseptic Technique: Ensure strict hand hygiene and clean the access port with an antiseptic swab before connecting tubing.
  5. Compatible Solutions: Use only 0.9% Sodium Chloride with blood products; never mix with dextrose or Ringer's lactate to prevent hemolysis or clotting.

Administration Techniques and Patient Monitoring

Administering blood products and IV infusions requires precise calculation of drop rates or pump settings. Blood components must be infused using specialized blood administration tubing equipped with an integral filter to catch micro-aggregates and debris. The transfusion must be initiated slowly—usually no faster than 2 ml per minute for the first fifteen minutes—while the nurse remains at the bedside to observe for immediate signs of an acute reaction. Regular monitoring of vital signs is scheduled at 15 minutes, hourly until completion, and one hour post-transfusion. For routine crystalloid infusions, peripheral cannula sites must be inspected frequently for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or extravasation, and IV cannulae should be rotated according to institutional infection control policy.

Complications and Emergency Management

Despite rigorous checks, adverse reactions to blood products and intravenous therapy can occur. Transfusion reactions are categorized into acute and delayed types. Acute hemolytic reactions, allergic reactions, febrile non-hemolytic reactions, and transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) demand immediate nursing intervention.

When a transfusion reaction is suspected, the nurse must act swiftly:

  • Stop the transfusion immediately.
  • Disconnect the blood tubing while leaving the IV access patent with normal saline using new tubing.
  • Notify the physician and blood bank instantly.
  • Return the blood bag and transfusion set to the blood bank for investigation.
  • Collect fresh blood and urine samples to check for hemolysis and renal function.
  • Administer emergency medications such as antihistamines, corticosteroids, or epinephrine as prescribed.
