Patient Drug History

Introduction to Patient Drug History

A patient drug history is a structured record of all pharmaceutical substances a person currently uses or has recently taken. This includes prescription medications, over-the-counter remedies, herbal preparations, and traditional African medicines. Obtaining this history forms a foundational duty for nurses during patient admission or clinic registration. Without an accurate picture of what a patient ingests, healthcare providers risk prescribing conflicting treatments, missing underlying toxicity, or failing to recognize drug-induced symptoms. In busy Nigerian hospital wards, taking a proper drug history prevents adverse events before they start.

Components of a Comprehensive Drug History

A thorough drug history requires gathering specific details beyond just the name of a medicine. Nurses must collect data systematically to ensure safety across transitions of care.

  • Drug Name: Distinguish between brand names and generic names to prevent duplication.
  • Dosage and Frequency: Exact strength and how often the patient takes it.
  • Route of Administration: Oral, topical, sublingual, or traditional rectal applications.
  • Duration of Use: How long the patient has been on the therapy.
  • Indication: What symptom or illness the drug is meant to treat.
  • Adverse Reactions: Any past allergies or bad experiences with the drug.

Techniques for Effective Medication Interviewing

Extracting an accurate medication list relies heavily on effective communication skills. Patients often forget names of drugs or confuse instructions. Nurses should use open-ended questions rather than leading ones. Instead of asking "You take your blood pressure medicine daily, right?", ask "How do you take your blood pressure medicine each day?". Another useful strategy is asking the patient to bring all their medication containers from home in a bag, often called the brown bag method, which reveals actual packaging, remaining pills, and expiration dates.

Over-the-Counter Drugs, Herbs, and Supplements

Many patients do not consider non-prescription items as 'real medicine.' Consequently, they often omit local herbal concoctions, vitamins, analgesics bought from roadside vendors, and dietary supplements from their initial report. Nurses must specifically ask about these substances. Local herbs and traditional remedies can interact dangerously with orthodox medicines, especially altering liver enzymes or affecting blood clotting. Documenting these products prevents missed interactions that could compromise patient safety.

Assessing Medication Adherence and Barriers

Collecting a drug list is only half the task; the nurse must also determine if the patient actually takes the medications as prescribed. Adherence barriers in resource-limited settings are multifaceted. Financial constraints often lead patients to skip doses or split tablets to make supplies last longer. Cultural beliefs, fear of side effects, complex dosing schedules, and physical challenges like poor eyesight also hinder compliance. Non-judgmental questioning allows patients to admit missed doses without fear of reprimand.

Documenting and Communicating Drug History

Once gathered, the drug history must be clearly documented in the patient's nursing care plan and medical record. Illegible handwriting or incomplete entries invite medication errors during shift handovers. Nurses must communicate any discrepancies found during medication reconciliation to the attending physician or pharmacist promptly. Standardized forms and electronic health records minimize confusion and ensure every member of the healthcare team relies on the same up-to-date medication profile.
