Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction and Definition of the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financial mechanism designed to ensure a steady, uninterrupted supply of essential medicines in public healthcare facilities. Initiated largely to combat frequent stockouts and out-of-stock syndromes in resource-limited settings, the scheme relies on a closed financial loop. Seed capital is provided initially to purchase a basic stock of drugs. When patients pay for these medications, the revenue generated does not enter a general government treasury. Instead, it goes directly back into purchasing fresh stock, allowing the fund to 'revolve' indefinitely.

In many Nigerian teaching hospitals, general hospitals, and primary health care centers, stockouts historically forced patients to purchase prescriptions from external, often expensive, private vendors. The DRF bridges this gap by institutionalizing drug availability within the facility itself. A common misconception is that the DRF is a free drug program; on the contrary, it is a cost-recovery system. Medications are sold to patients at affordable, subsidized prices that cover the replacement cost plus a small operational markup to absorb inflation and logistics expenses.

Core Objectives and Principles of the Scheme

The success of any DRF scheme depends on strict adherence to established operational principles. These guidelines protect the fund from depletion and ensure that the vulnerable populations who need medicines the most are not priced out of care.

  • Affordability: Drugs must be priced lower than prevailing open-market rates while remaining high in quality.
  • Availability: Essential medicines must remain in stock consistently, reducing reliance on outside pharmacies.
  • Accountability: Transparent financial tracking is mandatory to prevent diversion of funds or drugs.
  • Cost Recovery: The pricing structure must at minimum recoup the full replacement cost of the items sold.

When these principles work together, they build public trust in the public health system. Patients return to government facilities because they know they will find their prescribed medications without facing exploitative commercial markups.

Structure and Operation of the Fund

Operating a functional DRF requires a multidisciplinary committee rather than a single individual running the show. Typically, a DRF Management Committee includes pharmacists, accountants, internal auditors, and nursing representation. This committee oversees procurement, pricing policies, inventory control, and revenue collection.

Separation of duties is vital for operational integrity. The clinician prescribes, the pharmacist dispenses, and a separate cashier or automated point-of-sale system collects payments. Cash should rarely, if ever, change hands directly with the dispensing personnel to minimize opportunities for theft or stock diversion. Regular auditing of inventory against cash collections helps maintain transparency across the entire operational chain.

Procurement and Supply Chain Process

Procurement forms the backbone of the Drug Revolving Fund. Buying substandard medicines or purchasing at inflated market rates will quickly collapse the fund. Facilities must procure medicines using the Essential Medicines List (EML) as a guide, focusing on generic names rather than expensive branded equivalents.

Bulk purchasing directly from reputable manufacturers or accredited centralized medical stores helps secure favorable wholesale discounts. Once drugs arrive at the facility, proper warehousing practices are necessary. Store rooms must maintain optimal temperature and humidity to prevent degradation, particularly for sensitive items like vaccines and insulin. Stock rotation follows the First-Expired, First-Out (FEFO) principle to minimize financial losses from expired inventory.

Financial Management and Sustainability

Financial sustainability is the ultimate test of a Drug Revolving Fund. When cash flow breaks down, the fund stalls. A major threat to sustainability is credit sales and non-payment by patients or departments. Allowing staff or emergency cases to take drugs on credit without a strict recovery mechanism bleeds the fund of its working capital.

Surpluses generated from the modest markup are not meant for general hospital overheads like electricity bills or staff salaries. They belong exclusively to the DRF account to absorb market shocks and fund future procurements.

Role of Nurses and Healthcare Professionals in DRF

While pharmacists manage the bulk storage and dispensary, nurses play a critical frontline role in supporting the DRF ecosystem. Nurses are often the first to notice when a ward stock is running low or when a patient struggles to afford prescribed items. By practicing accurate ward stock inventory management and reporting low counts early, nurses prevent emergency stockouts.

Furthermore, nurses educate patients on why purchasing medications through the facility's DRF pharmacy is safer and more reliable than patronizing unregulated roadside medicine vendors. Nurses also participate in prescription auditing by ensuring that doctors prescribe from the facility's approved essential drug list, keeping the demand aligned with what the DRF can sustainably supply.

Challenges and Limitations in Implementation

Despite its noble design, many DRF schemes in public facilities struggle or collapse outright due to systemic hurdles. Bureaucratic delays in procurement approvals can leave a hospital dry of essential medicines for weeks. Political interference is another major stumbling block, such as when management is pressured to supply drugs on credit to influential individuals or government agencies without repayment.

Another significant threat is theft, pilferage, and poor inventory accounting. If drugs disappear from the shelves without equivalent cash in the box, the fund shrinks. Additionally, severe national inflation erodes purchasing power; if the markup percentage cannot keep pace with rising wholesale costs, the capital will eventually fail to buy the same quantity of goods.

Strategies for Monitoring, Evaluation, and Improvement

To keep a Drug Revolving Fund healthy, continuous monitoring and periodic evaluation are non-negotiable. Management must track key performance indicators such as stock turnover rate, percentage of stockouts, and ledger reconciliation accuracy. Monthly physical stock counts must be reconciled with sales records to catch discrepancies early.

Periodic reviews of drug pricing structures help accommodate changing exchange rates and supplier costs. Furthermore, training programs for staff involved in inventory and financial handling reduce administrative errors. When transparency is maintained through routine checks, the DRF fulfills its promise of providing enduring pharmaceutical care to the community.
