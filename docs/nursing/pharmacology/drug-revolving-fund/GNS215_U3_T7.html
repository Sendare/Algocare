Role of the Nurse in Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction and Objectives of the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a sustainable financing mechanism designed to ensure a steady, uninterrupted supply of essential medicines in public health institutions. In many Nigerian healthcare facilities, erratic drug supplies historically crippled service delivery. The DRF operates on a simple cycle: funds are used to purchase medicines, patients pay subsidized prices for these medicines upon dispensation, and the revenue generated is plowed back directly into procuring fresh stock.

The primary goals of this scheme include:

  • Eliminating stock-outs of vital pharmaceuticals.
  • Ensuring affordability for vulnerable patient populations.
  • Maintaining financial self-sufficiency independent of frequent government budgetary allocations.

A common misconception is that the DRF is a profit-making commercial venture for the hospital; rather, it is a service-oriented revolving pool where markups are strictly managed to cover procurement logistics, inflation, and replacement costs.

Core Principles of DRF Management

Successful implementation of the DRF relies on strict adherence to established administrative and operational principles. Without these guardrails, the fund easily collapses due to pilferage, poor record-keeping, or credit sales.

  • Revolving Integrity: Capital and stocks must continuously convert into one another without leakage.
  • Affordable Pricing: Prices must factor in replacement costs while remaining within reach of ordinary citizens.
  • Separation of Duties: Clinical staff, accounting personnel, and storekeepers must have clearly defined boundaries to prevent fraud.
  • Strict Cash Discipline: Cash collections must be remitted promptly to designated DRF bank accounts rather than held indefinitely in wards.

Specific Roles of the Nurse in DRF Implementation

Although the pharmacy department handles procurement and bulk storage, nurses play an indispensable frontline role in making the DRF work smoothly at the ward level. Nurses act as the final link between the drug supply chain and the patient.

  • Ward Stock Management: Maintaining tidy mini-inventories or emergency treatment trays in the wards.
  • Accurate Administration: Ensuring that prescribed DRF drugs are administered correctly to the right patient, minimizing waste from dropped or contaminated doses.
  • Patient Advocacy: Guiding indigent patients on how to access affordable medications through official DRF channels rather than buying expensive counterfeits outside.
  • Prompt Reporting: Notifying management immediately about stock discrepancies, damaged goods, or fast-moving items.

Inventory Control, Stock Monitoring, and Preventing Wastage

Uncontrolled drug loss in hospital wards can quietly drain the capital base of a DRF. Nurses must master basic inventory control techniques to safeguard ward-level supplies.

The First-In, First-Out (FIFO) rule must be strictly applied: older stocks with earlier expiry dates are placed in front and used before newer supplies. Nurses should conduct routine stock audits of emergency cupboards, checking for expired items, signs of moisture damage, or broken packaging.

Careless pill-splitting, inaccurate dose calculations, and failure to return unused medications to the treatment room all contribute to avoidable drug wastage. Prompt identification of slow-moving items allows the ward to request stock transfers before medications expire on the shelf.

Ensuring Rational Dispensing and Patient Safety

Rational drug use means patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community.

Nurses contribute to rational drug use by supporting adherence counseling and monitoring for adverse drug reactions. When patients understand how and why to take their medications, compliance improves, reducing the cycle of treatment failure and repeated hospital admissions. Furthermore, nurses must cross-check prescriptions against patient allergies and weight, especially in pediatric wards, preventing accidental toxicity or therapeutic failure.

Financial leakage can ruin a DRF faster than any other factor. Nurses who handle revenue collection points in primary healthcare settings or specialized clinics must maintain absolute transparency. Every monetary transaction must be backed by an official receipt, and cash must never be mixed with personal funds or kept in unsecure desk drawers. Reconciling daily prescription logs with cash collections ensures that every tablet accounted for matches the financial returns.

Challenges and Solutions in Sustaining the DRF System

Sustaining a Drug Revolving Fund in resource-constrained environments comes with significant operational hurdles. Common challenges include inflation eroding capital, bureaucratic delays in procurement, poor political will, and patient default on payments due to poverty.

To overcome these hurdles, healthcare facilities must implement realistic pricing reviews that keep pace with market changes without pricing drugs out of the reach of the poor. Establishing strong institutional committees that include nurses, pharmacists, and accountants ensures multidisciplinary oversight. Continuous staff training on inventory management and transparent bookkeeping keeps the system resilient against failure.
