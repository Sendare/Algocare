Antibiotics and Anti-infective Drugs

Introduction to Antibiotics and Anti-Infective Pharmacology

Anti-infective pharmacology deals with medications designed to treat infections caused by microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. The primary goal of antimicrobial therapy is to selectively destroy or inhibit the invading pathogen without causing significant harm to the host cells. This concept of selective toxicity is fundamental to pharmacology.

Antibiotics specifically target bacterial infections. They are broadly categorized based on their spectrum of activity into narrow-spectrum and broad-spectrum agents. Narrow-spectrum antibiotics are effective against a limited group of bacteria, making them ideal when the specific causative organism is known because they spare normal flora. Broad-spectrum antibiotics cover a wide range of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, serving as life-saving therapy in acute, severe infections before culture and sensitivity results return.

Mechanisms of Action and Classification of Antibacterial Agents

Antibacterial drugs disrupt vital bacterial life processes. Understanding these mechanisms helps nurses anticipate drug interactions and adverse effects.

  • Inhibition of cell wall synthesis: Drugs like penicillins and cephalosporins weaken the peptidoglycan layer, causing osmotic lysis.
  • Protein synthesis inhibition: Agents like macrolides and aminoglycosides bind to bacterial ribosomes to halt protein production.
  • Inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis: Fluoroquinolones interfere with enzymes necessary for DNA replication.
  • Antimetabolite activity: Sulfonamides block essential folic acid synthesis pathways inside the bacterial cell.

Antiviral, Antifungal, and Antitubercular Drugs

Treating infections caused by viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria requires specialized pharmaceutical agents because these pathogens differ structurally from typical bacteria.

  • Antivirals: Drugs like acyclovir interfere with viral nucleic acid replication. They suppress viral load but rarely cure the underlying infection completely.
  • Antifungals: Polyenes such as amphotericin B and azoles target fungal cell membranes by binding to ergosterol, altering membrane permeability.
  • Antitubercular drugs: Mycobacterium tuberculosis requires long-term multi-drug regimens, typically combining isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol to prevent drug resistance.

Antimicrobial Resistance and Nursing Implications

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when pathogens survive exposure to drugs designed to kill them. Overuse and misuse of anti-infective agents in healthcare and agriculture accelerate this process in Nigeria. When patients stop taking antibiotics early or share leftover prescriptions, resistant strains multiply and spread.

Nurses play a frontline role in antimicrobial stewardship. This involves verifying culture and sensitivity results before continuing broad-spectrum therapy, educating patients on completing full drug courses, and ensuring proper hand hygiene to prevent cross-contamination in hospital wards.

Patient Assessment and Pre-Administration Responsibilities

Before administering any anti-infective agent, thorough nursing assessment prevents severe adverse events and treatment failures. Nurses must check for known drug allergies, especially to penicillins and sulfonamides. Renal and liver function test results are crucial because many anti-infectives are metabolized or excreted through these organs.

Furthermore, obtaining clinical specimens for laboratory culture and sensitivity testing *before* giving the first dose of an antibiotic is a golden rule. Starting drugs beforehand can sterilize the site temporarily, leading to false-negative culture results and blind treatment choices.

Administration Guidelines and Adverse Effect Monitoring

Safe administration of anti-infectives requires strict adherence to timing, route, and dilution guidelines. Intravenous infusions must be monitored closely for signs of phlebitis or hypersensitivity reactions.

Nurses must monitor for common and severe adverse reactions:

  • Allergic reactions: Rashes, pruritus, and life-threatening anaphylaxis.
  • Superinfections: Overgrowth of opportunistic organisms like Candida albicans due to normal flora destruction.
  • Organ toxicity: Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity linked to aminoglycosides.

Patient Education and Clinical Stewardship

Patient education bridges the gap between hospital discharge and successful recovery at home. Nurses must teach patients to finish their entire course of antibiotics even after symptoms subside. Stopping medication early leaves surviving bacteria to multiply and cause relapse.

Practical guidance should include instructions on managing mild side effects, recognizing signs of allergic reactions, and avoiding the sharing of leftover drugs with family members. Clear communication in local languages ensures better compliance across diverse Nigerian patient populations.
