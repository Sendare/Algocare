Sympathomimetics

Introduction to Sympathomimetics and the Sympathetic Nervous System

Sympathomimetics are a class of drugs that mimic the effects of the sympathetic nervous system, often called the fight-or-flight system. When these drugs bind to adrenergic receptors, they trigger physiological responses designed to help the body handle stress. In the respiratory system, this activation leads to the relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle, opening up narrowed airways and making it easier to breathe. Understanding this foundation helps nurses anticipate how patients will react to bronchodilator therapy during an acute respiratory crisis.

Mechanism of Action in the Respiratory Tract

At the cellular level, respiratory sympathomimetics target specific adrenergic receptors located on airway smooth muscle cells.

  • Beta-2 adrenergic receptors: Stimulation of these receptors activates intracellular adenylate cyclase, increasing cyclic AMP (cAMP).
  • cAMP accumulation: Higher levels of cAMP reduce intracellular calcium concentrations within the muscle cells.
  • Bronchodilation: The drop in available calcium prevents muscle fibers from sliding together, causing the bronchioles to dilate.

This cascade explains why these agents reverse acute bronchoconstriction rapidly.

Classification of Respiratory Sympathomimetics

Respiratory sympathomimetics are categorized based on their duration of action and receptor specificity. Clinicians use these categories to tailor treatment plans for either immediate rescue or long-term disease control.

Selecting the correct category prevents inappropriate drug use, such as relying on a maintenance medication during an acute asthma attack.

Short-Acting Beta-Agonists (SABAs) in Acute Care

Short-acting beta-agonists like salbutamol are the frontline defense during acute bronchospasm episodes, such as asthma attacks or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations. They act within minutes to restore airflow, relieving distressing symptoms like wheezing and shortness of breath. Nurses must monitor clients closely after administration, as frequent reliance on a rescue inhaler often signals worsening, poorly controlled underlying inflammation that requires escalation of anti-inflammatory therapy.

Long-Acting Beta-Agonists (LABAs) in Maintenance Therapy

Long-acting agents like formoterol and salmeterol provide sustained bronchodilation lasting twelve hours or longer. They are prescribed for daily maintenance therapy to prevent bronchospasm, particularly nocturnal symptoms in asthma or chronic bronchitis. LABAs must never be used as monotherapy in asthma management due to safety warnings regarding severe exacerbations; instead, they are combined with inhaled corticosteroids to target both bronchoconstriction and airway inflammation safely.

Clinical Administration and Nursing Assessment

Before administering any sympathomimetic, the nurse must perform a targeted respiratory assessment, noting baseline respiratory rate, breath sounds, oxygen saturation, and work of breathing. Post-administration checks are equally vital to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and detect early signs of systemic absorption. Nurses should also review the client's cardiac history, as adrenergic stimulation naturally increases heart rate and can precipitate arrhythmias or myocardial ischemia in vulnerable individuals.

Side Effects, Adverse Reactions, and Toxicity

Although inhaled delivery targets the lungs, systemic absorption of sympathomimetics is common and produces predictable side effects. Clients frequently report fine skeletal muscle tremors, nervousness, restlessness, and palpitations. At high doses or during toxicity, these agents can cause severe tachycardia, hypokalemia, and dangerous cardiac arrhythmias. Recognizing these adverse reactions allows nurses to reassure clients experiencing mild jitteriness while intervening promptly if true toxicity develops.

Patient Education for Safe Inhaler Use

Proper inhaler technique dictates whether medication reaches the distal airways or merely coats the oral cavity. Nurses must teach clients how to use Metered-Dose Inhalers (MDIs) correctly, recommending spacer devices to minimize local side effects like hoarseness and oral candidiasis. Clients should demonstrate the technique back to the nurse, ensuring they exhale fully, actuate the device while inhaling slowly, and hold their breath for ten seconds to maximize drug deposition.
