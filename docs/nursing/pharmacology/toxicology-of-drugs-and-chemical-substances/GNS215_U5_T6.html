Selection of Treatments for Toxic Reactions

Principles of Toxicology and Patient Stabilization

Managing a toxic reaction begins long before identifying the exact poison. Immediate safety and survival depend on the ABCDE approach: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. When a patient presents to a Nigerian emergency department or clinic following suspected poisoning, nurses must first secure a patent airway and check for adequate ventilation. Circulation is evaluated by assessing pulses, capillary refill, and blood pressure to manage early signs of shock or cardiovascular collapse. Disability checks neurological status using tools like the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale, while exposure involves fully stripping the patient to locate hidden transdermal patches, insect stings, or caustic chemical spills. Time is the critical variable in toxicology; rushing stabilization to chase a diagnostic laboratory test often leads to preventable patient deterioration.

Patient Assessment and History in Acute Poisoning

Gathering an accurate history in an acute poisoning case often resembles detective work. Nurses must interview conscious patients, relatives, or bystanders to answer the crucial toxicological questions: What substance was taken? How much was ingested? When did the exposure occur? What route was involved? Empty bottles, containers, or traditional herbal concoctions brought along by relatives provide invaluable clues.

Physical examination requires a systematic head-to-toe search for toxicological syndromes or toxidromes. Recognizing patterns of symptoms guides immediate treatment decisions when the exact poison remains unknown.

Gastrointestinal Decontamination and Preventing Absorption

Stopping the body from absorbing ingested toxins is a major therapeutic goal, though its usefulness decreases rapidly as time passes. Methods vary depending on the toxin and the elapsed time since ingestion.

  • Activated Charcoal: Administered orally or via a nasogastric tube, it adsorbs many toxins in the gut, preventing systemic absorption. It is ineffective for heavy metals, alcohols, and corrosives.
  • Gastric Lavage: Involves washing out the stomach using a large-bore tube. It carries a high risk of aspiration and perforation, so it is rarely performed unless the ingestion is life-threatening and recent (within one hour).
  • Whole Bowel Irrigation: Uses a balanced polyethylene glycol solution to flush the entire gastrointestinal tract, useful for sustained-release drugs or body packers.

Enhancement of Elimination

Once a poison has been absorbed into the bloodstream, interventions shift toward accelerating its removal from the body. Nurses assist with various clearance techniques depending on the chemical properties of the toxic agent.

Administration of Specific Antidotes

Antidotes act through various mechanisms to neutralize poisons, block receptor sites, or provide alternative metabolic pathways. Nurses must administer these agents with precision, monitoring for rapid therapeutic responses as well as potential adverse drug reactions.

Common antidotes include naloxone for opioid overdoses, flumazenil for benzodiazepines, atropine for organophosphate poisoning, and N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol toxicity. A common clinical mistake is assuming every poisoning has a specific antidote; in reality, supportive care remains the cornerstone of toxicology because true antidotes exist for only a small fraction of toxic substances.

Supportive Care and Symptomatic Management

Because specific antidotes are rare, keeping the patient alive through supportive care is the nurse's most vital duty. Treatment focuses on maintaining vital organ function until the poison is metabolized and excreted naturally.

Cardiovascular stability requires continuous ECG monitoring and intravenous fluid administration to treat hypotension. Respiratory support may range from simple oxygen therapy via nasal cannula to mechanical ventilation for depressed respiratory centers or severe aspiration pneumonitis. Seizures, frequently triggered by insecticides or tricyclic antidepressants, require prompt intravenous administration of benzodiazepines to prevent permanent brain damage and rhabdomyolysis.

Monitoring, Complications, and Nursing Care

Intensive monitoring prevents delayed complications in poisoned patients. Nurses maintain strict fluid balance charts, monitor urine output to protect renal function, and perform frequent neurological checks. Complications such as aspiration pneumonia, acute kidney injury from rhabdomyolysis, and compartment syndrome from prolonged immobilization require proactive nursing surveillance. Psychological evaluation is mandatory prior to discharge if the poisoning was intentional, ensuring the patient receives adequate psychiatric support before returning to the environment where the self-harm attempt occurred.
