Drugs Used in the Treatment of Hypertension

Introduction to Hypertension and Antihypertensive Therapy

Hypertension, or persistently high blood pressure, remains a major cardiovascular risk factor across Nigerian communities, often leading to stroke, renal failure, and heart failure if left uncontrolled. Antihypertensive therapy aims to reduce systemic vascular resistance and cardiac output to protect target organs. Treatment typically begins with lifestyle modifications—such as dietary salt restriction and regular exercise—before progressing to pharmacotherapy. Nurses play a critical role in baseline blood pressure assessment, identifying secondary causes, and monitoring patient compliance with long-term drug regimens.

Diuretics: Mechanisms, Types, and Nursing Considerations

Diuretics lower blood pressure by promoting renal sodium and water excretion, thereby reducing blood volume.

  • Thiazide diuretics: (e.g., bendroflumethiazide, hydrochlorothiazide) act at the distal convoluted tubule and are standard first-line agents.
  • Loop diuretics: (e.g., furosemide) act on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle, producing rapid and potent diuresis, frequently used in fluid overload or renal impairment.
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics: (e.g., spironolactone) prevent potassium loss in the collecting tubules, counteracting the hypokalemia caused by thiazides and loops.

Nurses must monitor serum electrolytes, counsel patients to take doses in the morning to prevent nocturnal enuresis, and watch for orthostatic hypotension.

Sympatholytics: Beta-Blockers, Alpha-Blockers, and Central Acting Agents

Sympatholytics blunt the sympathetic nervous system's effects on the cardiovascular system.

  • Beta-blockers: (e.g., atenolol, propranolol) block beta-receptors to decrease heart rate and myocardial contractility.
  • Alpha-1 blockers: (e.g., prazosin) cause peripheral vasodilation by blocking alpha receptors on vascular smooth muscle.
  • Central acting agents: (e.g., methyldopa) stimulate alpha-2 receptors in the brain to reduce sympathetic outflow.

Careful monitoring is required for bradycardia with beta-blockers, first-dose syncope with alpha-blockers, and rebound hypertension if central agents are abruptly stopped.

Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) Inhibitors

The RAAS cascade is a powerful hormonal regulator of blood pressure and fluid balance. Inhibiting this system provides profound cardiovascular and renal protection, especially in diabetic patients.

A persistent dry cough is a well-known side effect unique to ACE inhibitors due to bradykinin accumulation. Additionally, all RAAS inhibitors carry a risk of hyperkalemia and must be used with caution alongside potassium supplements.

Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

Calcium channel blockers inhibit the influx of calcium ions into vascular smooth muscle and myocardial cells, leading to coronary and peripheral vasodilation.

  • Dihydropyridines: (e.g., amlodipine, nifedipine) have a strong affinity for blood vessels, making them potent vasodilators that frequently cause peripheral edema and reflex tachycardia.
  • Non-dihydropyridines: (e.g., diltiazem, verapamil) exert greater negative inotropic and chronotropic effects on the heart itself, making them useful for controlling heart rate alongside blood pressure.

Nurses should assess for lower limb swelling and monitor heart rate before administering non-dihydropyridine agents.

Management of Hypertensive Emergencies and Urgencies

Hypertensive crises are divided into emergencies, where extremely high blood pressure causes acute target organ damage (such as encephalopathy, myocardial infarction, or aortic dissection), and urgencies, where pressure is severely elevated without acute organ damage.

Emergencies require immediate admission to an intensive care unit and intravenous antihypertensives like hydralazine or labetalol to lower blood pressure gradually. Dropping the pressure too fast can precipitate cerebral ischemia. Urgencies are managed with oral medications over 24 to 48 hours in an outpatient or ward setting.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Patient Education

Long-term management of hypertension relies heavily on patient adherence, which is often challenging because hypertension is largely asymptomatic. Nurses must provide clear education regarding lifestyle changes, potential side effects, and the danger of stopping medication abruptly, which can cause rebound hypertension. Regular blood pressure logging, proper technique for home monitors, and consistent follow-up visits are essential components of safe, effective nursing care in community and hospital settings.
