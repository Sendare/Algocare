Antiarrhythmic Drugs

Introduction to Cardiac Electrophysiology and Arrhythmias

The heart beats through a coordinated sequence of electrical impulses. At rest, cardiac cells maintain a negative electrical charge inside the cell membrane, known as the resting membrane potential. When an impulse arrives, rapid ion shifts across the cell membrane create an action potential, triggering myocardial contraction.

An arrhythmia occurs when this normal electrical pathway is disturbed. Disruptions stem from abnormal impulse formation, such as irritable ectopic pacemakers, or abnormal impulse conduction, like re-entrant loops where an impulse circles endlessly. Recognizing these triggers helps nurses understand why specific medications are ordered to restore a normal sinus rhythm.

Vaughan Williams Classification of Antiarrhythmic Drugs

Antiarrhythmic medications are organized using the Vaughan Williams classification system, which groups drugs primarily by their dominant electrophysiological mechanism on the cardiac action potential.

  • Class I: Sodium channel blockers, which slow conduction velocity.
  • Class II: Beta-blockers, which suppress sympathetic activity on the heart.
  • Class III: Potassium channel blockers, which prolong repolarization.
  • Class IV: Calcium channel blockers, which slow conduction through the AV node.
  • Other agents: Miscellaneous drugs like adenosine and digoxin that do not fit neatly into the main four groups.

Class I Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Sodium Channel Blockers

Class I drugs block cardiac sodium channels, slowing the rapid depolarization phase of the action potential. This category is subdivided into three groups based on how strongly they bind to the channel and how they affect repolarization:

  1. Class IA: Moderate sodium channel block; also prolongs repolarization (e.g., Procainamide).
  2. Class IB: Weak sodium channel block; shortens repolarization (e.g., Lidocaine).
  3. Class IC: Strong sodium channel block; marked slowing of conduction with little effect on repolarization (e.g., Flecainide).

A common clinical observation is that strong sodium blockade can paradoxically worsen arrhythmias or depress myocardial contractility in damaged hearts.

Class II Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

Beta-blockers blunt the effect of catecholamines like adrenaline on the heart. By competitive antagonism at beta-1 receptors, they decrease automaticity in the SA node, slow conduction velocity through the AV node, and reduce myocardial contractility.

These properties make beta-blockers highly effective for controlling heart rates in supraventricular tachycardias and preventing adrenergic-triggered ventricular arrhythmias. Common agents include metoprolol and propranolol.

Class III Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Potassium Channel Blockers

Class III drugs prolong the action potential and extend the refractory period by blocking potassium channels during repolarization. Because heart muscle cells remain refractory longer, they cannot be prematurely stimulated by stray electrical impulses.

Amiodarone is the most widely used drug in this class, though it also possesses properties of sodium, calcium, and beta-blockers. Because amiodarone accumulates in body tissues, nurses must monitor patients for multi-organ side effects, including pulmonary toxicity and thyroid dysfunction.

Class IV Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Calcium Channel Blockers

Class IV drugs are non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, specifically verapamil and diltiazem. They inhibit the influx of calcium ions through L-type channels, primarily in the SA and AV nodes.

This action slows AV nodal conduction and prolongs AV nodal refractoriness, making these drugs ideal for terminating re-entrant supraventricular tachycardias that depend on the AV node for propagation. They should be used cautiously in patients with pre-existing heart failure due to their negative inotropic effects.

Miscellaneous Antiarrhythmic Agents

Several agents do not fit standard Vaughan Williams classes but play vital roles in managing rhythm disorders:

  • Adenosine: A naturally occurring nucleoside that transiently blocks AV nodal conduction. It is given as a rapid IV push to terminate paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT). It has an extremely short half-life of a few seconds.
  • Digoxin: Enhances vagal tone to slow AV nodal conduction while increasing myocardial contractility, useful in atrial fibrillation.
  • Magnesium sulfate: Used specifically to treat Torsades de Pointes, a unique polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Administering antiarrhythmic therapy requires rigorous clinical vigilance because these drugs can provoke new arrhythmias or worsen existing ones. Nurses must continuously monitor cardiac telemetry for changes in heart rate, rhythm, and intervals such as the PR, QRS, and QT durations.

Baseline and ongoing assessments must include blood pressure, serum electrolyte levels, renal function, and hepatic panels. For drugs like amiodarone, periodic thyroid and pulmonary function tests are also required to catch organ toxicity early.
