Antipsychotic Drugs

Introduction and Classification of Antipsychotic Drugs

Antipsychotic medications, historically called major tranquilizers or neuroleptics, form the backbone of pharmacological management for severe psychiatric disorders characterized by psychosis, such as schizophrenia and bipolar mania. These agents help patients regain contact with reality by reducing hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking.

Pharmacologists broadly divide these medications into two distinct groups based on their chemical structure, receptor-binding profiles, and side effect tendencies:

  • First-generation antipsychotics (FGAs): Also known as typical or conventional antipsychotics, developed earlier and strongly associated with movement disorders.
  • Second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs): Also known as atypical antipsychotics, developed later with a broader receptor profile and a lower risk of extrapyramidal side effects.

Mechanism of Action in the Central Nervous System

The therapeutic efficacy of antipsychotics hinges primarily on their interaction with neurotransmitter receptors in the brain, most notably dopamine.

  1. Dopamine D2 Receptor Blockade: Both classes block dopamine receptors in the mesolimbic pathway, which directly subdues positive symptoms of psychosis like hallucinations and delusions.
  2. Serotonin-Dopamine Antagonism: Second-generation agents additionally block 5-HT2A serotonin receptors, which helps alleviate negative symptoms and reduces the frequency of movement side effects.
  3. Other Receptor Systems: Antipsychotics frequently block muscarinic cholinergic, histaminergic, and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, explaining why these drugs cause diverse peripheral effects like sedation, dry mouth, and orthostatic hypotension.

First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics

First-generation antipsychotics are potent antagonists of central dopamine D2 receptors. Common examples include haloperidol, chlorpromazine, and fluphenazine. While highly effective at managing acute psychotic agitation and positive symptoms, their lack of receptor selectivity often leads to significant neurological side effects.

Because they block dopamine uniformly across all brain pathways, they frequently induce movement disturbances and elevate prolactin levels. Chlorpromazine tends to cause more sedation and blood pressure drops, whereas haloperidol carries a high potency for extrapyramidal reactions.

Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics

Second-generation antipsychotics revolutionized mental health care by offering effective psychosis management with a reduced incidence of movement disorders. Frequently prescribed agents include clozapine, olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, and aripiprazole.

These drugs combine dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism. Although they spare patients many extrapyramidal symptoms, they introduce a distinct metabolic burden. Nurses must monitor for significant weight gain, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and new-onset diabetes mellitus, particularly with clozapine and olanzapine.

Adverse Effects and Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

Blocking dopamine pathways outside the therapeutic target zones generates several distinct neurological movement disorders collectively known as extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS).

Beyond EPS, nurses should watch for anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention), sedation, and orthostatic hypotension resulting from alpha-adrenergic blockade.

Management of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare, unpredictable, but life-threatening medical emergency associated with the use of antipsychotic medications. It can develop at any point during therapy, though it most often appears shortly after initiation or following a dosage increase.

Classic clinical manifestations include extreme hyperthermia (often exceeding 40°C), severe lead-pipe muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability demonstrated by labile blood pressure, tachycardia, and profuse diaphoresis.

Immediate nursing and medical interventions:

  1. Discontinue the offending antipsychotic medication immediately.
  2. Initiate aggressive supportive care, including rapid cooling blankets and intravenous fluid resuscitation.
  3. Administer prescribed medical treatments such as dantrolene (a muscle relaxant) or bromocriptine (a dopamine agonist) to reverse the blockade.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care for patients prescribed antipsychotics requires vigilant monitoring for both therapeutic efficacy and adverse reactions. Nurses must establish baseline assessments before drug administration and maintain consistent surveillance throughout the treatment course.

Monitoring protocols include regular checks of blood pressure for orthostatic changes, tracking body weight and waist circumference for metabolic shifts, and assessing for early signs of movement disorders using standardized rating scales like the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS). Patient education is equally critical, emphasizing the importance of adherence, avoiding abrupt cessation to prevent withdrawal psychosis, and recognizing when to report dangerous symptoms like high fever or severe sore throat.
