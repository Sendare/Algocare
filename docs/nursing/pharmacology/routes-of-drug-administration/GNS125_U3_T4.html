Oral Route

Introduction and Physiological Principles of the Oral Route

The oral route involves taking medication by mouth for systemic or local gastrointestinal effects. It remains the most common, convenient, and cost-effective method of drug delivery in healthcare settings across Nigeria. Once swallowed, solid medications dissolve in the gastrointestinal fluids before absorption can begin.

Absorption primarily occurs across the epithelial cell membrane of the gastrointestinal tract. Most drugs move passively down a concentration gradient via lipid diffusion. Factors like gastric pH, mucosal surface area, and local blood flow dictate how quickly a drug crosses into the bloodstream. The stomach provides an acidic environment that favors the absorption of weak acids, while the alkaline nature of the small intestine promotes the absorption of weak bases.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Oral Medications

Clinical decision-making requires weighing the benefits and drawbacks of giving drugs by mouth.

  • Convenience: Patients can self-administer pills without special technical skills.
  • Economy: Mass production of tablets lowers treatment costs compared to injectables.
  • Safety: The digestive tract allows for retrieval or delayed absorption in cases of accidental overdose, unlike intravenous lines.
  • First-pass metabolism: Hepatic enzymes alter many drugs before they reach systemic circulation, reducing potency.
  • Variable absorption: Presence of food, GI motility disorders, and digestive enzymes can unpredictably alter drug levels.
  • Patient compliance: Unconscious, vomiting, or dysphagic patients cannot take oral medications safely.

Types of Oral Dosage Forms

Manufacturers formulate oral drugs in various physical states to control release rates and protect active ingredients.

Tablets contain active drug compressed with binders and fillers; some feature scored lines for splitting or enteric coatings to resist stomach acid. Capsules enclose medication in a gelatin shell that dissolves quickly in gastric fluid. Liquid preparations like syrups, elixirs, and suspensions eliminate the need for dissolution, leading to faster absorption rates. However, liquid suspensions require thorough shaking prior to every dose to ensure uniform distribution of solid particles.

The First-Pass Effect and Bioavailability

The first-pass effect is a phenomenon where the concentration of a drug is greatly reduced before it reaches the systemic circulation. After an oral drug is absorbed from the digestive tract, it enters the mesenteric blood network and flows directly through the portal vein into the liver. Hepatic enzymes metabolize a significant portion of the active drug into inactive metabolites before it ever reaches the heart and general circulation.

Bioavailability refers to the fraction of an administered drug that reaches systemic circulation unchanged. Drugs with a high first-pass metabolism have low oral bioavailability, often requiring much higher oral doses compared to parenteral routes to achieve a therapeutic effect.

Factors Affecting Oral Drug Absorption

Numerous physiological and environmental variables alter how a patient absorbs oral medications. Gastrointestinal motility plays a major role; severe diarrhea rushes drugs past absorptive surfaces too quickly, while delayed gastric emptying keeps drugs in the stomach longer.

Food interactions can either hinder or enhance absorption. High-fat meals delay stomach emptying, while certain dairy products bind to specific antibiotics like tetracyclines, forming insoluble compounds that the body cannot absorb. Furthermore, concurrent administration of multiple drugs can alter gastric pH or bind active ingredients in the lumen.

Nursing Responsibilities and Administration Guidelines

Nurses play a critical role in safe medication administration by adhering to established protocols. Always verify the patient's identity using two identifiers before giving any medication. Assess swallowing ability and check for gastrointestinal contraindications, such as active nausea or bowel obstruction, prior to administration.

Never crush sustained-release tablets, enteric-coated pills, or sublingual medications unless specifically cleared by a pharmacist or manufacturer guideline, as crushing destroys their special delivery mechanisms and can cause toxicity. Position the patient upright at a 45 to 90-degree angle to prevent aspiration. Finally, document the administration immediately after the patient swallows the medication.

Special Considerations for Vulnerable Populations

Administering oral medications to pediatric and geriatric patients requires specialized clinical adjustments. Older adults often experience physiological changes such as reduced gastric acid secretion, decreased gastrointestinal blood flow, and slower gastric emptying. Age-related decline in hepatic and renal function also increases the risk of drug accumulation and toxicity.

Pediatric patients require careful weight-based dose calculations using milligrams per kilogram. Young children often struggle to swallow solid pills safely, making flavored liquid suspensions or chewable tablets more appropriate choices. Nurses must verify all calculations with a colleague to prevent severe medication errors in vulnerable groups.
