Rectal Route

Introduction and Anatomical Considerations

The rectal route involves administering medications directly into the rectum for either local or systemic effects. The lower GI tract provides a unique absorptive surface, particularly useful when oral administration is impossible.

  • Rich vascular supply: The rectum contains a dense network of blood vessels, including the inferior, middle, and superior rectal veins.
  • First-pass metabolism bypass: Drugs absorbed via the inferior and middle rectal veins drain directly into the inferior vena cava, entering systemic circulation before passing through the liver.
  • Mucosal surface: The rectal mucosa is single-layered and columnar, allowing for efficient passive diffusion of many drug molecules.

Dosage Forms Used Rectally

Several pharmaceutical preparations are formulated specifically for rectal insertion. Choosing the correct form ensures proper drug release and absorption.

  1. Suppositories: Solid or semi-solid preparations that melt, soften, or dissolve at body temperature, releasing the active drug into the rectal mucosa.
  2. Enemas: Liquid suspensions or solutions instilled into the rectum, used mainly for local evacuation or targeted anti-inflammatory delivery.
  3. Rectal Gels and Ointments: Viscous preparations applied with applicators, frequently used for localized pain or itching associated with hemorrhoids.

Clinical Indications and Advantages

The rectal route serves as a valuable alternative in clinical practice when other routes are compromised. It offers specific physiological and practical benefits for diverse patient populations.

A patient experiencing persistent vomiting cannot retain oral medications, making the rectal route a reliable alternative. Pediatric patients who struggle with swallowing bitter pills often receive antipyretics via suppositories. Unconscious patients or those experiencing severe seizures require rapid intervention where intravenous access is delayed. Additionally, drugs that cause severe gastric irritation or undergo severe degradation by stomach acid are well-suited for rectal delivery.

Disadvantages and Contraindications

Despite its usefulness, rectal administration has notable limitations. Absorption can be erratic, unpredictable, and influenced by the presence of fecal matter in the lower bowel.

Local irritation from repeated administration can cause inflammation or proctitis. Patient acceptability is often low due to cultural beliefs, discomfort, or loss of privacy. Furthermore, certain clinical conditions prohibit the use of this route entirely.

  • Rectal bleeding: Active hemorrhage contraindicates insertion to prevent further trauma.
  • Recent rectal or bowel surgery: Insertion can disrupt fragile suture lines.
  • Severe diarrhea: The explosive evacuation prevents adequate drug retention and absorption.

Nursing Responsibilities and Administration Procedure

Safe administration of rectal medications requires careful preparation, patient communication, and adherence to infection control standards.

  1. Verify the medical order and check for any contraindications such as recent surgery or bleeding.
  2. Provide privacy and explain the procedure to the patient to reduce anxiety and gain cooperation.
  3. Put on clean gloves and position the patient in the left lateral Sims position to align with the natural curve of the lower bowel.
  4. Lubricate the rounded tip of the suppository with a water-soluble lubricant to ease insertion.
  5. Instruct the patient to take slow, deep breaths through the mouth to relax the anal sphincter.
  6. Gently insert the suppository past the internal anal sphincter (about 2.5 to 5 cm in adults).
  7. Instruct the patient to remain lying down for 15 to 20 minutes to prevent expulsion of the medication.

Patient Education and Safety

Educating the patient promotes compliance and ensures proper self-administration when discharged with rectal medications. Patients should understand how to store suppositories properly, usually in a cool place or refrigerator, because they melt at room temperature.

Instruct patients to remove any foil or plastic wrapping before insertion, as leaving the wrapper on prevents drug absorption and can cause physical trauma. Emphasize the importance of retaining the medication for the recommended duration before attempting bowel evacuation. Advise patients to report unusual symptoms such as persistent bleeding, severe cramping, or local irritation immediately.
