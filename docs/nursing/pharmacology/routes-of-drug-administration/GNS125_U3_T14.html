Intracardiac Route

Introduction and Indications for Intracardiac Administration

The intracardiac route involves the direct injection of medication into the heart chambers, most commonly the left or right ventricle. This is an absolute last-resort emergency procedure used primarily when cardiac arrest occurs and all other routes of administration, such as intravenous or intraosseous, are completely unavailable or ineffective. Because the drug goes straight into the heart muscle or chamber, it circulates through the coronary arteries and the rest of the body almost instantly.

Modern advanced cardiac life support guidelines have largely moved away from this route due to extreme risks, but nursing staff in Nigerian emergency departments and intensive care units must still understand it for rare, refractory arrest scenarios.

Anatomical Landmarks and Puncture Sites

Executing an intracardiac injection requires precise knowledge of surface anatomy to avoid puncturing major blood vessels or lung tissue.

  • Fourth Intercostal Space: The traditional needle insertion point is located at the fourth left intercostal space, close to the sternal margin.
  • Subxiphoid Approach: Alternatively, a subxiphoid angle can be used, directing the needle upward and backward toward the left shoulder.
  • Myocardial Depth: The clinician must pierce skin, subcutaneous fat, pectoral muscle, intercostal muscle, and the parietal pericardium before entering the ventricular wall.

Emergency Drugs Used Intracardiacally

Very few pharmacological agents are deemed suitable for direct cardiac injection. Epinephrine (adrenaline) is the classic drug administered this way to stimulate myocardial contractility and restore spontaneous circulation during profound cardiac arrest. Occasionally, atropine or calcium chloride may be considered if peripheral access is entirely absent. The concentrated drug acts instantly upon the cardiac conduction system and myocardium, bypassing the normal systemic circulation time entirely.

Step-by-Step Nursing Assistance and Procedure

During an intracardiac injection, the nurse plays a vital support role to the physician performing the procedure.

  1. Prepare Equipment: Assemble a long, spinal-type needle, a syringe loaded with the emergency drug, and skin antiseptics.
  2. Expose the Chest: Quickly expose the patient's chest and locate the fourth intercostal space on the left side.
  3. Assist Resuscitation: Maintain continuous chest compressions or pause them strictly as instructed for the needle insertion.
  4. Monitor Response: Observe the cardiac monitor instantly for any return of rhythm or signs of complications.

Risks, Complications, and Contraindications

The intracardiac route carries severe hazards that explain why it is rarely used in modern clinical practice. Puncturing the heart muscle can cause myocardial laceration, leading to fatal internal bleeding or cardiac tamponade where blood fills the pericardial sac and prevents the heart from beating. Other risks include accidental coronary artery puncture, pneumothorax from hitting the lung, and intractable ventricular fibrillation. The procedure is strictly contraindicated if peripheral IV or intraosseous access can still be established.
