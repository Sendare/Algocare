Mucous Membrane Route

Introduction to Mucous Membrane Drug Administration

The mucous membrane route involves administering medications across moist epithelial linings throughout the body. These membranes line various tracts, including the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary systems. Because these tissues feature a rich blood supply and thin cellular barriers, they allow drugs to enter the systemic circulation rapidly. This route serves as an alternative when oral administration is ineffective or when rapid systemic effects are needed without using a needle.

Sublingual and Buccal Administration

  1. Sublingual route: Medication is placed under the tongue where it dissolves and absorbs directly into the blood through the sublingual vessels.
  2. Buccal route: Medication is placed between the cheek and the gum, dissolving slowly for local or systemic effects.
  • First-pass metabolism avoidance: Both methods bypass the portal circulation, meaning the drug enters systemic circulation directly before passing through the liver.
  • Patient instruction: Clients must not chew, swallow, or drink anything until the tablet dissolves completely.

Ophthalmic, Otic, and Nasal Administration

These localized routes target specific sensory organs and cavities.

  • Ophthalmic: Eye drops or ointments placed into the lower conjunctival sac. The nurse must prevent the dropper tip from touching the eye to maintain sterility.
  • Otic: Ear drops instilled into the external auditory canal. For adults, pull the pinna upward and back; for young children, pull it down and straight back.
  • Nasal: Sprays or drops delivered into the nasal passages. Patients should blow their nose gently before administration to clear mucus blockages.

Rectal and Vaginal Administration

Rectal and vaginal routes provide both local and systemic effects, particularly when oral intake is compromised due to nausea or unconsciousness. Suppositories depend on body temperature to melt and release the active drug.

Rectal administration requires inserting the suppository past the internal anal sphincter, resting against the rectal mucosa. Vaginal medications are available as creams, foams, tablets, or suppositories, usually administered using an applicator while the patient lies in the dorsal recumbent position.

Advantages and Limitations

Mucous membrane administration offers distinct clinical benefits alongside notable drawbacks.

  • Advantages: Avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism for sublingual and buccal routes; provides rapid absorption; useful when patients are unconscious or vomiting.
  • Limitations: Erratic or incomplete absorption can occur if secretions block the surface; local irritation is common; patient cooperation is strictly required.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Safe administration relies heavily on rigorous nursing assessment and clear communication. Nurses must inspect the target mucosal tissue for integrity, inflammation, or lesions before administering any agent. Educating the patient is equally vital; clients must understand the exact placement technique, the importance of not swallowing sublingual tablets, and the need to complete the full course of localized treatments like vaginal or otic preparations.
