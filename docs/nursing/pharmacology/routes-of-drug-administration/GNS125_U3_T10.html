Intrathecal Route

Introduction to the Intrathecal Route

The intrathecal route involves delivering medication directly into the subarachnoid space, bathing the spinal cord and cerebrospinal fluid. Bypassing the blood-brain barrier allows drugs to act immediately on central nervous system receptors with smaller doses. Nurses encounter this route frequently in obstetric anesthesia, chronic pain management, and specific chemotherapy protocols. Because the drug enters the cerebrospinal fluid directly, systemic side effects often decrease, but precision becomes critical.

Anatomical Considerations of the Subarachnoid Space

A successful spinal injection depends on precise anatomical landmarks. The spinal needle must pass through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, and dura mater before entering the subarachnoid space. Clinicians typically target the L3-L4 or L4-L5 interspace, well below the termination of the spinal cord at L1 or L2, minimizing the risk of direct neural trauma.

Indications and Clinical Uses

  • Spinal Anesthesia: Rapid sensory and motor blockade for lower abdominal, pelvic, and lower extremity surgeries.
  • Analgesia: Administration of local anesthetics or opioids for acute labor pain or intractable chronic pain.
  • Chemotherapy: Delivery of agents like methotrexate directly to the central nervous system for leukemia or lymphoma prophylaxis.
  • Spasticity Management: Infusing baclofen via implanted pumps for severe cerebral palsy or spinal cord injury spasticity.

Equipment, Preparation, and Aseptic Technique

Intrathecal procedures demand absolute sterility to prevent devastating central nervous system infections like meningitis. The tray typically contains spinal needles with stylets, antiseptic skin preparation solutions, sterile drapes, local anesthetic for the skin, and precise measuring gauges. The stylet inside the spinal needle prevents epidermal cells from being pushed into the subarachnoid space, which could cause spinal tumors. Nurses verify all medication vials independently, ensuring zero preservatives are present, as preservatives can cause severe neurotoxicity.

Nursing Responsibilities During Administration

During the procedure, the nurse assists the patient into a curled, lateral decubitus position or a seated position, arching the back to widen the interspinous spaces. Throughout insertion, the nurse monitors vital signs, observes respiratory effort, and offers verbal reassurance. The nurse also watches the patient's face and posture for sudden movements, warning the clinician if the patient shifts. Clear communication reduces anxiety and helps maintain the required sterile field.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Patient Care

Post-procedure care focuses on monitoring neurological function and hemodynamic stability. Nurses assess sensory blockade regression by testing dermatome levels and monitor motor function in the lower extremities before allowing out-of-bed activity. Monitoring vital signs at regular intervals detects early signs of hypotension or respiratory depression, especially if opioids were included in the injection. Patients who remain flat risk fewer headaches, though modern pencil-point needles have significantly reduced post-dural puncture headache rates.

Complications, Risks, and Emergency Management

  • Post-Dural Puncture Headache (PDPH): Caused by persistent cerebrospinal fluid leakage through the dural hole, characterized by a severe frontal-occipital headache worsening upon sitting.
  • Total Spinal Anesthesia: A catastrophic event where the local anesthetic ascends too high, causing profound hypotension, apnea, and loss of consciousness requiring immediate airway support.
  • Infection: Meningitis or epidural abscess presenting with fever, nuchal rigidity, and severe back pain.
  • Hematoma: Spinal hematoma compressing the nerve roots, requiring urgent neurosurgical evaluation.
