Sublingual Route

Introduction and Anatomical Basis

The sublingual route involves placing a medication under the tongue, where it dissolves and enters the bloodstream directly. The floor of the mouth features a rich network of blood vessels, primarily supplied by the sublingual artery, a branch of the lingual artery. The mucosa beneath the tongue is remarkably thin and non-keratinized, featuring a high density of capillaries. These structural traits create an ideal bed for rapid drug diffusion, bypassing the harsh environment of the gastrointestinal tract entirely.

Mechanism of Absorption and Bioavailability

Once a sublingual tablet dissolves in the minimal moisture of the oral cavity, the drug molecules cross the epithelial barrier down a concentration gradient. Because venous drainage from the oral cavity flows straight into the internal jugular vein via the lingual veins, absorbed medications enter the systemic circulation immediately. This direct pathway completely avoids the portal circulation system, bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism. As a result, drugs administered this way often exhibit significantly higher bioavailability compared to standard oral tablets.

Advantages of Sublingual Administration

  • Rapid onset: Drugs reach peak plasma concentrations quickly, which is vital during acute emergencies like angina attacks.
  • Bypassing degradation: Acid-labile drugs are protected from destruction by stomach acids and digestive enzymes.
  • Convenience: Useful for patients who have severe dysphagia, persistent vomiting, or are unable to swallow conventional pills.
  • Easy termination: Absorption can be halted by spitting out the remaining tablet if severe adverse effects begin.

Disadvantages and Limitations

Despite clear benefits, the sublingual route has notable constraints:

  • Limited surface area: Only potent drugs that require small doses can use this route effectively.
  • Taste issues: Many medications have unpleasant or bitter tastes, reducing patient compliance.
  • Patient compliance: Eating, drinking, or talking too soon can dislodge the tablet and ruin the dose.
  • Mucosal damage: Frequent use of certain irritating formulations can cause localized inflammation or ulceration of the oral mucosa.

Common Medications and Clinical Uses

Clinicians select the sublingual route primarily for fast-acting interventions. Nitroglycerin tablets or sprays are the classic example, used for prompt relief of chest pain during myocardial ischemia. Other common agents include buprenorphine for opioid dependence or pain management, and certain rapid-acting anti-hypertensive drugs like nifedipine (though sublingual use of nifedipine is now used cautiously due to unpredictable drops in blood pressure). Hormones and anti-emetics are also sometimes formulated for mucosal absorption.

Nursing Administration Guidelines and Patient Education

Safe administration requires clear instruction. The nurse must advise the patient to place the tablet under the tongue and keep it there until fully dissolved. Instruct the patient not to eat, drink, or smoke while the tablet is dissolving, as this can wash the drug away or alter absorption. If the mouth is excessively dry, a small sip of water can be given before administration to moisten the mucosa, but the tablet itself must not be swallowed whole.
