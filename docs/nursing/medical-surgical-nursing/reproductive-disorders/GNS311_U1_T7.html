Management of Altered Female Physiological Processes

Understanding Altered Female Physiological Processes

Female physiological processes span menarche, the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause. Alterations occur when these normal hormonal or anatomical rhythms deviate from the expected baseline. In clinical practice across Nigeria, nurses frequently encounter patients presenting with dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia, amenorrhea, and menopausal transition symptoms. Recognizing these variations requires a firm grasp of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and how disruptions manifest physically and emotionally in patients.

Classification and Common Clinical Types

  • Menorrhagia: Excessive menstrual blood loss exceeding 80 millilitres per cycle or lasting longer than seven days.
  • Amenorrhea: The absence of menstruation, categorized into primary (failure of menses by age 15 with secondary sexual characteristics) and secondary (cessation of established menses for six months).
  • Dysmenorrhea: Severe, painful uterine cramping during menstruation, classified as primary (no organic pathology) or secondary (linked to conditions like endometriosis).
  • Premenstrual Syndrome: A combination of physical and mood disturbances occurring during the luteal phase of the cycle.

Etiology and Predisposing Factors

Altered female physiological processes stem from diverse origins. Endocrine imbalances, such as polycystic ovary syndrome, disrupt ovulation. Structural abnormalities like uterine fibroids or polyps alter the endometrial cavity, causing abnormal bleeding. Systemic illnesses, thyroid disorders, extreme weight fluctuations, and chronic stress also interfere with gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulsation. Identifying these underlying triggers guides targeted clinical intervention.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment

A thorough nursing assessment begins with a detailed menstrual and obstetric history, noting cycle length, duration, and pad count per day. Physical examination involves abdominal palpation and a pelvic exam when appropriate and within scope. Diagnostic evaluations often include serum hormone panels (FSH, LH, prolactin, thyroid-stimulating hormone), a full blood count to check for anemia resulting from blood loss, and pelvic ultrasonography to visualize pelvic structures.

Medical and Pharmacological Interventions

Pharmacological management aims to regulate cycles, reduce bleeding, and alleviate pain. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, easing primary dysmenorrhea and reducing blood loss. Hormonal contraceptives, including oral pills and progestin-releasing intrauterine devices, thin the endometrium and stabilize hormonal fluctuations. Iron supplementation is routinely prescribed for patients with anemia secondary to menorrhagia.

Surgical Options and Perioperative Care

When medical therapy fails, surgical interventions become necessary. Procedures range from minimally invasive hysteroscopic removal of polyps or submucosal fibroids to endometrial ablation and hysterectomy. Perioperative nursing care involves assessing baseline laboratory results, providing informed consent support, monitoring for postoperative hemorrhage, managing pain, and encouraging early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis.

Nursing Care Planning and Patient Education

Effective nursing care plans focus on managing acute pain, relieving fatigue from anemia, and addressing body image disturbances related to reproductive alterations. Patient education is vital for demystifying conditions and ensuring adherence to treatment regimens. Nurses must instruct patients on how to track their menstrual cycles accurately, recognize warning signs like severe pelvic pain or heavy bleeding, and report side effects of hormonal medications promptly.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Unmanaged alterations in female physiology lead to significant physical and psychosocial complications. Chronic blood loss results in severe iron-deficiency anemia, causing generalized weakness, pallor, and reduced exercise tolerance. Untreated hormonal imbalances can lead to infertility, impacting family planning and mental well-being. With timely diagnosis and appropriate medical or surgical intervention, the overall prognosis is generally favorable, allowing patients to regain normal functioning and quality of life.
