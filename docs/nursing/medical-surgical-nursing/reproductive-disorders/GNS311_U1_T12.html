Pathophysiology of Female Reproductive Disorders

Hormonal Regulation and Dysregulation Mechanisms

The female reproductive cycle depends on a precise feedback loop between the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary gland, and ovaries. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which triggers the pituitary to secrete follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). These gonadotropins stimulate the ovaries to produce estrogen and progesterone.

Dysregulation at any level of this axis disrupts ovulation and menstruation. For instance, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) features an abnormal feedback loop where elevated LH levels relative to FSH lead to excess androgen production by the ovarian theca cells. Insulin resistance frequently worsens this imbalance by increasing ovarian sensitivity to LH and reducing hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin synthesis, which raises free circulating testosterone.

Pathophysiology of Menstrual Disorders

Menstrual abnormalities stem from structural uterine lesions, ovulatory dysfunction, or endocrinopathies. Menorrhagia involves heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding, often linked to local endometrial mechanisms such as impaired vasoconstriction or excessive local production of vasodilatory prostaglandins.

  • Amenorrhea: The absence of menstruation, categorized as primary (failure of menses to initiate by age 15) or secondary (cessation of established menses for three or more months).
  • Dysmenorrhea: Painful menstruation caused by excessive uterine contractions and ischemia mediated by high levels of endometrial prostaglandins during the luteal phase.
  • Oligomenorrhea: Infrequent menstrual cycles occurring at intervals greater than 35 days, typically reflecting chronic anovulation.

Mechanisms of Uterine Pathologies

Uterine structural abnormalities frequently disrupt normal endometrial shedding and myometrial function. Leiomyomas, commonly known as fibroids, are benign monoclonal tumors arising from smooth muscle cells of the myometrium. Growth is strongly influenced by estrogen and progesterone, which stimulate cellular proliferation and extracellular matrix accumulation.

Another common pathology is endometrial hyperplasia, characterized by an abnormal proliferation of endometrial glands relative to stroma. This condition is driven by prolonged, unopposed estrogen stimulation—meaning estrogen is present without adequate progesterone to induce secretory differentiation and regular shedding. Unopposed estrogen occurs during perimenopause, with anovulatory cycles, or from hormone-secreting ovarian tumors, carrying a risk of progression to endometrial carcinoma if atypical cells develop.

Pathology of Ovarian Disorders and Cysts

Ovarian cysts are fluid-filled sacs that develop on or within the ovary, often categorized as functional or neoplastic. Follicular cysts form when a mature follicle fails to rupture and release an ovum, continuing to grow instead. Corpus luteum cysts develop if the corpus luteum fails to regress, sealing its center and accumulating fluid or blood.

In contrast, Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) involves multiple small antral follicles arrested in development due to hormonal imbalances. The ovarian cortex becomes thickened and scarred from multiple failed ovulations, giving the classic polycystic appearance on ultrasound.

Pathophysiology of Endometriosis and Adenomyosis

Both endometriosis and adenomyosis involve the presence of ectopic endometrial tissue outside its normal location, yet their clinical presentations and anatomical sites differ.

Endometriosis is the presence of functional endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterine cavity, most commonly on the ovaries, uterosacral ligaments, and peritoneal surfaces. During menstruation, these ectopic foci bleed, causing localized inflammation, fibrosis, and adhesion formation. Adenomyosis involves the invasion of endometrial tissue directly into the uterine myometrium, causing the uterus to become uniformly enlarged, boggy, and tender due to hypertrophy of surrounding smooth muscle.

Infectious and Inflammatory Conditions of the Pelvis

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is an ascending infection of the upper female genital tract, typically originating from sexually transmitted pathogens like *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. Bacteria ascend from the vagina and cervix, traversing the endometrium to infect the fallopian tubes and surrounding peritoneal structures.

This infection causes acute inflammation, mucosal edema, and purulent exudate, leading to salpingitis (inflammation of the fallopian tubes). Over time, repeated or severe infections cause scarring, adhesions, and tubal occlusion, which significantly increase the risk of ectopic pregnancy and tubal factor infertility.

Malignancies of the Female Reproductive System

Cancers of the female reproductive tract arise from different cellular layers and exhibit distinct risk profiles. Cervical cancer is strongly linked to persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes, particularly HPV 16 and 18. Viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 inactivate host tumor suppressor proteins p53 and Rb, driving unregulated cellular proliferation in the transformation zone of the cervix.

Ovarian cancer, frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, often originates from the surface epithelium or fallopian tube epithelium. It spreads rapidly via peritoneal seeding throughout the abdominal cavity. Endometrial cancer typically presents early with abnormal uterine bleeding, facilitating prompt diagnosis in many cases.
