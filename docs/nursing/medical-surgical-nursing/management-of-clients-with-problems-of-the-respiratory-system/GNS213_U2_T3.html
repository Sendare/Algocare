Upper Airway Infections

Anatomy and Physiology of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract consists of structures outside the thorax, including the nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, pharynx, and larynx. These structures warm, filter, and humidify inspired air before it reaches the delicate lower airway tissues. The mucous membranes and ciliated epithelium trap foreign particles and pathogens, serving as the primary mechanical defense of the respiratory system. When these defensive barriers are compromised by viral or bacterial invasion, inflammation ensues, triggering localized symptoms such as congestion, rhinorrhea, and sore throat.

Common Etiologies and Pathophysiology

Most upper airway infections are viral in origin, with rhinoviruses, influenza viruses, and adenoviruses being the most frequent culprits. Bacterial infections, such as those caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, account for a smaller percentage but often present with more severe symptoms. Transmission typically occurs via droplet spread or direct contact with contaminated surfaces. Once pathogens attach to the mucosal epithelium, localized cellular destruction and immune response activation lead to vasodilation, edema, and excessive mucus production.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Patients with upper airway infections present with a constellation of symptoms depending on the specific site of inflammation.

  • Rhinitis: Sneezing, nasal congestion, and clear rhinorrhea.
  • Pharyngitis: Scratchy or painful throat, difficulty swallowing, and visible tonsillar exudate.
  • Laryngitis: Hoarseness or complete loss of voice.

During assessment, nurses must evaluate vital signs, inspect the oropharynx for redness or swelling, auscultate lung fields to ensure the lower airway remains clear, and assess respiratory effort.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosis of upper airway infections is primarily clinical, relying on patient history and physical examination. However, specific tests may be utilized when bacterial infection is suspected or during outbreaks.

Routine complete blood counts are rarely necessary for mild infections but may show leukocytosis in acute bacterial cases.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management focuses primarily on symptom relief because viral infections are self-limiting. Analgesics and antipyretics such as paracetamol help manage fever and body aches. Decongestants and antihistamines reduce nasal secretions and mucosal swelling. Antibiotics are strictly reserved for confirmed or strongly suspected bacterial infections, such as streptococcal pharyngitis, to prevent complications like rheumatic fever. Overusing antibiotics for viral colds contributes to global antimicrobial resistance.

Nursing Care Plan and Airway Management

The priority nursing diagnosis for severe upper airway infections is Ineffective Airway Clearance or Risk for Ineffective Breathing Pattern related to mucosal swelling and excessive secretions. Nurses encourage adequate fluid intake to thin respiratory secretions and promote comfortable breathing. Humidified air or steam inhalation can soothe irritated upper airway passages. Continuous monitoring for signs of airway obstruction, such as stridor, restlessness, or sternal retractions, is critical, particularly in vulnerable populations like young children.

Complications and Patient Education

While most upper airway infections resolve uneventfully, untreated or severe cases can lead to secondary complications such as acute otitis media, sinusitis, bronchitis, or peritonsillar abscess. Patient education focuses on infection control practices, including proper hand hygiene and covering the mouth when coughing or sneezing. Clients must understand the importance of completing the full course of prescribed antibiotics even if symptoms improve early, to ensure complete bacterial eradication and prevent recurrence.
