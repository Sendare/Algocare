Obstruction of the Upper Respiratory Airway

Anatomy and Physiology of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract consists of structures located outside the chest cavity, including the nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, and upper portion of the trachea. These structures are responsible for warming, filtering, and humidifying inspired air before it reaches the delicate gas-exchange units in the lungs. The larynx acts as a vital protective sphincter, preventing aspiration of food and foreign objects during swallowing through the coordinated closure of the epiglottis and vocal cords. Because this anatomical pathway is narrow, particularly at the level of the glottis and pharynx, even minor mucosal swelling or a small foreign body can significantly compromise airflow.

Definition and Pathophysiology

Upper airway obstruction refers to any blockage in the passage from the nose and mouth down to the trachea that impedes the free flow of air into the lungs. Pathophysiologically, this blockage creates an immediate increase in airway resistance. As the patient attempts to inhale against a resistance, negative pressure inside the pharynx increases, which can pull surrounding soft tissues inward and worsen the occlusion. Without prompt intervention, this mechanical barrier leads to alveolar hypoventilation, hypercapnia, severe hypoxemia, respiratory arrest, and ultimately hypoxic brain injury or death.

Etiology and Common Causes

Airway obstruction can arise from acute events or chronic disease processes. Recognizing the underlying cause dictates the emergency response required by the nurse and medical team.

  • Foreign Body Aspiration: Ingestion of food or objects, common in children or adults with impaired swallowing.
  • Soft Tissue Swelling: Angioedema, severe allergic reactions, burns, or infections like epiglottitis.
  • Structural Abnormalities: Laryngeal tumors, vocal cord paralysis, or subglottic stenosis.
  • Loss of Muscle Tone: Tongue falling back against the posterior pharyngeal wall in an unconscious patient.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Assessment of upper airway obstruction requires quick visual and auditory evaluation. Nurses must identify signs of distress before complete asphyxia occurs.

  • Stridor: A high-pitched, turbulent musical sound heard mostly on inspiration, indicating partial laryngeal or tracheal narrowing.
  • Retractions: Inward pulling of the skin around the ribs, sternum, and suprasternal notch as the patient uses accessory muscles.
  • Cyanosis: A late, ominous sign of inadequate oxygenation, seen around the lips and nail beds.
  • Behavioral Changes: Restlessness, anxiety, and confusion resulting from cerebral hypoxia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Rapid Assessment

When a patient presents with upper airway compromise, immediate clinical evaluation takes precedence over diagnostic imaging. Nurses must assess airway patency using the look, listen, and feel approach. Pulse oximetry helps monitor oxygen saturation, though it may lag behind acute changes in ventilation. In non-emergent or stabilized situations, soft-tissue neck radiography, direct laryngoscopy, or flexible bronchoscopy may be performed to pinpoint the exact site and nature of the obstruction.

Emergency Management and Immediate Interventions

Emergency management aims to restore airway patency without delay. For a conscious choking adult, the nurse or responder delivers back blows and abdominal thrusts, known as the Heimlich maneuver. If the patient loses consciousness, lower them to the floor and initiate cardiopulmonary resuscitation while checking for foreign objects before rescue breaths. For non-foreign body obstructions caused by soft tissue swelling, positioning the head with a chin-lift or jaw-thrust maneuver pulls the tongue forward. Insertion of an oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway may temporarily maintain patency until advanced airway management is secured.

Medical and Surgical Management

When non-invasive maneuvers fail or when obstruction stems from severe inflammation, medical and surgical interventions become necessary. Medical treatment often involves nebulized epinephrine to reduce mucosal edema in croup or anaphylaxis, alongside systemic corticosteroids. If medication fails to open the passage, advanced surgical procedures are required. Endotracheal intubation bypasses upper airway blocks. When facial trauma or severe laryngeal edema prevents oral or nasal intubation, an emergency cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy must be performed immediately to establish a surgical airway.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Post-intervention nursing care focuses on maintaining airway clearance, monitoring respiratory status, and preventing complications. Nurses must frequently suction artificial airways, monitor oxygen saturation, and observe for signs of respiratory fatigue. Patients recovering from airway obstruction or those with chronic swallowing difficulties require targeted education. This includes eating slowly, chewing food thoroughly, avoiding known allergen triggers, and recognizing early symptoms of respiratory distress so they can seek help before an emergency occurs.
