Under-Water Seal Drainage

Introduction and Principles of Under-Water Seal Drainage

Under-Water Seal Drainage (UWSD) is a specialized medical system used to re-establish normal negative pressure within the intrapleural space. The normal pleural cavity has sub-atmospheric pressure, which keeps the lungs expanded against the chest wall. When air, blood, or fluid enters this space due to trauma, surgery, or disease, the lung collapses. The UWSD acts as a one-way valve, allowing air and fluid to escape from the chest cavity while preventing anything from flowing back inside.

The system relies on gravity and water seals. The basic setup involves a collection chamber, a water seal chamber, and sometimes a suction control chamber. The distal end of the drainage tube sits beneath 2 centimeters of sterile water. This water column creates a seal: positive pressure from the patient's chest (during expiration or coughing) pushes air out through the water, bubbling it away. When the patient breathes in, the water rises slightly in the tube but cannot enter the chest because the suction or the water level blocks the return path.

Indications for Chest Tube Insertion

Chest tubes connected to UWSD systems are inserted when the integrity of the pleural space is compromised, disrupting normal respiratory mechanics.

  • Pneumothorax: Accumulation of air in the pleural space, which can be spontaneous or traumatic.
  • Hemothorax: Presence of blood in the pleural cavity, commonly resulting from chest trauma or thoracic surgery.
  • Pleural Effusion: Abnormal collection of fluid in the pleural space due to heart failure, infection, or malignancy.
  • Empyema: Presence of pus within the pleural cavity, requiring drainage and washing.
  • Chylothorax: Leakage of lymphatic fluid into the thoracic cavity from the thoracic duct.

Types of Chest Drainage Systems

Chest drainage systems have evolved from traditional glass three-bottle setups to modern, compact disposable plastic units.

System TypeStructure & Function
Traditional Three-Bottle SystemUses separate bottles for collection, water seal, and suction control. Heavy and prone to breakage.
Disposable Three-Chamber SystemCombines collection, water seal, and dry or wet suction control into one plastic unit. Most commonly used in modern wards.
Dry Suction SystemsUses a mechanical regulator instead of water to control suction intensity, allowing higher negative pressure levels.

Modern units simplify nursing care by integrating clear calibration markers on the collection chamber, making fluid volume measurement straightforward and accurate.

Pre-Procedural and Procedural Nursing Responsibilities

Preparing a patient for chest tube insertion requires both physical readiness and psychological reassurance. Nurses must ensure informed consent is signed and gather all necessary equipment, including the drainage system, sterile water, local anesthetic, suture materials, and a dressing kit. Baseline vital signs and oxygen saturation levels must be recorded.

During the procedure, position the patient semi-Fowler's or upright to facilitate access to the pleural space and prevent injury to the diaphragm. Support the patient emotionally and monitor their vital signs continuously for signs of shock, vagal response, or severe pain. Once the doctor inserts the tube and connects it to the UWSD, immediately secure all tubing connections with adhesive tape or plastic cable ties to prevent accidental disconnection. Tape all junctions securely, as an open joint allows atmospheric air to rush into the pleural space.

Ongoing Assessment and Monitoring of the Patient

Regular assessment prevents complications and tracks the patient's recovery. Monitor respiratory rate, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation every four hours or as ordered. Auscultate both lung fields to confirm bilateral air entry; diminished breath sounds on the affected side may indicate persistent lung collapse or a blocked tube.

Observe the water seal chamber closely. Normal tidaling—the rise and fall of the water level with the patient's breathing—indicates that the chest tube is patent and communicating with the pleural space. If tidaling stops abruptly, check for a kinked tube, dependent loops, or complete re-expansion of the lung. Measure drainage output every shift, noting the color and consistency of the fluid. Report any sudden surge of bright red blood exceeding 100 mL per hour immediately, as this signifies active hemorrhage.

Management and Maintenance of the Drainage System

Proper day-to-day management ensures the drainage unit functions efficiently. Always keep the drainage system below the level of the patient's chest to prevent gravity from pushing fluid back into the pleural space. Secure the unit to a stable stand or hook it to the bed frame to prevent accidental tipping.

Ensure tubing remains straight without loops or coils that can pool fluid and obstruct airflow. Never clamp a chest tube routinely during patient transfer or ambulation; clamping can quickly cause a tension pneumothorax by trapping accumulating air. Clamping is reserved only for specific moments, such as changing the drainage unit or assessing for an air leak. Maintain the fluid level in the water seal chamber at the 2 cm mark, adding sterile water if evaporation occurs over time.

Troubleshooting and Emergency Interventions

Emergency situations can arise rapidly with chest drainage systems, requiring swift nursing action.

  • Accidental Disconnection: If the tube pulls away from the unit, submerge the distal end of the chest tube in a container of sterile water immediately to create a temporary water seal and prevent air entry. Never leave the patient.
  • Chest Tube Pulls Out: If the tube slips completely out of the chest wall, cover the site instantly with a sterile petrolatum gauze dressing taped on three sides. This allows air to escape during exhalation while preventing air from being sucked in during inhalation. Notify the doctor immediately.
  • Continuous Bubbling in Water Seal: Indicates an air leak in the system. Systematically clamp the tubing starting close to the chest and moving downward. If bubbling stops when clamped near the patient, the leak is inside the chest or at the insertion site.

Chest Tube Removal and Post-Removal Care

Chest tubes are removed when the lung has fully re-expanded, air leaks have ceased, and drainage drops to minimal levels (typically less than 100-200 mL per 24 hours). The procedure is usually performed by a doctor, with the nurse assisting.

Explain the procedure to the patient and administer prescribed analgesics 30 minutes beforehand to ease discomfort. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath, hold it, and bear down (Valsalva maneuver) during removal. This increases intrathoracic pressure and prevents air from entering the pleural space as the tube is pulled out. The clinician applies an airtight petroleum jelly gauze dressing immediately over the insertion site, secured with heavy adhesive tape. Post-removal, monitor the patient for recurrence of respiratory distress, check vital signs, and observe the dressing for signs of bleeding or air leakage.
