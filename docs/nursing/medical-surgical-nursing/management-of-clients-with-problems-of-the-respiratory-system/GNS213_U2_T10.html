Clinical Manifestations of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Symptoms and Pathophysiology

Respiratory disorders disrupt the primary function of the lungs: gas exchange. When the airway, alveolar tissue, or pulmonary circulation becomes compromised, the body triggers specific clinical warning signs. Pathophysiological changes such as bronchospasm, mucosal edema, alveolar collapse, or pulmonary fibrosis alter lung compliance and resistance. These mechanical alterations force the respiratory muscles to work harder, generating symptoms like shortness of breath, unusual breathing patterns, and audible adventitious sounds. Nurses must connect these visible and audible clues directly to the underlying tissue damage or ventilation-perfusion mismatch occurring inside the chest.

Assessment of Dyspnea and Breathing Difficulties

Dyspnea, or subjective shortness of breath, is one of the most distressing symptoms a patient can report. Nurses evaluate dyspnea using standardized rating scales, noting its onset, duration, and triggering factors like exertion or lying flat.

  • Orthopnea: Difficulty breathing when lying flat, commonly seen in left ventricular failure or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Paroxysmal Nocturnal Dyspnea: Sudden awakening from sleep with severe shortness of breath, relieved by sitting upright.
  • Tachypnea: A persistent respiratory rate above normal limits, often an early sign of hypoxemia or metabolic acidosis.
  • Use of accessory muscles: Engagement of the sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles indicating severe respiratory distress.

Evaluation of Cough and Sputum Production

A cough is a protective reflex designed to clear the tracheobronchial tree of secretions and foreign irritants. When assessing a cough, determine whether it is dry and hacking or productive of sputum. Sputum characteristics provide critical diagnostic clues regarding the nature of the underlying pulmonary pathology.

Hemoptysis and Chest Pain

Hemoptysis involves coughing up blood from the lower respiratory tract and must be carefully differentiated from upper gastrointestinal bleeding or epistaxis. The volume of blood and its association with fever or weight loss helps isolate conditions like bronchiectasis, tuberculosis, or pulmonary malignancy.

Pulmonary chest pain requires urgent evaluation to rule out cardiac ischemia. Lung tissue itself lacks pain fibers, but inflammation of the parietal pleura creates sharp, localized pain that worsens sharply with inspiration or coughing. This pleuritic chest pain signals conditions such as pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, or pleural effusion.

Cyanosis and Gas Exchange Indicators

Cyanosis is a bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes caused by an excess of deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood capillaries. It is a late and sometimes unreliable indicator of hypoxia, especially in patients with severe anemia who lack sufficient hemoglobin to show a blue tint despite low oxygen levels. Central cyanosis affects the tongue and lips, pointing toward systemic arterial desaturation, whereas peripheral cyanosis affects fingers and toes, often resulting from vasoconstriction or sluggish blood flow.

Auscultatory Findings and Abnormal Breath Sounds

Auscultation reveals the acoustic properties of airflow through the respiratory tree. Normal vesicular sounds are soft and low-pitched over peripheral lung fields. Pathological findings include:

  • Crackles (Rales): Discontinuous, popping sounds heard during inspiration as collapsed alveoli snap open or fluid moves in airways.
  • Wheezes: Continuous, musical high-pitched sounds caused by air rushing through narrowed, constricted airways.
  • Stridor: A harsh, high-pitched monophonic inspiratory sound indicating upper airway obstruction.
  • Pleural Friction Rub: A grating, leather-like sound produced when inflamed pleural layers rub together.

Systemic and Chronic Manifestations

Long-standing respiratory disease profoundly affects the entire body. Chronic hypoxia forces the bone marrow to produce excess red blood cells, resulting in secondary polycythemia and increased blood viscosity. Over time, chronic pulmonary hypertension places an excessive workload on the right side of the heart, leading to cor pulmonale and peripheral edema. Finger clubbing, characterized by bulbous enlargement of the fingertips, often develops in chronic hypoxic conditions like bronchiectasis or lung cancer due to chronic tissue hypoxia and localized vasodilation.
