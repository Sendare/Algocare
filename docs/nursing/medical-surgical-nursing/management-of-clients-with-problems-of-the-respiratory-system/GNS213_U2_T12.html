Complications of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction and Pathophysiological Overview

Respiratory disorders rarely remain isolated to the airways and lung parenchyma. When primary conditions like pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or asthma progress unchecked, secondary systemic and local complications develop rapidly. The delicate mechanics of ventilation, gas exchange, and perfusion easily become disrupted, placing immense strain on both the respiratory system and distant organs. Recognizing early physiological shifts prevents irreversible tissue damage and systemic collapse.

Hypoxemia and Acute Respiratory Failure

Hypoxemia represents a deficient oxygen supply in arterial blood, which quickly escalates into acute respiratory failure when the respiratory system can no longer perform adequate gas exchange.

  • Type 1 Respiratory Failure: Characterized by hypoxemia with normal or low carbon dioxide levels, typically caused by ventilation-perfusion mismatch or shunt.
  • Type 2 Respiratory Failure: Defined by hypercapnia combined with hypoxemia, resulting from alveolar hypoventilation where the lungs fail to ventilate adequately.

Clinical signs include tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, cyanosis, and altered mental status as the brain registers oxygen deprivation.

Pulmonary Hypertension and Cor Pulmonale

Chronic hypoxia leads to pulmonary vasoconstriction, increasing vascular resistance within the pulmonary bed. Over time, sustained high pressure in the pulmonary arteries forces the right ventricle to work harder against increased afterload. This chronic strain results in right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual right-sided heart failure, a condition known as cor pulmonale. Nurses caring for patients with chronic lung diseases must monitor for peripheral edema, distended neck veins, and an enlarged liver as indicators of this progressive complication.

Atelectasis and Pleural Effusion

Alveolar collapse, or atelectasis, frequently occurs postoperatively or due to prolonged immobility and retained secretions, reducing the surface area available for gas exchange. Conversely, pleural effusion involves an abnormal accumulation of fluid within the pleural space, compressing the underlying lung tissue. Both conditions diminish breath sounds over the affected lung zones and cause progressive dyspnea. Identifying dullness to percussion assists clinicians in differentiating fluid accumulation from other pulmonary densities.

Pneumothorax and Hemothorax

Air or blood entering the pleural cavity disrupts the negative pressure required for lung expansion. A pneumothorax introduces air, while a hemothorax introduces blood, both leading to partial or complete lung collapse. A tension pneumothorax represents a life-threatening emergency where air enters the pleural space but cannot escape, shifting the mediastinum and compressing the heart. Immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube insertion remains vital for survival.

Acid-Base Imbalances

Respiratory efficiency directly dictates blood pH. When ventilation is impaired, carbon dioxide accumulates, forming carbonic acid and resulting in respiratory acidosis. Conversely, hyperventilation causes excessive elimination of carbon dioxide, leading to respiratory alkalosis.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous surveillance is essential to detect subtle deterioration in respiratory patients. Nurses must evaluate respiratory rate, rhythm, depth, and the presence of adventitious breath sounds like crackles or wheezes. Pulse oximetry provides continuous tracking of oxygen saturation, though clinicians must account for poor peripheral perfusion or nail polish interference. Monitoring lab values, particularly arterial blood gases and electrolyte panels, completes the clinical picture.

Collaborative Management and Patient Education

Managing respiratory complications requires coordinated intervention among nurses, physicians, respiratory therapists, and physical therapists. Treatment modalities include supplemental oxygen therapy, bronchodilator administration, chest physiotherapy, and mechanical ventilation support. Patient education focuses on teaching effective coughing techniques, pursed-lip breathing, and recognizing early warning signs of respiratory distress to prevent readmission.
