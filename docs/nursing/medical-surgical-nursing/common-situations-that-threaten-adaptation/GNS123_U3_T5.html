Shock

Introduction to Shock and Pathophysiology

Shock is a life-threatening medical condition where the cardiovascular system fails to deliver enough oxygen and nutrients to meet the metabolic demands of vital organs. At its core, shock is not just about low blood pressure; it is a profound state of cellular and tissue hypoxia. When cells are deprived of oxygen, they switch from aerobic metabolism to anaerobic metabolism. This shift produces inadequate adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and leads to a buildup of lactic acid, causing metabolic acidosis. Over time, the lack of energy causes cellular swelling, membrane damage, and the release of destructive intracellular enzymes, ultimately resulting in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). The body attempts to compensate for failing perfusion through neural, hormonal, and chemical mechanisms, such as the sympathetic nervous system releasing catecholamines to increase heart rate and constrict blood vessels, and the kidneys activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system to retain sodium and water.

Stages of Shock

Shock progresses through distinct clinical phases if the underlying cause remains uncorrected. Understanding these phases helps nurses intervene before irreversible damage occurs.

  1. Compensated Stage: Blood pressure remains within normal limits because homeostatic mechanisms like tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction successfully maintain perfusion to vital organs.
  2. Decompensated Stage: Compensatory mechanisms begin to fail. Blood pressure drops, systolic pressures fall below 90 mmHg, perfusion to the brain and kidneys becomes inadequate, and signs of end-organ dysfunction appear.
  3. Irreversible Stage: Cellular damage is so severe and widespread that organ failure is complete. Administration of fluids and vasoactive drugs no longer responds, leading inevitably to death.

Hypovolemic Shock: Causes and Nursing Care

Hypovolemic shock occurs when there is a significant reduction in intravascular volume. In resource-limited settings, this is frequently seen following postpartum hemorrhage, severe dehydration from gastroenteritis or cholera, and traumatic injuries with massive blood loss.

  • Hemorrhagic Causes: Trauma, gastrointestinal bleeding, ruptured ectopic pregnancy.
  • Fluid Loss Causes: Severe vomiting, diarrhea, burns, diabetic ketoacidosis.

Nursing care focuses on rapid volume replacement using isotonic crystalloids like normal saline or Ringer's lactate, and whole blood when available. Nurses must secure large-bore peripheral intravenous lines, monitor strict fluid balance through catheterization, keep the patient warm to prevent hypothermia, and position the patient flat or with legs slightly elevated to promote venous return.

Cardiogenic and Obstructive Shock

Cardiogenic shock stems from the heart's inability to pump effectively, most commonly due to a massive myocardial infarction, severe heart failure, or dysrhythmias. The pump failure leads to a drop in cardiac output despite adequate intravascular volume.

Obstructive shock, conversely, occurs when a physical obstruction prevents blood from entering or leaving the heart chambers. Common causes include massive pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, and cardiac tamponade.

Management differs fundamentally from hypovolemic shock because aggressive fluid administration can easily overwhelm a failing or obstructed heart, causing acute pulmonary edema. Nurses must monitor for jugular venous distension, muffled heart sounds, or tracheal deviation while supporting cardiac output with prescribed inotropic agents and relieving the mechanical obstruction.

Distributive Shock: Anaphylactic, Septic, and Neurogenic

Distributive shock is characterized by massive vasodilation and maldistribution of blood flow, leaving the actual blood volume normal but functionally inadequate.

  • Septic Shock: Triggered by severe infection, leading to a dysregulated host immune response, widespread vasodilation, capillary leak, and myocardial depression.
  • Anaphylactic Shock: An acute, life-threatening hypersensitivity reaction causing severe bronchospasm, vasodilation, and urticaria, typically triggered by drugs, insect stings, or foods.
  • Neurogenic Shock: Caused by spinal cord injury above T6, disrupting sympathetic nervous system output and resulting in profound vasodilation, hypotension, and classic bradycardia instead of reflex tachycardia.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring in Shock

Early recognition of shock saves lives. Nurses must assess key parameters that reflect tissue perfusion and oxygenation status. Vital signs show early trends: a rising heart rate and a narrowing pulse pressure often precede hypotension. Skin assessment reveals cool, pale, and clammy skin in hypovolemic and cardiogenic shock, whereas septic or neurogenic shock may present with warm, flushed skin initially. Capillary refill time becomes sluggish, exceeding three seconds. Neurological status is a sensitive indicator of cerebral perfusion; restlessness, confusion, or lethargy points to hypoxia. Urine output measured via an indwelling catheter is one of the most reliable indicators of end-organ perfusion, with a target output of at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour.

General Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management of shock centers on restoring tissue perfusion, supporting failing organs, and treating the underlying trigger. Oxygen therapy is administered immediately to maximize the oxygen content of available blood cells. Fluid resuscitation is tailored to the specific shock type, using crystalloids cautiously while monitoring hemodynamic responses. When fluids alone fail to restore adequate blood pressure, vasoactive medications are initiated. Vasopressors like adrenaline or noradrenaline cause peripheral vasoconstriction to raise systemic vascular resistance. Inotropic drugs such as dopamine or dobutamine strengthen myocardial contractility in cardiogenic or septic shock. In septic shock, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics must be administered within the first hour after obtaining blood cultures.

Nursing Interventions and Emergency Protocols in Nigerian Clinical Settings

Providing nursing care for patients in shock within resource-constrained Nigerian hospitals requires resourcefulness, prioritization, and teamwork. When power outages affect central monitors, nurses must rely on manual blood pressure measurements, frequent palpation of peripheral and central pulses, and vigilant bedside observation. Securing reliable intravenous access can be difficult in dehydrated or collapsed patients, requiring nurses to master peripheral cannulation or assist with cutdown procedures and central venous line insertion when permitted. Improvising with available resources, ensuring prompt administration of prescribed emergency drugs, maintaining detailed fluid balance charts, and reassuring anxious families are all essential daily competencies for the Nigerian clinical nurse.

Complications and Prognosis of Shock

Prolonged or inadequately treated shock triggers severe systemic complications that drastically worsen prognosis. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) frequently develops due to widespread activation of the clotting cascade, leading to simultaneous microvascular thrombosis and severe bleeding. Acute kidney injury (AKI) results from prolonged renal ischemia, often requiring dialysis. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) occurs when inflammatory mediators damage alveolar-capillary membranes, causing refractory hypoxemia. Ultimately, these complications converge into multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), where two or more organ systems fail simultaneously. The prognosis depends directly on how quickly the underlying cause is identified, the severity of the initial insult, and the promptness of resuscitation.
