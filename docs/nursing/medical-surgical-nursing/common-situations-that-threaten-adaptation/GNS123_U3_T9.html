Unconsciousness

Definition and Spectrum of Altered Consciousness

Unconsciousness is a state where a person is unable to respond to people and other environmental stimuli. It represents the extreme lower end of the spectrum of altered levels of consciousness. Nurses must differentiate between several clinical states along this continuum:

  • Confusion: Inability to think clearly and quickly, with impaired attention and decision-making.
  • Lethargy: A state of drowsiness where the patient falls asleep if not continuously stimulated.
  • Obtundation: A more profound reduction in alertness with lowered interest in environmental stimuli and slow response to sensory input.
  • Stupor: A condition of unresponsiveness from which the patient can only be awakened by vigorous and repeated physical or painful stimuli.
  • Coma: A state of complete unresponsiveness where the patient cannot be awakened even with maximal painful stimuli.

Anatomy of the Ascending Reticular Activating System

Arousal and wakefulness depend primarily on the intact function of the Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) and its projections to the cerebral hemispheres. The ARAS is a complex network of nuclei and fibers located in the brainstem, specifically spanning the pons, midbrain, and medulla.

To maintain consciousness, at least one functioning cerebral hemisphere must receive continuous stimulatory signals from the ARAS. Damage to both cerebral hemispheres or a direct lesion within the brainstem reticular core will disrupt this pathway, resulting in a loss of consciousness. Lesions that affect only one cerebral hemisphere typically cause focal neurological deficits rather than unconsciousness, unless swelling creates secondary brainstem compression.

Causes of Altered Consciousness: AEIOU TIPS

Etiologies of unconsciousness are broadly divided into structural brain lesions and diffuse metabolic or toxic insults. The mnemonic AEIOU TIPS helps clinicians systematically recall major systemic and metabolic causes:

  • A: Alcohol and Alkalo-toxins
  • E: Epilepsy, Encephalopathy, Endocrine disorders
  • I: Insulin (hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia)
  • O: Overdose (opioids, sedatives)
  • U: Uremia (renal failure)
  • T: Trauma, Temperature (hypothermia, heatstroke)
  • I: Infection (meningitis, sepsis)
  • P: Psychiatric conditions, Porphyria
  • S: Stroke, Shock, Space-occupying lesions

Pathophysiology and Herniation Syndromes

When intracranial pressure (ICP) rises due to hemorrhage, tumor, or edema, the brain tissue shifts from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure. This mechanical displacement is called brain herniation and rapidly compromises consciousness by compressing the brainstem.

  1. Uncal Herniation: The medial temporal lobe shifts downward through the tentorium cerebelli. This compresses the third cranial nerve, causing a fixed and dilated pupil on the affected side, followed by motor paralysis.
  2. Central Herniation: Downward displacement of the diencephalon and brainstem stretches the basilar artery, causing ischemia, pinpoint pupils, and hyperventilation.
  3. Tonsillar Herniation: Cerebellar tonsils push downward through the foramen magnum, compressing the respiratory and cardiac centers, which quickly leads to cardiorespiratory arrest.

Neurological Assessment Using the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the standard clinical tool for assessing and documenting the depth of impaired consciousness. It evaluates three distinct behavioral responses:

Assessment CategoryMaximum ScoreMinimum ScoreEvaluated Response
Eye Opening (E)41Spontaneous, to speech, to pain, or none
Verbal Response (V)51Oriented, confused, inappropriate words, incomprehensible sounds, or none
Motor Response (M)61Obeys commands, localizes pain, withdraws, abnormal flexion, extension, or none

The total score ranges from 3 (deep coma or brain death) to 15 (fully alert). A score of 8 or less generally indicates severe head injury and the need for airway protection.

Emergency Management and Airway Protection

Immediate management of an unconscious patient follows the standard ABCDE resuscitation framework. Preserving life and preventing secondary brain injury take absolute precedence over diagnostic investigations.

  • Airway: Assess patency. An unconscious patient often loses pharyngeal muscle tone, causing the tongue to obstruct the airway. Insert an oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway and prepare for endotracheal intubation if the GCS is 8 or lower.
  • Breathing: Check respiratory rate and effort. Administer high-flow oxygen to maintain adequate arterial oxygen saturation and prevent cerebral hypoxia.
  • Circulation: Check pulse, blood pressure, and capillary refill. Establish large-bore intravenous access and treat hypotension immediately to ensure adequate cerebral perfusion pressure.
  • Disability: Check pupillary reflexes and blood glucose. Administer IV 50% dextrose immediately if hypoglycemia is suspected.

Nursing Care and Complication Prevention

Unconscious patients depend entirely on the nursing team for survival and prevention of secondary complications. Nursing interventions must be systematic and routine:

  • Skin Integrity: Reposition the patient every two hours, use pressure-relieving mattresses, and keep skin clean and dry to prevent pressure ulcers.
  • Eye Care: Apply lubricating eye drops or ointment and tape eyelids shut if the corneal reflex is absent, preventing corneal drying and ulceration.
  • Joint Mobility: Perform passive range-of-motion exercises regularly to prevent joint contractures and venous stasis.
  • Hygiene and Elimination: Provide regular oral care to reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia risks, and monitor urinary output via an indwelling catheter.
