Fever

Normal Body Temperature Regulation and Thermoregulation

Body temperature is regulated primarily by the hypothalamus, which acts as the body's internal thermostat. Normal core body temperature typically ranges between 36.5°C and 37.5°C, fluctuating slightly throughout the day due to circadian rhythms, physical activity, and hormonal changes.

Thermoregulation involves a delicate balance between heat production and heat loss mechanisms.

  • Heat production: Generated mainly through cellular metabolism in the liver and skeletal muscle contraction, such as shivering.
  • Heat loss: Achieved via radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation of sweat from the skin surface.

When ambient temperatures or internal conditions shift, autonomic nervous system pathways trigger physiological responses like vasodilation to dissipate heat or vasoconstriction to conserve it.

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fever

Fever, medically known as pyrexia, is a temporary elevation in body temperature above the normal range, driven by an upward resetting of the hypothalamic set-point. This response is distinct from hyperthermia, where the body's set-point remains unchanged, but thermoregulatory mechanisms are overwhelmed by environmental heat or excessive metabolic production.

The pathophysiology of fever involves endogenous pyrogens, such as interleukins and tumor necrosis factor, released by immune cells in response to infection, inflammation, or tissue injury. These signaling molecules travel to the brain and stimulate the synthesis of prostaglandin E2 in the preoptic area of the hypothalamus. Prostaglandin E2 alters the firing rate of temperature-sensitive neurons, raising the thermal set-point. The body then perceives its normal temperature as too cold, triggering shivering and vasoconstriction to actively raise core temperature to the new target.

Etiology and Common Causes of Fever in Nigerian Clinical Practice

In Nigerian healthcare settings, fever is one of the most frequent presenting complaints across pediatric and adult wards. Infectious agents remain the leading cause, reflecting regional epidemiological profiles.

  • Malaria: Caused by Plasmodium species, particularly Plasmodium falciparum, marked by periodic chills, rigors, and high temperatures.
  • Typhoid Fever: Caused by Salmonella typhi, often presenting with a stepwise climbing fever, relative bradycardia, and abdominal discomfort.
  • Respiratory Tract Infections: Includes community-acquired pneumonia, acute bronchitis, and viral upper respiratory infections.
  • Urogenital Infections: Urinary tract infections, frequently linked to anatomical factors or catheterization.
  • Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers: Outbreaks such as Lassa fever require high clinical suspicion and strict barrier nursing precautions.

Documenting the pattern of a fever provides valuable diagnostic clues for the attending clinician.

Clinical Assessment and Vital Signs Monitoring

Assessing a febrile patient requires a systematic physical evaluation alongside frequent vital signs monitoring. Nurses must measure temperature using reliable routes—tympanic, oral, axillary, or core methods—while accounting for route-specific differences in accuracy.

Beyond the thermometer reading, evaluate hemodynamic parameters. Tachycardia frequently accompanies fever because every 1°C rise in body temperature typically increases the heart rate by about 10 beats per minute. Tachypnea may also develop as the body attempts to compensate for metabolic acidosis or increased oxygen demand. Monitor skin warmth, check for diaphoresis during the defervescence phase, and look for associated symptoms such as headache, muscle aches, rigors, or altered mental status.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Tests

Laboratory investigations help confirm the underlying etiology of a fever, guiding targeted antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory therapy.

  • Microscopy, Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs): Essential in Nigeria for detecting malaria parasites in blood films.
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis often points to a bacterial infection, whereas leukopenia or neutropenia may suggest viral illness or typhoid.
  • Blood and Urine Cultures: Crucial for isolating specific bacterial pathogens and performing sensitivity testing before starting broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Widal Test or Serology: Used alongside clinical findings for diagnosing typhoid and other endemic infections.

Nursing Interventions and Medical Management

Nursing care for a febrile patient focuses on comfort, safety, and supporting medical therapies prescribed by the physician.

Antipyretics such as paracetamol help reset hypothalamic control and alleviate discomfort, though they do not cure the underlying infection. Administer medications strictly according to calculated weight and dosage schedules. Encourage adequate oral hydration to replace fluid losses from sweating and increased metabolic rate, and monitor input and output.

Physical cooling measures, such as tepid sponging, are sometimes utilized, but aggressive ice-water immersion or alcohol rubs are avoided because they induce shivering, which paradoxically drives core temperature higher and causes vasoconstriction.

Potential Complications and Patient Education

Prolonged or very high fevers can lead to significant clinical complications if left unmanaged. Dehydration is common due to increased fluid evaporation and diaphoresis. In vulnerable pediatric populations, rapid temperature spikes can trigger febrile seizures, which require calm nursing management to maintain airway patency and protect the child from injury.

Discharge education must empower patients and their families. Teach caregivers how to correctly administer prescribed antipyretics, measure temperature accurately, and recognize warning signs that necessitate prompt return to the health facility, such as persistent vomiting, severe headache, neck stiffness, or confusion.
