Stress

Introduction to Stress and General Adaptation Syndrome

Stress is a physical and psychological response to internal or external demands that challenge a person's ability to cope. Hans Selye introduced the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) to explain how the body reacts to chronic or acute stress through three distinct stages:

  1. Alarm Reaction: The initial shock phase where the sympathetic nervous system activates, releasing adrenaline and noradrenaline to prepare the body for fight or flight.
  2. Stage of Resistance: The body attempts to adapt to the ongoing stressor, repairing damage while maintaining elevated hormone levels to cope with the continued demand.
  3. Stage of Exhaustion: Prolonged exposure depletes the body's energy and resources, leading to compromised immunity, tissue damage, and potential collapse if the stressor is not removed.

Pathophysiology and Neuroendocrine Response to Stress

When a stressor is perceived, the brain's amygdala signals the hypothalamus to trigger two primary neuroendocrine pathways. The first is the fast-acting sympathomedullary (SAM) pathway, which stimulates the adrenal medulla to secrete epinephrine and norepinephrine, raising heart rate, blood pressure, and glucose levels immediately.

The second is the slower hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), stimulating the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH then prompts the adrenal cortex to release cortisol, the primary stress hormone. Cortisol ensures sustained energy by mobilizing fats and amino acids, but prolonged elevation suppresses immune function and inflammatory responses.

Types and Sources of Stress in Clinical and Daily Life

Stress originates from various physical, psychological, and environmental sources. In clinical settings, patients face unique stressors such as loss of autonomy, pain, fear of the unknown, and financial strain from hospital bills.

  • Acute Stress: Short-term pressure resulting from immediate threats, such as a sudden trauma or a major examination.
  • Chronic Stress: Long-lasting pressure from persistent difficulties, such as poverty, abusive relationships, or chronic illness.
  • Eustress: Positive stress that motivates and focuses energy, such as starting a new job or planning a wedding.
  • Distress: Negative stress that causes anxiety, impairs performance, and leads to physical and mental breakdown.

Systemic Physiological and Psychological Effects of Chronic Stress

Prolonged exposure to stress hormones causes wear and tear on body tissues, a concept known as allostatic load.

Body SystemEffect of Chronic Stress
CardiovascularPersistent hypertension, increased risk of atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction
GastrointestinalExacerbation of peptic ulcers, irritable bowel syndrome, and changes in gut motility
ImmuneIncreased susceptibility to infections, delayed wound healing, and chronic low-grade inflammation
PsychologicalAnxiety, clinical depression, emotional exhaustion, and cognitive memory deficits

Nurses must recognize that these systemic changes delay recovery in medical-surgical patients and complicate existing chronic illnesses.

Nursing Assessment and Screening Tools for Stress Levels

Assessing stress requires both subjective data collection and objective observation. Nurses should evaluate the patient's verbal reports of anxiety, sleep disturbances, appetite changes, and feelings of overwhelm. Non-verbal cues such as restlessness, tremors, rapid speech, and facial tension provide valuable insight.

Standardized screening tools help quantify stress levels. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) measures how unpredictable and overloaded individuals find their lives. The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) helps evaluate the severity of somatic and psychological anxiety symptoms. Routine screening allows nurses to identify vulnerable patients early and incorporate targeted interventions into the nursing care plan.

Coping Mechanisms, Defense Mechanisms, and Adaptation

Coping refers to the cognitive and behavioral strategies people use to manage stress. Problem-focused coping targets the causes of stress directly by changing the situation, whereas emotion-focused coping regulates the emotional response to distress without altering the stressor itself.

When stress becomes overwhelming, the ego may employ unconscious defense mechanisms to protect against anxiety:

  • Denial: Refusing to acknowledge painful realities or facts.
  • Projection: Attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or impulses to another person.
  • Rationalization: Creating logical excuses for unacceptable behaviors or feelings.
  • Regression: Returning to an earlier, more childlike stage of development during stress.

Nursing Management and Stress Reduction Interventions

Nursing management of stress focuses on promoting relaxation, enhancing coping skills, and creating a therapeutic environment. Interventions can be categorized into physiological and psychological strategies.

  • Relaxation Techniques: Guide patients through deep breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, and guided imagery to lower heart rate and reduce cortisol.
  • Environmental Control: Minimize noise, dim lights at night, and organize care to allow uninterrupted periods of rest.
  • Therapeutic Communication: Provide active listening, validate the patient's feelings, and offer clear, honest information regarding their treatment plan to reduce uncertainty.
  • Lifestyle Guidance: Encourage physical activity, balanced nutrition, and adequate sleep hygiene to build resilience against future stressors.

Complications of Unmanaged Stress in Medical-Surgical Patients

Unmanaged stress directly impacts clinical outcomes in medical-surgical nursing. Prolonged stress delays wound healing due to cortisol-mediated suppression of collagen synthesis and inflammatory cell function. It also increases the risk of nosocomial infections because immune competence declines.

Furthermore, high stress elevates blood pressure and heart rate, increasing myocardial oxygen demand, which can trigger chest pain or myocardial infarction in vulnerable patients. Gastrointestinal complications, such as stress ulcers and paralytic ileus, occur due to impaired mucosal blood flow. Recognizing these risks allows nurses to prioritize stress management as an essential component of holistic patient care.
