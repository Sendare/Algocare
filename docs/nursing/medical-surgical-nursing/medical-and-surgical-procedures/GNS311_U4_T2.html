Suturing of Simple Lacerations

Introduction and Indications for Suturing Simple Lacerations

Suturing is a fundamental clinical skill used to close skin wounds, restore anatomical alignment, and promote primary intention healing. Not every skin tear requires closure with thread; superficial abrasions or clean wounds under minimal tension often heal well without intervention. The primary goals of suturing are to achieve adequate hemostasis, eliminate dead space where fluid can accumulate, and minimize scar formation.

Indications for wound closure depend heavily on timing and depth:

  • Fresh wounds: Lacerations presented within 6 to 12 hours of injury are prime candidates for primary closure.
  • Depth: Wounds extending into the subcutaneous tissue or causing gaping of the skin edges.
  • Cosmetic outcome: Lacerations across high-tension facial areas where scarring must be minimized.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Wound Evaluation

Before touching a wound with a needle, the nurse must perform a systematic evaluation. Check the patient's tetanus vaccination history; a booster is typically required if the last dose was over five years ago for dirty wounds, or ten years for clean minor cuts. Neurovascular assessment distal to the laceration is non-negotiable—test motor function, sensory perception, and capillary refill.

Inspect the wound bed thoroughly using adequate lighting to identify hidden foreign bodies like glass, gravel, or wood splinters. Probe gently with a sterile instrument if necessary. Document the exact length, depth, and any tendon or nerve involvement before administering any numbing medication.

Required Equipment, Instruments, and Local Anesthesia

Gathering a complete sterile tray before starting prevents breaks in sterile technique. A standard minor surgery pack includes needle holders, thumb forceps (with or without teeth), suture scissors, and a scalpel.

Local infiltration uses agents like lidocaine to block nerve conduction temporarily.

  • Plain Lidocaine: Provides rapid onset lasting around one to two hours.
  • Lidocaine with Epinephrine: Vasoconstricts blood vessels, reducing bleeding and prolonging the anesthetic effect. Never use epinephrine-containing solutions on end-artery structures like fingers, toes, the nose, or the penis, as the resulting vasoconstriction can cause tissue necrosis.

Wound Preparation: Cleansing and Aseptic Technique

Infection prevention starts long before the first stitch is placed. High-pressure irrigation is the single most effective step in reducing wound bacterial counts. Use normal saline delivered via a syringe and splash shield or an intravenous catheter to flush out microscopic debris. Avoid harsh antiseptics like full-strength povidone-iodine or hydrogen peroxide directly inside the open wound bed, as these chemicals damage healthy granulation tissue and delay healing.

Maintain a sterile field throughout the process. Scrub the surrounding skin in a circular motion moving outward from the wound edge.

Selection of Suture Materials, Needles, and Needle Holders

Choosing the right suture depends on the body site, tissue tension, and whether the material is absorbable or non-absorbable.

Needles come in different shapes and cross-sections. Cutting needles feature a sharp edge designed to pierce tough dermal tissue, while tapered needles glide through softer tissues like bowel or fascia without cutting them. Clamp the needle with the needle holder approximately one-third to one-half of the distance from the swaged end to the point.

Step-by-Step Technique for Simple Interrupted Sutures

The simple interrupted suture is the workhorse of wound closure because each stitch is tied and cut independently.

  1. Entry: Hold the needle holder perpendicular to the skin. Enter the skin at a 90-degree angle, rotating your wrist to follow the curve of the needle.
  2. Passage: Take an equal bite of tissue on both sides of the wound, ensuring the depth of the bite matches the width.
  3. Knot Tying: Use instrument ties, typically throwing a double wrap (surgeon's knot) followed by single locking throws to secure the knot off to one side of the wound line.
  4. Cutting: Leave the tail ends about 5 to 7 millimeters long so they do not poke adjacent skin or sink into the healing wound.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Patient Instructions

Clear home care instructions prevent unnecessary complications. Keep the sutured area dry for the first 24 to 48 hours, after which gentle washing with clean water and mild soap is encouraged to remove dried blood and crusts. Apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly or prescribed antibiotic ointment to keep the wound moist and prevent scabs from forming, which makes scar remodeling smoother.

Instruct the patient to avoid soaking the wound in baths or swimming pools until stitches are removed. Warn them to watch out for spreading redness, pus, or throbbing pain, and provide a clear date for follow-up review.

Suture Removal Guidelines and Timeline

Leaving sutures in too long causes permanent track marks ('train-track scars'), while removing them too early risks wound dehiscence. Timing depends directly on the blood supply and skin tension of the anatomical site:

  • Face: 3 to 5 days (due to rich blood supply and low tension)
  • Scalp and Arms: 7 to 10 days
  • Chest, Abdomen, and Legs: 10 to 14 days
  • Back and Joints: 14 days or longer

To remove, clean the site with antiseptic, gently lift the knot with forceps, slide the tip of the suture scissors under the thread close to the skin, and pull the thread out smoothly in one direction so exposed surface material does not drag through the wound channel.

Potential Complications and Recognition of Wound Infection

Monitoring for complications is essential during every post-procedure check. Wound infection is the most common adverse outcome, typically presenting 48 to 72 hours after injury. Classic signs include expanding erythema, localized warmth, purulent discharge, and systemic fever.

Other potential issues include wound dehiscence caused by premature suture removal or excessive physical tension, hematoma formation from inadequate hemostasis, and keloid or hypertrophic scarring. If signs of deep infection appear, prompt removal of select sutures and culture collection may be necessary.
