Suturing of Wounds in Emergencies

Introduction and Patient Assessment in Emergency Wounds

Emergency wound management begins the moment a patient arrives at the clinic or accident and emergency unit. The primary goal is to restore tissue integrity, minimize scarring, and prevent infection. Clinical evaluation must first rule out life-threatening injuries before focusing on the local wound.

During the initial assessment, the nurse or clinician must determine:

  • Mechanism of injury: Was it a clean knife cut, a blunt tear, or a dirty bite?
  • Time elapsed: Wounds older than six to twelve hours carry a significantly higher risk of primary closure infection.
  • Tetanus status: When was the patient's last tetanus toxoid booster?
  • Neurovascular status: Can the patient feel and move the body part distal to the injury?

Aseptic Technique and Local Anesthesia

A clean field is mandatory before any needle touches the skin. Nurses must establish a sterile working area, wear sterile gloves, and properly drape the wound perimeter with antiseptic solutions like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine, avoiding direct contact with the open wound bed where possible to prevent chemical irritation.

Local infiltration uses agents such as lignocaine (lidocaine) to block nerve conduction temporarily. Adding adrenaline (epinephrine) to the local anesthetic prolongs the duration of action and reduces bleeding by causing local vasoconstriction. However, adrenaline-containing solutions must never be used in end-arterial structures like fingers, toes, the nose, or the penis, as severe ischemia and tissue necrosis can result.

Wound Cleansing, Irrigation, and Debridement

Debris and bacteria remaining inside a wound will cause infection regardless of how neatly it is sutured. Irrigation is the cornerstone of cleansing. Using high-volume, low-pressure fluid streams—typically normal saline delivered via a syringe and angiocatheter—washes away loose particles without driving bacteria deeper into the tissue.

Debridement involves the surgical removal of devitalized, dead, or heavily contaminated tissue edges. A jagged, necrotic wound margin will not heal well and serves as a culture medium for pathogens. Sharp debridement creates fresh, bleeding borders that promote rapid cellular migration and proper re-epithelialization.

Selecting the right suture depends on the tissue layer. Absorbable sutures lose their tensile strength over weeks and are broken down by the body, making them ideal for subcutaneous closure. Non-absorbable sutures maintain tensile strength and must be manually removed after the skin has healed.

Needle geometry matters immensely. Cutting needles feature a sharp edge on the inner curve to slice through dense dermal tissue easily, whereas round-bodied taper needles part delicate tissues like bowel or muscle without cutting them.

Basic Suturing Techniques and Knot Tying

The simple interrupted suture is the workhorse of emergency medicine. Each stitch is placed, tied, and cut independently, which means if one suture fails or becomes infected, the rest of the wound remains secure.

  • Simple Interrupted: Versatile, easy to place, allows drainage between gaps.
  • Vertical Mattress: Excellent for everting wound edges and reducing dead space.
  • Continuous (Running): Fast to place, distributes tension evenly, but catastrophic if the single knot fails.

Knot tying secures the suture loop. A proper surgical knot requires an initial double throw followed by single locking throws in alternating directions to prevent slippage.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Complications

Once suturing is complete, the wound must be cleaned of dried blood and dressed with a sterile, non-adherent covering to protect it from external contamination. Clear patient instructions are vital for preventing secondary complications.

Patients must be advised to keep the wound dry for at least twenty-four to forty-eight hours, watch for redness, foul discharge, warmth, or spreading pain, and return for suture removal on schedule—typically five to seven days for the face and ten to fourteen days for the back or lower extremities.

Potential complications include wound dehiscence (re-opening), localized abscess formation, suture reaction, and permanent hypertrophic scarring.
