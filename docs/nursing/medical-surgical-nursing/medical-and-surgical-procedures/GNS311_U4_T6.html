Intravenous Infusion

Introduction and Purpose of Intravenous Infusion

Intravenous (IV) infusion is the direct administration of fluids, electrolytes, medications, or blood products into a patient's venous system. This route bypasses the digestive tract, ensuring immediate bioavailability and rapid therapeutic action. Nurses utilize IV therapy primarily for rapid fluid resuscitation, correcting electrolyte imbalances, delivering targeted pharmacological treatments, and providing total parenteral nutrition when the gastrointestinal tract is non-functional.

Anatomy and Selection of Peripheral Veins

Selecting an appropriate vein is vital for successful cannulation and minimizing patient discomfort. Nurses typically target the upper extremities, starting distally to preserve proximal sites for future use.

  • Metacarpal veins: Located on the back of the hand; easily visible but prone to movement and accidental dislodgement.
  • Cephalic vein: Runs up the lateral side of the arm; offers a straight, stable pathway suitable for larger catheters.
  • Basilic vein: Runs up the medial side of the arm; large and easy to cannulate, though close to nerves and brachial artery.
  • Median cubital vein: Located in the antecubital fossa; wide and anchored well, but flexion at the elbow can kink the cannula.

Types of Intravenous Solutions and Fluids

Intravenous fluids are categorized based on their tonicity relative to human blood plasma, which dictates how water shifts between compartments.

Fluid CategoryMechanism of ActionCommon Clinical Example
IsotonicStays in the intravascular space; expands extracellular volume without fluid shifts.0.9% Normal Saline, Ringer's Lactate
HypotonicShifts fluid out of blood vessels and into cells; hydrates intracellular compartments.0.45% Sodium Chloride
HypertonicDraws fluid out of cells and into the vascular space; reduces cellular swelling.3% Sodium Chloride, 10% Dextrose

Misconception: Nurses often assume 5% Dextrose in Water (D5W) remains isotonic once infused. Because the body rapidly metabolizes the dextrose, D5W quickly becomes hypotonic in the body.

Equipment Needed for IV Cannulation and Infusion

Gathering supplies before entering the patient's room ensures an efficient and sterile procedure. Missing a single item increases the risk of contamination or procedural failure.

  1. IV Cannula: Selected based on gauge size (e.g., 18G for rapid fluids/blood, 20G-22G for routine medications).
  2. Infusion Set: Administration tubing, either gravity drip or secondary tubing.
  3. IV Fluid Bag: The correct prescribed solution, checked for integrity and clarity.
  4. Skin Antiseptic: Chlorhexidine gluconate or 70% alcohol swab.
  5. Tourniquet: Used to engorge the peripheral veins.
  6. Transparent Dressings: Secures the site and allows continuous visual inspection.
  7. Personal Protective Equipment: Clean gloves.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

Performing venipuncture requires adherence to strict aseptic technique and patient communication. Explain the process to reduce anxiety.

  • Verify the medical order and patient identity using two identifiers.
  • Perform hand hygiene and put on non-sterile gloves.
  • Apply the tourniquet 3 to 4 inches above the chosen insertion site.
  • Cleanse the skin with an antiseptic using a friction motion and allow it to air dry completely.
  • Anchor the vein by pulling the skin taut distally.
  • Insert the needle bevel-up at a 15 to 30-degree angle until a flash of blood appears in the hub.
  • Advance the catheter slightly, withdraw the needle while pushing the catheter forward, release the tourniquet, and connect the infusion tubing.

Calculating and Regulating Infusion Flow Rates

Nurses must regulate intravenous drips accurately to prevent fluid overload or under-dosing. When gravity sets are used, calculate the flow rate using the drop factor printed on the tubing package.

$$\text{Flow Rate (drops/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time (minutes)}}$|

Standard drop factors include macro-drip sets (10, 15, or 20 drops/mL) and micro-drip sets (60 drops/mL). Always double-check calculations involving high-alert medications or pediatric patients.

Ongoing Nursing Monitoring and Maintenance

An active IV site requires vigilant, routine nursing assessments to catch complications early. Inspect the insertion site every shift for redness, swelling, tenderness, or leakage. Check that the infusion rate matches the medical prescription and that the tubing is free of kinks or air bubbles. Dressing changes must occur immediately if the tape becomes damp, loose, or visibly soiled. Flushing intermittent saline locks with normal saline maintains patency and prevents blood from clotting inside the catheter lumen.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Management

Intravenous therapy carries local and systemic risks that demand immediate nursing intervention.

  • Infiltration: Fluid leaks into surrounding tissues. Symptoms include swelling, coolness, and pallor. Stop infusion and remove catheter.
  • Extravasation: Infiltration involving a vesicant drug that causes tissue necrosis. Stop infusion, leave catheter to aspirate drug if possible, and notify prescriber.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall causing pain, warmth, and erythema along the vein path. Remove catheter and apply warm compresses.
  • Air Embolism: Air enters the circulatory system. Symptoms include sudden dyspnea and drop in blood pressure. Place patient in Trendelenburg position on the left side.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Infusion

When intravenous therapy is no longer required or if a complication develops, the nurse discontinues the infusion. Wash hands, put on gloves, and clamp the tubing. Gently peel away the dressing and tape while stabilizing the catheter. Place sterile gauze lightly over the insertion site without applying pressure while the cannula is pulled out parallel to the skin. Once removed, apply firm, continuous pressure with dry gauze for at least one to two minutes to achieve hemostasis, then secure with a clean bandage.
