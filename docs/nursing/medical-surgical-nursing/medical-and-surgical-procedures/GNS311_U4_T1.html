Review of Aseptic Technique

Introduction to Aseptic Technique

Aseptic technique refers to a set of practices designed to prevent the contamination of open wounds, sterile sites, or equipment by pathogenic microorganisms. Unlike sterile technique, which aims to eliminate all microorganisms completely, aseptic technique focuses on reducing the number of microbes to an absolute minimum to prevent infection. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, maintaining this standard is the primary line of defense against healthcare-associated infections. It involves a combination of hand hygiene, barrier techniques, and meticulous environmental control during clinical procedures.

Medical Asepsis Versus Surgical Asepsis

  • Medical Asepsis (Clean Technique): Reduces the number and transfer of pathogens. Used for routine daily care such as administering oral medications, feeding, and general ward nursing.
  • Surgical Asepsis (Sterile Technique): Eliminates all microorganisms from an object or area. Required for invasive procedures like catheter insertion, major surgery, and dressing changes on deep wounds.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is universally recognized as the single most effective measure to interrupt the chain of infection. Nurses must perform hand hygiene using soap and water when hands are visibly soiled, or an alcohol-based hand rub if hands are clean. Standard precautions dictate that blood, body fluids, secretions, and non-intact skin must be treated as potentially infectious for every patient, regardless of their diagnosed condition.

Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment creates a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and potential pathogens. The correct sequence for putting on (donning) PPE is gown, mask, goggles or face shield, and finally gloves. When removing (doffing) PPE, gloves are removed first because they are the most contaminated, followed by eyewear, gown, and mask. Gloves must never replace hand hygiene; hands are cleaned immediately after glove removal.

Establishing and Maintaining a Sterile Field

A sterile field is an area free of all microorganisms that serves as a safe workstation during invasive procedures. When opening sterile packages, the outermost flap is opened away from the body first to avoid reaching over the sterile contents. The outer one-inch border of a sterile drape is considered contaminated. Any sterile item that falls below waist level, touches a non-sterile surface, or gets wet is immediately discarded as contaminated.

Clinical Procedures and Preventing Infections

Strict adherence to aseptic technique directly reduces the incidence of healthcare-associated infections like surgical site infections and catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Nurses must monitor for clinical signs of infection such as localized redness, swelling, increased warmth, purulent discharge, and systemic fever. Prompt documentation and reporting of these findings ensure timely medical intervention and safeguard patient recovery.
