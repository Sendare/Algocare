Leprosy

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Leprosy

Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. This acid-fast bacillus primarily targets the peripheral nerves and the skin, but it can also affect the upper respiratory tract mucosa, eyes, and testes. Transmission occurs mainly through droplets from the nose and mouth during close and frequent contact with untreated cases.

Unlike many other bacteria, *Mycobacterium leprae* multiplies very slowly, leading to an incubation period that typically lasts between three to five years, though symptoms can take up to twenty years to appear. The organism thrives in cooler areas of the body, which explains why skin and peripheral nerves are heavily involved. Cellular immunity determines how the infection manifests in the human host, dividing the disease spectrum into distinct clinical forms based on the patient's immune response.

Classification and Clinical Manifestations

Clinical classification of leprosy relies on the Ridley-Jopling system, which guides treatment protocols. Operationally, the World Health Organization classifies the disease into paucibacillary and multibacillary forms based on skin lesion counts.

  • Paucibacillary (PB): Patients present with one to five skin lesions and negative skin smears. Lesions are typically hypopigmented with complete loss of sensation.
  • Multibacillary (MB): Patients have six or more skin lesions, widespread nerve involvement, or positive skin smears. Lesions can be macules, papules, or nodular infiltrations known as lepromas.

Nerve damage leads to hallmark signs such as anesthetic patches, muscle weakness, and thickened peripheral nerves, particularly the ulnar, median, and common peroneal nerves.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Assessment

Diagnosing leprosy depends heavily on thorough physical and neurological examinations. Healthcare workers assess skin lesions for sensory loss using light touch, temperature discrimination, and pinprick tests. Palpation of peripheral nerve trunks for enlargement and tenderness is a routine part of the assessment.

Confirmatory testing involves slit-skin smears taken from earlobes and skin lesions, stained with the Ziehl-Neelsen technique to visualize acid-fast bacilli. Skin biopsies may also be performed for histopathological examination. A definitive diagnosis requires at least one of the following cardinal signs: hypopigmented or reddish skin patches with definite sensory loss, thickened peripheral nerves, or the presence of acid-fast bacilli on a skin smear.

Pharmacological Management and Multidrug Therapy

Monotherapy for leprosy leads rapidly to drug resistance, which prompted the introduction of Multidrug Therapy (MDT) by the World Health Organization. MDT combines two or more bactericidal and bacteriostatic medications to clear the infection and prevent resistance.

  • Rifampicin: A potent bactericidal agent given monthly under supervision.
  • Dapsone: A daily bacteriostatic sulfone medication.
  • Clofazimine: A daily and monthly medication that also possesses anti-inflammatory properties.

Treatment duration varies by classification. Paucibacillary cases typically require a 6-month course of Rifampicin and Dapsone. Multibacillary cases require a 12-month course of Rifampicin, Dapsone, and Clofazimine. Strict adherence is vital to achieve a permanent cure and stop transmission.

Complications and Reactional States

Patients with leprosy can experience acute inflammatory episodes known as reactional states, which threaten nerve function and tissue integrity. These reactions can occur before, during, or after completing multidrug therapy.

  • Type 1 (Reversal) Reactions: Delayed hypersensitivity responses causing sudden inflammation of existing skin lesions and acute neuritis. Nerves become swollen and painful, risking permanent paralysis if untreated.
  • Type 2 (Erythema Nodosum Leprosum) Reactions: Immune-complex mediated reactions characterized by painful subcutaneous nodules, fever, iritis, and orchitis, seen mainly in multibacillary patients.

Management of these reactions often requires high-dose corticosteroids like prednisolone to reduce nerve inflammation and prevent irreversible disability.

Nursing Care and Prevention of Disabilities

Nursing care for leprosy focuses heavily on protecting anesthetic extremities and preventing secondary injuries. Because patients cannot feel pain, pressure, or heat, they are prone to burns, cuts, and pressure ulcers, particularly on the hands and feet.

Nurses teach self-care practices, including daily inspection of hands and feet for injuries, soaking and oiling dry skin to prevent cracking, and wearing protective footwear. Wound care must be meticulous to prevent infection. Assessment of handgrip and footdrop helps monitor nerve function over time. Educating the patient on how to modify daily activities protects vulnerable limbs from repetitive trauma.

Patient Education, Rehabilitation, and Stigma Reduction

Social stigma remains one of the greatest barriers to early leprosy detection and treatment completion. Misconceptions about the disease cause patients to hide symptoms, delaying care until disabilities occur. Comprehensive nursing care involves counseling patients and their families to correct these myths, emphasizing that leprosy is fully curable and that MDT renders patients non-infectious quickly.

Rehabilitation encompasses physical therapy for muscle weakness, reconstructive surgery for deformities, and livelihood support to reintegrate affected individuals into society. Empowering communities through health education fosters a supportive environment that encourages early reporting and adherence.
