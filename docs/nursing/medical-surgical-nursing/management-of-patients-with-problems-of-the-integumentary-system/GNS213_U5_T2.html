Assessment of Clients with Problems of the Skin Hair and Nails

Introduction to Integumentary Assessment in Nursing

The integumentary system is the largest organ system in the human body, serving as the primary barrier against environmental pathogens, mechanical trauma, and fluid loss. For nursing students in Nigeria, mastering integumentary assessment is essential because skin changes frequently reflect underlying systemic illnesses such as severe malaria, anemia, malnutrition, or endocrine disorders. Assessment requires a holistic approach that integrates physical inspection with a thorough understanding of cultural skin variations and environmental exposures common in tropical regions.

Health History and Biographical Data

Gathering a detailed client history helps uncover the root cause of dermatological complaints. The nurse must inquire about the onset, duration, and progression of any rash, lesion, or pruritus. Key areas to explore include:

  • Occupational history: Exposure to harsh chemicals, prolonged sun, or agricultural pesticides.
  • Family history: Genetic predispositions to eczema, psoriasis, or skin malignancies.
  • Medication review: Recent use of new drugs, herbal concoctions, or bleaching creams that frequently precipitate adverse cutaneous reactions.
  • Travel history: Movement to regions endemic for specific fungal or parasitic skin infections.

Preparation and Equipment for Physical Examination

A successful physical examination of the skin, hair, and nails requires a well-lit room, preferably with natural sunlight, to accurately observe skin color and undertones. Privacy and comfort must be maintained by draping unexamined body parts.

Essential tools include:

  • Penlight: For illuminating cavities, mucosal membranes, and assessing depth of lesions.
  • Magnifying glass: To inspect fine details of primary lesions or parasite burrows.
  • Ruler or tape measure: To accurately record the size of lesions in centimeters.
  • Gloves: To maintain universal precautions when touching open wounds or infectious skin conditions.

Physical Assessment: Inspection and Palpation

Physical assessment relies heavily on inspection and palpation. The nurse inspects the entire integument systematically from head to toe, noting general pigmentation, vascularity, and lesions.

Palpation evaluates texture, moisture, temperature, and skin turgor. To test skin turgor, gently pinch a fold of skin over the clavicle or forearm and release it; prompt recoil indicates adequate hydration, whereas tenting signals fluid volume deficit. Hair is assessed for distribution, texture, and hygiene, while nails are inspected for shape, angle, and capillary refill.

Assessing Primary and Secondary Skin Lesions

Skin lesions are categorized into primary lesions, which develop on previously unaltered skin, and secondary lesions, which result from changes to primary lesions due to scratching, trauma, or healing.

Diagnostic Tests and Laboratory Investigations

When physical assessment yields inconclusive findings, diagnostic tests provide objective confirmation. A common bedside procedure is the wood's light examination, which uses ultraviolet light to detect fungal or bacterial fluorescence. For suspected fungal infections, a skin scraping is mounted on a microscope slide with potassium hydroxide (KOH).

Skin biopsies—punch, shave, or excisional—are performed for definitive histological analysis of persistent or suspicious lesions. Blood cultures and complete blood counts are ordered when systemic infection or severe inflammatory skin disease is suspected.

Documentation and Reporting Findings

Accurate documentation is critical for tracking the progression of dermatological conditions and ensuring continuity of care. The nurse must record the precise anatomical location, size in centimeters, color, elevation, texture, and any associated exudate or odor of lesions. Vague descriptions such as 'bad rash on leg' are unacceptable. Instead, notes should be clear, objective, and professional, using standard medical terminology to facilitate effective interprofessional communication.
