Skin Snip

Introduction and Clinical Indications

The skin snip procedure is a minor diagnostic surgical technique primarily used to detect microfilariae in the skin. Its main application in tropical nursing and medicine is the diagnosis of onchocerciasis, commonly known as river blindness, caused by the parasitic nematode *Onchocerca volvulus*. Adult worms live in subcutaneous nodules, producing microfilariae that migrate into the dermal layers where the skin snip retrieves them.

Key clinical indications for performing a skin snip include:

  • Suspected Onchocerciasis: Patients presenting with chronic pruritus, skin depigmentation, or leopard skin changes in endemic areas.
  • Subcutaneous Nodules: Evaluating firm, non-tender lumps where adult worms may congregate, often called onchocercomas.
  • Ocular Symptoms: Assessing patients with visual impairment or keratitis living in high-risk transmission zones near fast-flowing rivers.
  • Epidemiological Surveys: Screening populations in endemic rural communities to monitor mass drug administration programs.

Equipment Preparation and Sterile Technique

Performing a skin snip requires careful assembly of sterile instruments and adherence to strict infection control protocols. Because the procedure involves breaching the epidermis, preventing secondary bacterial contamination is a primary nursing responsibility.

Essential equipment includes:

  • Sterile scalpel blade (typically a size 11 or 15) or a specialized corneo-scleral punch.
  • Surgical forceps or needle holders to gently grasp a tiny cone of skin.
  • Antiseptic solution such as chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine for site cleansing.
  • Normal saline and microscopic slides or a multi-well plate containing distilled water or saline.
  • Personal protective equipment including sterile gloves, gowns, and eye protection due to exposure risk to body fluids.

Step-by-Step Procedure Technique

The execution of a skin snip demands precision to obtain an adequate dermal sample while minimizing patient discomfort and deep bleeding. Unlike a full-thickness biopsy, the snip should strictly involve the epidermis and upper dermis.

  1. Site Selection: Choose a clinically appropriate site, commonly the iliac crest, scapula, or outer calf, depending on regional diagnostic guidelines.
  2. Skin Preparation: Cleanse the chosen area thoroughly with an antiseptic agent and allow it to dry completely.
  3. Elevation: Use sterile forceps to gently lift a small cone of skin, avoiding deep subcutaneous tissue to prevent heavy bleeding.
  4. Excision: Use a sterile scalpel blade to slice off the elevated skin papule, ensuring the sample is roughly 1 to 2 millimeters in diameter.
  5. Hemostasis: Apply direct pressure with sterile gauze to control any minor capillary bleeding at the harvest site.

Specimen Handling and Laboratory Processing

Once the tissue sample is excised, proper handling is vital to encourage the microfilariae to emerge from the skin matrix into the liquid medium for accurate identification.

Processing StepAction RequiredClinical Rationale
TransferPlace tissue into saline or waterPrevents the tissue specimen from drying out
IncubationLeave specimen undisturbedAllows microfilariae time to migrate out of the dermis
ExaminationView under a light microscopeVisualizes actively swimming larvae for confirmation

If no larvae emerge immediately, plates are often incubated for 24 hours to check for delayed migration before a negative result is officially recorded.

Post-Procedure Care and Potential Complications

Although a skin snip is minimally invasive, nursing vigilance is required to manage the tiny wound and monitor for potential adverse events. Patients should be instructed on how to keep the site clean and dry to promote rapid healing.

  • Bleeding Control: Ensure hemostasis is complete before discharging the patient; apply a small sterile adhesive bandage.
  • Infection Surveillance: Teach the patient to watch for signs of local infection such as increasing redness, warmth, or purulent discharge.
  • Mazzotti Reaction Awareness: If the patient is concurrently treated with antifilarial drugs like ivermectin, monitor for systemic allergic reactions caused by dying microfilariae, featuring fever, rash, and hypotension.
