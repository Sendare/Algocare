Disorders of the Small Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Small Intestine

The small intestine spans roughly six meters and forms the longest section of the gastrointestinal tract. It divides structurally into three continuous segments:

  • Duodenum: Receives chyme from the stomach along with bile and pancreatic secretions to neutralize acidity and begin enzymatic breakdown.
  • Jejunum: Features prominent circular folds called plicae circulares designed for maximum absorption of nutrients.
  • Ileum: Contains Peyer's patches for immune defense and absorbs remaining vitamin B12 and bile salts.

Microscopic projections called villi and microvilli dramatically increase the mucosal surface area, creating the brush border where final digestion occurs. Active transport and passive diffusion move digested carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals directly into the blood and lymphatic capillaries.

Inflammatory and Infectious Conditions

Inflammatory disorders of the small bowel disrupt normal mucosal integrity, impairing both digestion and fluid balance. Crohn's disease is a chronic transmural inflammatory condition that can affect any part of the GI tract from mouth to anus, though the terminal ileum is a frequent site. It presents with skip lesions, cobblestone mucosal appearance, and deep ulcerations that risk stricture formation and fistulae.

Infectious enteritis results from bacterial, viral, or parasitic invasion. Organisms like *Salmonella*, *Giardia lamblia*, or rotavirus damage enterocytes, leading to acute secretory or osmotic diarrhea. Clinical management prioritizes fluid resuscitation, electrolyte replacement, and identifying the causative agent through stool microscopy or culture before administering targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Intestinal Obstruction

An intestinal obstruction occurs when normal forward propulsion of bowel contents is blocked. Obstructions are broadly categorized as mechanical or functional:

  • Mechanical obstruction: Physical blockage inside the lumen or outside the wall, such as adhesions from prior surgeries, hernias, tumors, or intussusception.
  • Functional obstruction (ileus): Impairment of peristalsis without physical blockage, commonly seen following abdominal surgery, severe electrolyte imbalance, or peritonitis.

Early signs include high-pitched tinkling bowel sounds above the obstruction, colicky abdominal pain, and nausea with bilious vomiting. As the condition worsens, bowel sounds disappear, the abdomen becomes distended, and third-space fluid shifts can precipitate hypovolemic shock and bowel ischemia.

Malabsorption Syndromes

Malabsorption syndromes occur when the small intestine fails to adequately absorb nutrients, vitamins, and minerals. Celiac disease is an immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten ingestion in genetically susceptible individuals. Exposure causes flattening of the mucosal villi, leading to chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea, and weight loss.

Short bowel syndrome develops following massive surgical resection of the small intestine due to trauma, infarction, or Crohn's disease. The remaining bowel cannot absorb sufficient nutrients and water, requiring specialized long-term nutritional support like total parenteral nutrition (TPN) alongside gradual enteral adaptation therapy.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Thorough assessment of a patient with suspected small intestinal disorders requires detailed health history focusing on abdominal pain patterns, stool characteristics, and weight changes. Physical examination centers on inspection for distension, auscultation for bowel sound changes, and gentle palpation to detect tenderness or masses.

Diagnostic investigations confirm clinical suspicions:

  • Abdominal X-ray: Detects free air, dilated bowel loops, or air-fluid levels indicative of obstruction or perforation.
  • Endoscopy / Colonoscopy: Direct visualization of the mucosa with biopsy capability to diagnose celiac disease or Crohn's.
  • CT Enterography: Specialized cross-sectional imaging that highlights bowel wall thickening, inflammation, and strictures.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies for small intestinal disorders aim to relieve symptoms, correct nutritional deficits, and resolve structural complications. Medical therapy often includes anti-inflammatory agents, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and targeted fluid and electrolyte replacement. Bowel rest via temporary nil per os (NPO) status and nasogastric tube decompression is standard for acute obstructions.

Surgical intervention becomes necessary when medical management fails or life-threatening complications arise. Procedures range from strictureplasty and bowel resection with anastomoses to lysis of adhesions. Nurses play a crucial role in monitoring post-operative patients for signs of anastomotic leak, hemorrhage, and return of normal bowel motility.

Nursing Care Plan, Complications, and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for small intestinal disorders focuses on stabilizing physiological status and preventing life-threatening complications such as bowel perforation, peritonitis, and septic shock. Nurses monitor vital signs closely for fever, tachycardia, and hypotension, which signal worsening sepsis or internal bleeding.

Patient education is tailored to the specific diagnosis. For celiac disease, teaching emphasizes strict, lifelong adherence to a gluten-free diet. For inflammatory bowel conditions, education covers recognizing flare-up triggers, medication compliance, and identifying signs of obstruction. Proper wound care and ostomy education are provided when surgical diversion is performed.
