Disorders of the Large Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Large Intestine

The large intestine, spanning roughly 1.5 meters in adults, comprises the cecum, appendix, colon, rectum, and anal canal. Unlike the small intestine, the mucosal lining of the colon lacks villi. Instead, it features numerous crypts of Lieberkühn that secrete mucus to lubricate hard stool and protect the epithelial wall from abrasive friction.

The primary physiological roles include:

  • Water and electrolyte absorption: Reabsorbing sodium and water from the remaining indigestible food residue, converting liquid chyme into formed stool.
  • Vitamin synthesis: Resident gut flora ferment dietary fiber, producing vitamin K and certain B-complex vitamins.
  • Storage and elimination: Storing fecal matter in the sigmoid colon and rectum until mass peristalsis triggers defecation.

Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Evaluating large intestine disorders requires a structured nursing assessment alongside diagnostic imaging and endoscopic visualization. Nurses must thoroughly investigate abdominal pain, changes in bowel habits, rectal bleeding, tenesmus, and unexplained weight loss.

Key diagnostic tools include:

  • Colonoscopy: The gold standard for directly visualizing the entire colonic mucosa, allowing simultaneous biopsy and polypectomy.
  • Sigmoidoscopy: Examines only the rectum and sigmoid colon, requiring less bowel preparation.
  • Barium Enema: Uses radiopaque contrast to highlight colonic strictures, fistulas, or obstructions via X-ray.
  • Stool Tests: Fecal occult blood tests (FOBT) and stool cultures identify microscopic bleeding, ova, parasites, or bacterial pathogens.

Inflammatory Bowel Diseases: Crohn's and Ulcerative Colitis

Diverticular Disease and Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis refers to the presence of multiple small outpouchings (diverticula) in the colonic wall, typically formed at weak points where blood vessels penetrate the circular muscle layer. A low-fiber diet increases intraluminal pressure, promoting diverticula formation, most commonly in the sigmoid colon.

Diverticulitis develops when a diverticulum becomes obstructed by fecalith, leading to inflammation, microperforation, and localized infection. Patients present with left lower quadrant abdominal pain, low-grade fever, nausea, and leukocytosis. Uncomplicated cases are managed with outpatient clear liquid diets and broad-spectrum antibiotics, whereas complicated cases involving abscess, perforation, or obstruction necessitate hospitalization, bowel rest, and possible surgical resection.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome and Functional Disorders

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) is a common functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by chronic abdominal pain and altered bowel habits without structural or biochemical abnormalities. Diagnosis relies on the Rome IV criteria, which require recurrent abdominal pain at least one day per week in the last three months, associated with two or more of the following: related to defecation, associated with a change in stool frequency, or associated with a change in stool form.

IBS is categorized into subtypes: IBS-C (predominant constipation), IBS-D (predominant diarrhea), and IBS-M (mixed). Management centers on lifestyle adjustments, stress reduction, dietary modifications like a low-FODMAP diet, and symptom-specific pharmacotherapy such as laxatives or antidiarrheals.

Colorectal Polyps and Colorectal Cancer

Colorectal cancer is a major global health concern, frequently developing via the adenoma-carcinoma sequence where benign mucosal growths (polyps) undergo malignant transformation over several years. Polyps are classified histologically as hyperplastic (generally benign) or adenomatous (precancerous).

Risk factors include advanced age, family history, inflammatory bowel disease, smoking, physical inactivity, and diets high in red or processed meats. Screening via colonoscopy allows early detection and removal of adenomatous polyps, halting cancer progression. Clinical manifestations of established cancer include rectal bleeding, unexplained iron deficiency anemia, changes in stool caliber, and abdominal cramping.

Intestinal Obstruction of the Large Bowel

Large bowel obstruction (LBO) occurs when the passage of intestinal contents is mechanically blocked. Common causes include colorectal cancer, volvulus (twisting of the bowel), and severe diverticular strictures. Unlike small bowel obstructions, LBOs often have a more insidious onset because the colon can compensate by absorbing fluid and distending.

Key clinical features include progressive abdominal distension, absolute constipation, cramping lower abdominal pain, and feculent vomiting in late stages. A closed-loop obstruction, where both ends of a bowel loop are sealed, poses an immediate risk of ischemia, gangrene, and perforation due to mounting intraluminal pressure.

Nursing Management and Patient Care

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with large intestine disorders focuses on symptom management, hemodynamic stabilization, and psychological support. Nurses monitor vital signs for signs of peritonitis or septic shock, maintain strict fluid balance records, and administer prescribed analgesics and anti-emetics.

For patients undergoing bowel surgery or ostomy creation, preoperative education and stoma site marking are essential. Postoperatively, nurses assess stoma viability—looking for a pink, moist appearance—and monitor output for volume and consistency while teaching patients and caregivers self-management techniques.
