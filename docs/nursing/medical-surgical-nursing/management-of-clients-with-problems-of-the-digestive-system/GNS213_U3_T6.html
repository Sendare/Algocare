Disorders of the Stomach

Anatomy and Physiology of the Stomach

The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped organ situated in the upper left quadrant of the abdomen, acting as a temporary reservoir for ingested food. Anatomically, it is divided into four main regions: the cardia, fundus, body, and pylorus. The gastric wall features specialized cells within its mucosal lining that secrete gastric juice, which is vital for digestion.

  • Mucous neck cells: Produce alkaline mucus that protects the gastric epithelium from self-digestion by acid.
  • Parietal cells: Secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl), lowering gastric pH to activate digestive enzymes, and intrinsic factor, which is essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum.
  • Chief cells: Release pepsinogen, the inactive precursor to pepsin, which initiates protein digestion.
  • G cells: Secrete the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate acid secretion and gastric motility.

Gastritis: Acute and Chronic Forms

Gastritis is an inflammation of the gastric mucosa, categorized primarily as acute or chronic based on the duration and onset of mucosal injury. Acute gastritis often results from sudden mucosal irritants such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), heavy alcohol consumption, or acute stress. Chronic gastritis persists over a longer period and is commonly linked to Helicobacter pylori infection or autoimmune reactions attacking parietal cells.

The pathophysiology involves a breakdown of the normal gastric mucosal barrier. When this protective layer is compromised, endogenous acid and pepsin diffuse back into the tissue, causing cellular damage, edema, hemorrhage, and local inflammatory responses. While acute forms may heal rapidly once the trigger is removed, chronic gastritis can lead to gastric atrophy and pernicious anemia over time.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD) involves erosive lesions in the mucosal membrane of the gastrointestinal tract exposed to gastric acid and pepsin, most commonly found in the proximal duodenum or the stomach. The two primary etiologies are chronic H. pylori infection and the regular use of NSAIDs, both of which impair mucosal defense mechanisms. Patients typically present with a burning epigastric pain that often correlates with meal timing: gastric ulcers may worsen shortly after eating, whereas duodenal ulcer pain frequently improves with food intake and worsens when the stomach is empty.

Complications of PUD require vigilant nursing observation. Hemorrhage occurs when an ulcer erodes through a blood vessel, leading to hematemesis or melena. Perforation happens when the ulcer creates a full-thickness hole in the stomach or duodenal wall, spilling gastrointestinal contents into the peritoneal cavity and causing sudden, severe abdominal rigidity. Obstruction can also develop due to edema or scar tissue at the pylorus.

Gastric Cancer and Neoplasms

Gastric cancer is a malignant neoplasm arising most frequently from the glandular epithelium of the stomach mucosa, with adenocarcinoma accounting for the vast majority of cases. Risk factors include persistent H. pylori infection, diets high in salted or smoked foods, smoking, and a history of gastric polyps or pernicious anemia. Early-stage gastric cancer is often asymptomatic, which frequently delays diagnosis until the disease has advanced.

As the tumor grows, clinical manifestations emerge, including unexplained weight loss, early satiety, persistent abdominal discomfort, and iron-deficiency anemia due to chronic occult blood loss. Metastasis can occur via local extension, lymphatic spread, or hematogenous routes to the liver and peritoneum. Management relies heavily on surgical resection when caught early, supplemented by chemotherapy and radiation therapy.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Comprehensive assessment of a patient with gastric disorders begins with a detailed health history focusing on pain characteristics, dietary habits, medication use, particularly NSAIDs, and a history of H. pylori infection. Physical examination may reveal epigastric tenderness, abdominal distension, or signs of acute peritonitis if a complication has developed.

Diagnostic modalities confirm underlying structural or mucosal pathology:

  • Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD): The gold standard for visualizing the gastric mucosa directly and obtaining tissue biopsies for histology and rapid urease testing.
  • H. pylori testing: Includes non-invasive stool antigen tests, urea breath tests, and serology, alongside biopsy-based methods during EGD.
  • Barium swallow: Radiographic imaging that outlines the stomach contour, useful for identifying strictures or large ulcers when endoscopy is unavailable.
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC): Assesses for anemia resulting from chronic gastrointestinal bleeding or vitamin B12 malabsorption.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management of gastric disorders aims to relieve symptoms, promote mucosal healing, eradicate infectious agents, and prevent recurrences. Pharmacotherapy forms the cornerstone of treatment for gastritis and peptic ulcer disease.

  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Drugs like omeprazole block the final step of acid production, providing rapid relief and high healing rates.
  • H2-Receptor Antagonists: Agents such as famotidine reduce gastric acid secretion by blocking histamine receptors on parietal cells.
  • Antacids: Provide rapid, temporary neutralization of existing gastric acid.
  • Antibiotics: A multi-drug regimen combining antibiotics like clarithromycin, amoxicillin, or metronidazole with a PPI is used to eradicate H. pylori.
  • Cytoprotective agents: Medications like sucralfate form a protective barrier over the ulcer crater, shielding it from acid and pepsin.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgical intervention becomes necessary when gastric disorders result in severe complications such as intractable bleeding, perforation, obstruction, or malignant neoplasms. Common procedures include partial gastrectomy, vagotomy to reduce acid secretion by cutting the vagus nerve, and pyloroplasty to widen the pyloric sphincter.

Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring vital signs, managing surgical pain, maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, and carefully managing nasogastric (NG) tube drainage. Nurses must keep the NG tube patent and avoid repositioning or irrigating it without a specific medical order after gastric surgery, as this could disrupt delicate suture lines. Early ambulation and respiratory exercises prevent postoperative pulmonary complications and deep vein thrombosis.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

The nursing care plan for patients with gastric disorders addresses priority problems such as acute pain, risk for fluid volume deficit, imbalanced nutrition, and anxiety. Interventions include monitoring vital signs for signs of hemorrhage, administering prescribed medications, and providing tailored dietary guidance.

Patient education must emphasize lifestyle modifications to prevent symptom flare-ups and promote healing. Nurses should instruct patients to avoid known irritants such as NSAIDs, alcohol, caffeine, and spicy foods. In cases involving total or partial gastrectomy, education must include instructions on managing dumping syndrome—characterized by rapid gastric emptying leading to dizziness, sweating, and palpitations—by eating small, frequent meals low in simple sugars and separating liquids from solid food intake.
