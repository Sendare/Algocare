Disorders of the Mouth

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oral Cavity

The oral cavity serves as the entry point to the digestive and respiratory systems. It is lined with stratified squamous epithelium that protects underlying tissues from mechanical and chemical trauma during mastication. Salivary glands, comprising the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands, produce saliva containing amylase and lipase to begin carbohydrate and lipid digestion while maintaining oral mucosal moisture. The tongue, a muscular organ anchored to the floor of the mouth, manipulates food boluses and houses taste buds. Proper functioning of these structures relies on intact cranial nerves, particularly the trigeminal and facial nerves, which govern sensation and motor movement.

Common Infectious and Inflammatory Conditions

  • Candidiasis: A fungal infection caused by Candida albicans, presenting as white plaques on the oral mucosa that can be scraped off, frequently seen in immunocompromised patients.
  • Herpes Labialis: Caused by Herpes Simplex Virus type 1, characterized by painful vesicular eruptions on the lips and perioral skin.
  • Aphthous Ulcers: Commonly known as Canker sores, these are painful, non-contagious shallow ulcers inside the mouth with a yellowish base and red border.
  • Gingivitis: Early-stage periodontal inflammation marked by swollen, bleeding gums without underlying bone loss, usually resulting from poor oral hygiene.

Premalignant Lesions and Oral Cancers

Oral malignancies primarily manifest as squamous cell carcinomas arising from the mucosal epithelium. Long-term exposure to tobacco, excessive alcohol consumption, and chronic irritation significantly increase malignant transformation. Premalignant lesions serve as important clinical warnings:

  • Leukoplakia: Adherent white patches on the mucous membranes that cannot be scraped off and carry a moderate risk of malignant conversion.
  • Erythroplakia: Red, velvety patches that have a significantly higher risk of progressing to squamous cell carcinoma than white lesions.

Early detection through routine inspection of the lateral borders of the tongue and floor of the mouth is vital for improving survival rates.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A systematic oral assessment involves inspecting the lips, buccal mucosa, gingiva, palate, tongue, and oropharynx using adequate lighting and a tongue depressor. Nurses must evaluate mucosal integrity, hydration status, color, and any presence of lesions, exudate, or bleeding. Diagnostic evaluations often include tissue biopsies for suspicious persistent lesions, microbial cultures for persistent infections, and imaging studies such as panoramic radiography or CT scans to stage oral cancers or assess jaw involvement. Pain assessment remains central, as oral lesions frequently interfere with speaking, swallowing, and nutritional intake.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological interventions depend directly on the underlying etiology of the oral disorder. Antifungal agents such as nystatin suspension or clotrimazole troches are prescribed for oral candidiasis. Topical corticosteroids or local anesthetics like viscous lidocaine help manage the discomfort of aphthous ulcers or localized stomatitis. Antibiotic therapy is reserved for bacterial infections or severe periodontal disease. Systemic analgesics address pain, while specialized mouthwashes containing chlorhexidine reduce bacterial load and promote healing after minor oral procedures.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgical management ranges from simple excision of benign lesions and biopsies to extensive resections for oral cancers, which may involve partial glossectomy, mandibulectomy, or neck dissection. Following extensive oral surgery, maintaining a patent airway is the absolute priority due to postoperative edema, bleeding, and altered oral anatomy. Nurses must monitor for hemorrhage, manage pain effectively, and ensure proper nutritional support, often transitioning from parenteral fluids to enteral feeding via nasogastric or gastrostomy tubes as healing progresses.

Oral Hygiene, Nutrition, and Patient Education

Prevention and management of oral disorders heavily depend on consistent self-care practices. Nurses must educate patients on proper tooth brushing techniques, daily flossing, and the use of alcohol-free mouth rinses to prevent plaque accumulation and gingivitis. Nutritional counseling is essential for patients experiencing oral pain or dysphagia; soft, non-citric, lukewarm foods prevent further irritation of delicate mucosal surfaces. Furthermore, cessation counseling regarding tobacco and alcohol use remains a critical intervention to reduce the incidence of premalignant and malignant oral lesions.
