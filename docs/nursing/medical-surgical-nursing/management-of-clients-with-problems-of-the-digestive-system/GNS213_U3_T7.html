Disorders of the Duodenum

Anatomy and Physiology of the Duodenum

The duodenum forms the first and shortest segment of the small intestine, measuring roughly twenty-five centimeters in length. Shaped like a C, it curves around the head of the pancreas and receives vital secretions necessary for digestion. Structurally, it transitions the acidic chyme coming from the stomach into a more alkaline environment suitable for intestinal enzymes.

  • Superior part: The initial segment, often called the duodenal bulb, is the common site for peptic ulcers.
  • Descending part: Contains the major duodenal papilla where bile from the liver and digestive juices from the pancreas empty into the lumen.
  • Inferior part: Crosses horizontally over the major blood vessels supplying the lower abdomen.
  • Ascending part: Rises upward to join the jejunum at the duodenojejunal flexure, supported by the suspensory muscle of the duodenum.

Endocrine and exocrine functions occur here continuously. Specialized glands in the submucosa, known as Brunner's glands, secrete alkaline mucus to protect the duodenal lining from gastric acid damage.

Peptic Ulcer Disease and Duodenal Ulcers

Duodenal ulcers represent a major subset of peptic ulcer disease, characterized by breaks in the mucosal lining that extend through the muscularis mucosa. Unlike gastric ulcers, duodenal lesions typically present with burning epigastric pain that improves temporarily after eating food or taking antacids.

Two primary etiologies dominate clinical presentations in Nigerian healthcare facilities:

  1. Helicobacter pylori infection: A spiral-shaped bacterium that colonizes the mucosal layer, weakening the protective barrier and increasing acid secretion.
  2. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: Medications like ibuprofen or diclofenac inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, which normally maintains mucosal blood flow and mucus production.

Complications can develop rapidly if erosion deepens. Hemorrhage occurs when the ulcer erodes into nearby blood vessels, presenting as melena or hematemesis. Perforation into the peritoneal cavity leads to sudden, severe acute abdomen requiring emergency surgical intervention.

Inflammatory and Infectious Duodenitis

Duodenitis is the inflammation of the duodenal mucosa, often sharing similar causes with peptic ulcer disease. Endoscopic examination reveals mucosal erythema, edema, and sometimes superficial erosions without deep crater formation. Patients frequently complain of postprandial fullness, nausea, and burning upper abdominal discomfort.

Infection remains a primary trigger, with Helicobacter pylori leading the list. Parasitic infections, such as Giardia lamblia, can also target the proximal small bowel, causing malabsorption and chronic inflammation. Additionally, ingestion of corrosive substances or heavy alcohol use irritates the delicate mucosal layer, leading to acute inflammatory responses. Management focuses on identifying the underlying trigger, eradicating causative pathogens with targeted antimicrobial therapy, and administering mucosal protective agents to promote healing.

Duodenal Obstruction and Ileus

Obstruction of the duodenum prevents the normal passage of chyme and digestive secretions downstream, leading to gastric and duodenal dilation. Causes range from mechanical barriers to functional motor failure.

  • Mechanical Obstruction: Caused by chronic peptic ulcer scarring, malignant tumors of the pancreas or duodenum, or foreign bodies.
  • Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome: Compression of the third part of the duodenum between the abdominal aorta and the superior mesenteric artery, often seen after rapid weight loss.
  • Paralytic Ileus: A functional impairment of peristalsis following abdominal surgery, severe trauma, or electrolyte imbalances.

Clinical manifestations include projectile non-bilious or bilious vomiting depending on the level of obstruction, visible upper abdominal distension, and electrolyte derangements due to continuous loss of gastric acid and chlorides. Nurses must monitor fluid balance strictly and maintain nasogastric decompression to relieve proximal pressure.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Accurate diagnosis of duodenal disorders requires a thorough clinical history focusing on pain characteristics, dietary habits, and medication history, particularly the use of pain relievers. Physical examination may reveal epigastric tenderness, while severe complications present with board-like rigidity and absent bowel sounds.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy aims to reduce gastric acid secretion, eradicate infectious agents, and enhance mucosal defense mechanisms. Proton pump inhibitors, such as omeprazole, suppress gastric acid production effectively and promote rapid ulcer healing. Histamine-2 receptor antagonists offer an alternative acid-suppression mechanism, though they are generally less potent than proton pump inhibitors.

Eradicating Helicobacter pylori requires a multi-drug regimen, typically combining a proton pump inhibitor with at least two antibiotics like clarithromycin and amoxicillin or metronidazole for a duration of one to two weeks. Antacids provide rapid, short-term relief by neutralizing existing luminal acid. Cytoprotective agents like sucralfate coat the ulcer base, shielding it from acid and pepsin attack. Nurses must educate patients on completing full antibiotic courses even when symptoms subside to prevent treatment failure and bacterial resistance.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgery becomes necessary when duodenal disorders fail medical therapy or present with life-threatening complications like massive hemorrhage, intractable obstruction, or perforation. Procedures range from emergency oversewing of a bleeding ulcer to definitive surgeries that reduce acid production.

Common surgical procedures include vagotomy, which cuts the vagus nerve branches to decrease acid stimulation, often paired with a drainage procedure like pyloroplasty or partial gastrectomy. In cases of severe perforation, an emergency laparotomy is performed to wash out the peritoneal cavity and close the defect.

Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring vital signs for hemorrhage or sepsis, managing surgical pain, maintaining patency of nasogastric tubes without flushing unless specifically ordered, and gradually reintroducing oral fluids once bowel sounds return. Nurses must watch for dumping syndrome, a common complication after gastric surgeries characterized by early postprandial weakness, dizziness, and diarrhea.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

A comprehensive nursing care plan for duodenal disorders addresses pain relief, nutritional balance, anxiety reduction, and prevention of recurrence. Nurses assess pain intensity regularly and administer prescribed analgesics or anti-ulcer medications promptly.

Dietary counseling is vital for long-term recovery. Patients should avoid known irritants such as spicy foods, caffeine, alcohol, and carbonated beverages. Eating smaller, frequent meals prevents large fluctuations in gastric acid levels. Quitting cigarette smoking is strongly encouraged, as nicotine impairs mucosal healing and increases acid production.

Discharge education must emphasize medication adherence, recognizing warning signs of complications like severe abdominal pain, vomiting blood, or black tarry stools, and keeping follow-up appointments for diagnostic re-evaluations.
