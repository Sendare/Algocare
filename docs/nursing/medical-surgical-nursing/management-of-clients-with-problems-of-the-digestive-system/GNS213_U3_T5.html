Disorders of the Oesophagus

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oesophagus

The oesophagus is a muscular, collapsible tube measuring about twenty-five centimeters in length, connecting the pharynx to the stomach. Food is propelled downward through this conduit via coordinated wave-like smooth muscle contractions known as peristalsis. At the proximal and distal ends, specialized circular muscle groups act as valves. The upper oesophageal sphincter prevents air from entering the gastrointestinal tract during breathing, while the lower oesophageal sphincter acts as a barrier to stop acidic gastric contents from splashing upward. When these mechanisms fail, patients experience irritation and structural changes along the mucosal lining.

Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures for Oesophageal Disorders

Evaluating upper gastrointestinal complaints requires a combination of clinical history and specialized investigations. Common diagnostic modalities include:

  • Oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD): Direct visualization of the mucosal lining using a flexible scope to identify inflammation, ulcers, or masses.
  • Barium Swallow: An X-ray imaging procedure utilizing a contrast medium to highlight structural abnormalities, strictures, or hernias.
  • Manometry: Measures the intraluminal pressure and muscular coordination during swallowing.
  • 24-Hour pH Monitoring: Tracks acid exposure levels within the distal oesophagus over a full day.

Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD) and Hiatus Hernia

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease occurs when chronic exposure to gastric juice damages the oesophageal squamous epithelium, presenting clinically as pyrosis or retrosternal burning pain. Contributing factors include transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation and delayed gastric emptying. A hiatus hernia frequently coexists, where a portion of the stomach pushes upward through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the thoracic cavity. This anatomical displacement compromises the mechanical barrier of the lower sphincter, worsening acid reflux symptoms. Unchecked chronic GORD can cause metaplasia known as Barrett's oesophagus, a known precursor to malignancy.

Oesophagitis and Oesophageal Ulceration

Oesophagitis involves inflammation of the mucosal lining, most frequently triggered by prolonged exposure to gastric acid, though infections like candidiasis or corrosive chemical ingestion also play a role. When mucosal damage deepens past the epithelial layer, discrete oesophageal ulcers form, manifesting as severe painful swallowing, known as odynophagia. Patients may report retrosternal pain that worsens with eating. Management focuses on acid suppression using proton pump inhibitors, mucosal protectants, and treating underlying infectious or chemical causes to prevent stricture formation.

Oesophageal Motility Disorders: Achalasia and Spasms

Motility disorders disrupt the normal passage of food without necessarily involving physical obstruction or acid reflux. Achalasia is characterized by the failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax during swallowing, combined with the absence of normal peristalsis in the lower two-thirds of the oesophagus. This leads to progressive retention of food and significant dilatation proximal to the blockage. Diffuse oesophageal spasms cause uncoordinated, painful contractions that mimic cardiac chest pain. Treatment ranges from calcium channel blockers and pneumatic dilation to surgical myotomy.

Oesophageal Varices and Management of Bleeding

Portal hypertension—most commonly secondary to liver cirrhosis—forces blood to bypass the liver through collateral vessels, including the submucosal veins of the lower oesophagus. These vessels become dilated and tortuous, forming oesophageal varices that carry a high risk of rupture and massive, life-threatening upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Acute bleeding episodes require urgent resuscitation, intravenous vasoactive drugs like terlipressin to lower portal pressure, and endoscopic band ligation. Nurses monitor vital signs closely for signs of hypovolemic shock and ensure the airway remains protected from aspiration.

Oesophageal Tumours and Carcinoma

Malignant tumours of the oesophagus present in two primary histological forms: squamous cell carcinoma, often linked to smoking and alcohol, and adenocarcinoma, which frequently arises from Barrett's metaplasia secondary to chronic reflux. Progressive dysphagia—initially for solids and eventually progressing to liquids—is the hallmark symptom, accompanied by significant unexplained weight loss. Diagnosis relies on endoscopy with tissue biopsy. Management options include surgical resection, radiotherapy, and palliative interventions to maintain nutritional intake when cure is no longer achievable.

Nursing Care and Patient Management in Oesophageal Disorders

Holistic nursing care for patients with oesophageal disorders centers on nutritional support, symptom management, and complication prevention. Nurses assess swallowing ability carefully before initiating oral intake to minimize aspiration risks. For individuals with structural obstruction or severe dysphagia, enteral nutrition via feeding tubes may be necessary. Post-operative care following oesophageal surgery requires vigilant monitoring of respiratory status, chest drainage systems, and surgical anastomotic sites for signs of leakage. Patient education on dietary modifications, such as eating smaller, frequent meals and avoiding recumbency right after eating, supports long-term recovery.
