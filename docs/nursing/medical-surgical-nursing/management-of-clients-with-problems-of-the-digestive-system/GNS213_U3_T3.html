Gastric Aspiration and Analysis

Introduction and Purpose of Gastric Aspiration

Gastric aspiration involves withdrawing contents from the stomach using a tube passed through the nose or mouth. Clinical teams perform this procedure primarily to decompress the stomach, obtain specimens for laboratory analysis, or remove ingested poisons. In Nigerian health facilities, it serves as a crucial diagnostic and therapeutic intervention for patients presenting with acute poisoning, severe upper gastrointestinal bleeding, or suspected bowel obstruction. Removing stagnant secretions reduces the risk of vomiting and subsequent aspiration pneumonia, protecting vulnerable airway passages.

Indications and Clinical Contraindications

Identifying who needs gastric aspiration requires careful clinical judgment, as certain conditions make the procedure hazardous.

  • Indications: Acute poisoning within a specific window, persistent vomiting due to gastric outlet obstruction, upper gastrointestinal bleeding assessment, and collection of gastric secretions for acid analysis.
  • Contraindications: Ingested strong acids or alkalis that risk esophageal perforation, severe facial trauma, known esophageal strictures, and recent upper gastrointestinal surgery.

Necessary Equipment and Preparation

Gathering the correct tools before starting ensures safety and efficiency. A standard tray includes a nasogastric tube of appropriate size, water-soluble lubricant, clean gloves, a stethoscope, a 50ml catheter-tip syringe, adhesive tape for securing the tube, pH indicator paper, and a basin.

Preparing the patient involves explaining the procedure step-by-step to gain cooperation. Position the patient in a high Fowler's position if conscious, which eases tube passage and prevents choking. Privacy screening and a reassuring bedside manner help reduce anxiety.

Procedure for Nasogastric Tube Insertion and Aspiration

  1. Measurement: Measure the tube from the tip of the nose to the earlobe, then down to the xiphisternum, marking the distance with tape.
  2. Insertion: Lubricate the tip and gently insert it through the nostril downward and backward toward the throat, asking the patient to swallow as it passes.
  3. Verification: Confirm placement by auscultating air injected through the syringe over the stomach, testing aspirate pH, or checking via abdominal X-ray.
  4. Securing and Aspiration: Tape the tube securely to the nose and attach the syringe to withdraw stomach contents gently.

Gastric Analysis Parameters and Testing Methods

Gastric analysis evaluates the secretory function of the stomach by measuring parameters like basal acid output and maximal acid output. Nurses collect baseline fasting gastric juice, followed by specimens collected after stimulating acid production using agents like pentagastrin. Laboratory technicians measure volume, pH, and titratable acidity. These metrics help diagnose conditions such as Zollinger-Ellison syndrome or pernicious anemia by revealing whether the gastric mucosa produces excessive or negligible acid.

Interpretation of Results in Digestive Disorders

Analyzing gastric test results provides insight into various pathologies. An abnormally high acid output points toward peptic ulcer disease or Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, where gastrin-secreting tumors drive relentless acid production. Conversely, achlorhydria—the total absence of hydrochloric acid—strongly suggests chronic atrophic gastritis or pernicious anemia. Correlating these laboratory findings with the patient's clinical symptoms ensures accurate diagnosis and targeted medical management.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care

Nursing care during gastric aspiration extends across pre-procedure, intra-procedure, and post-procedure phases. The nurse maintains accurate intake and output records, monitoring the volume and color of drained secretions. Oral hygiene is vital because keeping the tube in place dries the mucous membranes. Regular assessment of nasal skin integrity prevents pressure ulcers caused by tape friction. Monitoring vital signs ensures early detection of fluid and electrolyte imbalances resulting from continuous gastric drainage.

Potential Complications and Prevention

Although routine, gastric aspiration carries risks if proper protocols are ignored. Potential complications include trauma to the nasal mucosa, accidental placement into the respiratory tract, electrolyte disturbances from fluid loss, and aspiration pneumonia. Nurses prevent these adverse events by rigorously confirming tube placement before every instillation or feeding, securing the tube properly, and monitoring serum electrolyte levels. Recognizing early signs of respiratory distress ensures prompt intervention.
