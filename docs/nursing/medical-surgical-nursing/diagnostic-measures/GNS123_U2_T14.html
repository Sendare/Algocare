Positron Emission Tomography (PET)

Introduction and Underlying Principles of PET

Positron Emission Tomography is an advanced nuclear medicine imaging technique that visualizes metabolic and biochemical changes in living tissues. Unlike standard X-rays or computed tomography scans that only show static anatomical structures, a PET scan reveals how organs and tissues are functioning at a cellular level.

The procedure relies on the administration of a radiopharmaceutical, which is a safe, short-lived radioactive tracer attached to a natural body compound like glucose. As the tracer breaks down inside the body, it emits tiny particles called positrons. When a positron collides with an electron, they annihilate each other and release a pair of gamma photons shooting in opposite directions. The PET scanner ring detects these gamma rays to create a detailed, colorful map showing areas of high and low metabolic activity.

Radiopharmaceuticals and Tracer Dynamics

  • Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG): The most common tracer used in clinical practice, acting like glucose to highlight high-energy cells such as cancer cells or active brain regions.
  • Radiotracer half-life: The duration it takes for half of the radioactive material to decay, which is typically very short (around 110 minutes for fluorine-18).
  • Uptake period: The mandatory resting time required between injecting the tracer and starting the scan so the compound can accumulate in target tissues.
  • Excretion pathway: Most radiopharmaceuticals are cleared safely from the body through the renal system into the urine.

Clinical Indications in Medical-Surgical Nursing

PET imaging plays a vital role across multiple medical-surgical specialties by guiding diagnosis, staging, and treatment evaluation. In oncology, it helps detect primary tumors, assess metastasis, and determine if chemotherapy is working. In neurology, it assists in mapping seizure foci and evaluating neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer's disease. Cardiology utilizes PET to identify viable myocardial tissue following a myocardial infarction, helping physicians decide if revascularization will benefit the patient.

Pre-Procedure Patient Preparation and Assessment

Thorough preparation ensures accurate scan results and prevents procedure cancellation. Nurses must instruct patients to fast for four to six hours prior to the appointment, though drinking plain water is strongly encouraged.

Blood glucose monitoring is critical for diabetic patients. If blood sugar levels are excessively high, circulating glucose will compete with the FDG tracer, resulting in a false-negative or uninterpretable scan. Strenuous exercise should be avoided for 24 hours before the test. Nurses must also screen for claustrophobia, pregnancy, and breastfeeding status, providing reassurance and necessary accommodations.

Intra-Procedure Care and Nursing Responsibilities

During the uptake phase, the patient rests quietly in a dimly lit, comfortable room for 45 to 60 minutes. The nurse ensures the room is warm and quiet to minimize muscle tension and brown fat activation.

Once the uptake period concludes, the patient voids their bladder immediately before entering the scanner room to reduce radiation exposure to the pelvic organs and eliminate motion artifacts from a full bladder. The scan itself takes about 30 to 45 minutes, during which the patient must remain completely still while the table moves through the ring-shaped detector.

Post-Procedure Care and Radiation Safety

Radiation safety principles guide the immediate post-procedure period. Although the radiopharmaceutical decays rapidly, nurses must educate patients on simple precautions to protect family members and hospital staff.

Patients should drink plenty of fluids after the scan to flush residual radioactive material out of their bladder through frequent urination. For the remainder of the day, patients are advised to limit prolonged, close contact with pregnant women and infants as a precautionary measure. Standard hand hygiene after using the restroom is sufficient for decontamination.
