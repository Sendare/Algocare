MRI

Introduction and Underlying Physics of MRI

Magnetic Resonance Imaging is a diagnostic tool that creates detailed cross-sectional pictures of body structures without using ionizing radiation like X-rays or CT scans. Instead, it relies on a powerful magnetic field and radiofrequency waves. The human body is mostly water, meaning it contains billions of hydrogen atoms. Each hydrogen atom has a proton at its core that acts like a tiny spinning magnet.

When a patient enters the strong magnetic field of the MRI scanner, these protons line up in the same direction. The machine then sends out a radiofrequency pulse that knocks the protons out of alignment. Once the pulse stops, the protons return to their original positions, releasing signals as they realign. A computer reads these signals and translates them into high-resolution grayscale images. Different tissues release signals at different rates, allowing radiologists to clearly distinguish between healthy and diseased organs.

Clinical Indications in Medical-Surgical Nursing

Doctors order MRIs when they need superior visualization of soft tissues, blood vessels, ligaments, and the central nervous system. Common reasons for this imaging study include:

  • Brain and Spinal Cord Disorders: Detecting multiple sclerosis plaques, brain tumors, strokes, and spinal nerve compression.
  • Musculoskeletal Injuries: Evaluating torn ligaments, meniscal tears in the knee, rotator cuff damage, and bone infections.
  • Cardiovascular Conditions: Assessing heart muscle damage after a myocardial infarction and congenital heart defects.
  • Abdominal and Pelvic Issues: Examining organs like the liver, uterus, and prostate for masses or structural abnormalities.

Safety Protocols and Absolute Contraindications

The massive magnetic field inside the scanning room is permanently active, even when the machine is not actively imaging. This creates severe safety hazards for anyone carrying metallic implants or objects. Nurses must carefully screen patients before they step near the scanning suite.

Absolute contraindications include metallic foreign bodies in the eye, certain aneurysm clips, and older pacemakers or defibrillators that can malfunction or heat up in the magnetic field. Relative contraindications involve claustrophobia, pregnancy during the first trimester, severe kidney disease when contrast is needed, and non-removable tattoos containing metallic ink that might cause skin irritation.

Comprehensive Patient Preparation

Thorough preparation reduces patient anxiety and prevents dangerous safety breaches. Nursing responsibilities begin days or hours before the scheduled scan.

  1. Screening and Consent: Complete a detailed safety checklist to rule out metal implants, occupational metal exposure, and pregnancy. Obtain informed consent if contrast dye is required.
  2. Removing Metal Items: Instruct the patient to remove jewelry, watches, hearing aids, belts, hairpins, and removable dental appliances. Provide a secure locker for valuables.
  3. Changing Clothes: Have the patient change into a hospital gown to ensure no hidden zippers, snaps, or metallic threads enter the suite.
  4. Addressing Claustrophobia: Assess the patient's anxiety levels. Administer prescribed sedatives or anti-anxiety medications well before the scan if the patient is severely enclosed-space phobic.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Care

During the scan, the patient lies flat on a motorized table that slides into a narrow tunnel. The machine produces loud tapping, clicking, and thumping noises as the gradients switch rapidly. Nurses must provide earplugs or specialized headphones to protect hearing and allow communication.

If the patient requires gadolinium-based contrast media to highlight blood vessels or lesions, the nurse establishes an intravenous line before the procedure. Throughout the imaging session, which can last from 30 to 90 minutes, the nurse monitors the patient via an intercom system and a viewing window, watching closely for signs of panic or adverse contrast reactions.

Post-Procedure Care and Complication Management

Once the scan is complete, the motorized table slides back out of the tunnel. The nurse assists the patient to a sitting position slowly, as lying flat for a prolonged period can cause orthostatic hypotension, especially if mild sedatives were given.

If gadolinium contrast was administered, the nurse monitors for allergic reactions such as hives, nausea, or shortness of breath, though these are less common than with iodine-based CT contrast. Encourage the patient to increase fluid intake to help flush the contrast agent out through the kidneys. Patients who received sedatives must be discharged in the care of a responsible adult and advised not to drive or operate machinery for the rest of the day.
