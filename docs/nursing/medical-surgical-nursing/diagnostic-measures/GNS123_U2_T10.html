X-Ray

Introduction to X-Ray Imaging

X-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation used to visualize internal structures of the human body. When X-ray beams pass through the body, different tissues absorb the radiation at varying rates depending on their density. Dense structures like bone absorb most of the radiation and appear white on the developed image, whereas softer tissues like fat and muscle absorb less and appear in shades of gray. Air-filled spaces, such as the lungs, offer very little resistance and appear black.

Nursing staff encounter X-ray imaging daily across emergency wards, outpatient departments, and intensive care units. Understanding how these images are formed allows nurses to communicate effectively with radiographers and prepare patients adequately for diagnostic examinations.

Principles of Radiation Safety

Radiation exposure carries cumulative biological risks, making safety protocols a core duty for nurses in diagnostic settings. The foundational pillars of radiation protection are time, distance, and shielding.

  • Time: Minimize the duration of exposure around active radiation sources.
  • Distance: Increase physical separation from the radiation source, as intensity drops sharply with distance following the inverse square law.
  • Shielding: Utilize lead aprons, thyroid collars, and mobile barriers to block scatter radiation.

Nurses must ensure that any woman of childbearing age is screened for potential pregnancy before entering the X-ray suite to protect the developing fetus from teratogenic effects.

Clinical Indications and Uses

Physicians order X-rays to diagnose, monitor, and evaluate various acute and chronic medical conditions. Common clinical indications include:

  • Chest X-rays: Assessing for pneumonia, pulmonary edema, cardiomegaly, and pneumothorax.
  • Abdominal X-rays: Identifying bowel obstruction, free air under the diaphragm, or radiopaque foreign bodies.
  • Musculoskeletal X-rays: Locating bone fractures, joint dislocations, and degenerative bone disease.

Patient Preparation and Pre-Procedural Assessment

Effective preparation reduces the need for repeat exposures, sparing the patient from unnecessary radiation. Nurses must verify the exact clinical order against the patient's identity band to prevent wrong-site imaging.

Before the patient enters the X-ray room, instruct them to remove all metal objects, jewelry, buttons, and zippers near the area of interest, as these create dense artifacts that obscure anatomical details. For abdominal or pelvic scans, dietary restrictions or bowel preparation may be required to clear gas and fecal matter that mimic pathology.

The Nursing Role During the Procedure

During the actual exposure, the nurse often assists with patient positioning, especially when dealing with confused, pediatric, or critically ill patients attached to life support lines. If the patient is unstable, the nurse must monitor intravenous lines, oxygen tubing, and indwelling catheters to ensure they remain secure and do not tangle or pull during movement.

Clear communication is vital. The nurse instructs the patient when to hold still and, during chest imaging, when to take a deep breath and hold it to maximize lung expansion.

Post-Procedure Care and Safety

Once the X-ray images are captured and verified by the radiologist for quality, post-procedure care focuses on patient comfort and safety restoration. If contrast media was used, nurses monitor for delayed allergic reactions or encourage increased fluid intake to promote renal clearance.

Patients who left their units return to their beds, and proper alignment is restored. Nurses document the procedure, noting the date, time, reason, and any specific positioning challenges encountered.

Interpreting Common X-Ray Findings

While formal interpretation is the responsibility of the radiologist or attending physician, nurses must recognize critical abnormalities to initiate timely interventions. On a chest radiograph, a normal lung field appears dark gray with branching white lines representing pulmonary blood vessels. Conditions like pleural effusion appear as blunting of the costophrenic angles with fluid accumulation, while tension pneumothorax displays hyperlucency without lung markings and a shift of the mediastinum.

On abdominal films, the presence of free air under the diaphragm indicates a perforated hollow viscus, which is a surgical emergency.

Challenges and Considerations in Nigerian Healthcare

Providing diagnostic imaging services in many Nigerian healthcare facilities introduces unique operational hurdles. Frequent electrical power fluctuations and public grid failures necessitate reliable alternative power sources, such as heavy-duty generators or uninterrupted power supply units, to prevent sudden machine shutdowns during examinations.

Equipment maintenance backlogs and shortages of personal protective gear or lead aprons increase occupational hazards for nursing staff and radiographers. Nurses must advocate for regular equipment servicing, adhere strictly to institutional safety protocols, and educate patients who may hold cultural fears or misconceptions regarding radiation exposure.
