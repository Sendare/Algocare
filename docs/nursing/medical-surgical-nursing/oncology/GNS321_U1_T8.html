Management of Patients with Cancer

Introduction to Cancer Care and Treatment Goals

Cancer management requires a structured approach centered on specific therapeutic objectives. Treatment goals generally fall into three distinct categories based on disease stage and patient status.

  • Cure: Eradication of the malignant disease, restoring the patient to normal life expectancy.
  • Control: Containing tumor growth and preventing further spread when a complete cure is unattainable.
  • Palliations: Alleviating symptoms, maintaining functional capacity, and maximizing comfort when disease progression is irreversible.

Nurses play a pivotal role in explaining these objectives to patients and families, helping them adjust expectations as the disease trajectory evolves.

Surgical Management in Oncology

Surgery remains one of the oldest and most effective interventions for solid tumors. Depending on the clinical intent, surgical procedures serve several distinct purposes in oncology:

  • Diagnostic Biopsy: Obtaining tissue samples to establish histological diagnosis and tumor grading.
  • Curative Resection: Removing the entire tumor along with a margin of healthy surrounding tissue to ensure clear margins.
  • Prophylactic Surgery: Removing non-vital organs or tissues that are at high risk of malignant transformation due to genetic mutations.
  • Palliative Surgery: Relieving complications such as bowel obstruction or severe pain to improve quality of life when a cure is impossible.
  • Reconstructive Surgery: Restoring form or function following extensive tumor resection, commonly seen in breast or facial cancers.

Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring for hemorrhage, infection, and deep vein thrombosis, alongside managing surgical site drains and promoting early mobilization.

Principles and Nursing Care of Patients Undergoing Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy uses ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of malignant cells, leading to cell death. It can be delivered externally via a machine (teletherapy) or internally via implanted radioactive sources (brachytherapy). Rapidly dividing normal tissues, such as the skin, GI tract mucosa, and bone marrow, are also affected, leading to predictable side effects.

Skin care is a major nursing responsibility for patients receiving external beam radiation. Nurses instruct patients to wash the irradiated area gently with lukewarm water and mild soap, avoiding scrubbing, adhesive tapes, and exposure to extreme temperatures or sunlight. For patients with internal radiation implants, safety protocols include limiting visitor time, maintaining distance from the radiation source, and wearing a dosimeter badge to monitor cumulative occupational exposure.

Chemotherapy Administration and Safety Precautions

Chemotherapy involves systemic medications designed to interrupt cell replication. Because these agents are hazardous, strict safety protocols govern their preparation and administration. Nurses must wear personal protective equipment, including powder-free chemotherapy gloves, gowns, and eye protection when handling drugs and body fluids containing residual agents.

Extravasation represents a major risk during intravenous chemotherapy administration. If a vesicant drug leaks into surrounding subcutaneous tissues, it can cause severe tissue necrosis. Nurses must immediately stop the infusion, disconnect the tubing, and follow institutional extravasation protocols, which may involve aspirating residual drug and applying warm or cold compresses depending on the specific agent.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

Unlike traditional chemotherapy that attacks all rapidly dividing cells, targeted therapies and immunotherapies focus on specific molecular characteristics of cancer cells. Targeted drugs block specific proteins or receptors involved in tumor growth, whereas immunotherapy harnesses the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy malignant cells.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors, a common form of immunotherapy, can unleash an overactive immune response, leading to immune-related adverse events affecting the bowel, lungs, liver, or endocrine glands. Nurses monitor patients for unusual diarrhea, persistent cough, or endocrine abnormalities, educating them to report these signs early to prevent severe organ damage.

Oncological Emergencies and Acute Complications

Oncological emergencies can arise at any stage of the disease process, requiring prompt recognition and intervention to prevent mortality.

  • Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS): Compression of the superior vena cava by tumor mass, leading to facial edema, jugular venous distention, and dyspnea.
  • Spinal Cord Compression: Tumor invasion into the epidural space, causing severe back pain, motor weakness, and sensory loss.
  • Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS): Rapid destruction of malignant cells releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids into the bloodstream, resulting in severe electrolyte imbalances and acute kidney injury.
  • Hypercalcemia of Malignancy: Release of parathyroid hormone-related protein by tumors, causing high serum calcium levels manifested by muscle weakness, lethargy, and dehydration.

Pain and Symptom Management in Cancer

Pain is one of the most feared symptoms in oncology, yet it can be successfully managed in the majority of patients using the World Health Organization analgesic ladder. Nurses must conduct regular, comprehensive pain assessments, evaluating intensity, location, and character using standardized scales.

Beyond pharmacological approaches, symptom management addresses concurrent challenges such as cancer-related fatigue, nausea, and cachexia. Antiemetics administered proactively before chemotherapy can prevent severe vomiting. Nutritional counseling and appetite stimulants assist in managing wasting syndromes, supporting overall treatment tolerance.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care

A cancer diagnosis impacts not only physical well-being but also emotional, social, and spiritual health. Patients frequently experience anxiety, depression, body image disturbance, and existential distress. Nurses provide therapeutic communication, facilitate support group referrals, and involve pastoral care services when appropriate.

Palliative care should be integrated early in the cancer trajectory, alongside active disease-directed treatments. It focuses on improving quality of life for both patients and their families by addressing physical comfort, emotional distress, and advance care planning. Nurses guide families through difficult discussions regarding end-of-life preferences, ensuring patient autonomy and dignity are respected.
