Intravenous Pyelogram

Introduction and Definition of Intravenous Pyelogram

An intravenous pyelogram (IVP) is a specialized radiological examination used to visualize the urinary tract, including the kidneys, ureters, and urinary bladder. The procedure involves the intravenous injection of a radiopaque contrast medium that is rapidly filtered by the glomeruli and excreted by the kidneys. As the iodine-containing dye travels through the renal structures, serial X-ray images are captured at timed intervals. This imaging technique highlights the internal contours of the renal pelvis, calyces, and ureteral pathways, allowing healthcare providers to assess both anatomical structure and physiological drainage. While modern cross-sectional imaging like CT scans has reduced reliance on IVP in many tertiary centers, the test remains valuable in resource-constrained environments across Nigeria for diagnosing obstructive uropathy, renal calculi, and structural anomalies.

Indications and Clinical Purposes of IVP

Clinicians order an intravenous pyelogram to investigate various lower and upper urinary tract pathologies. Common clinical indications include:

  • Urolithiasis: Locating radiopaque kidney stones within the renal pelvis or ureters.
  • Hematuria: Identifying the underlying source of blood in the urine, such as a small tumor or vascular malformation.
  • Recurrent Urinary Tract Infections: Detecting anatomical abnormalities like congenital duplications or strictures that predispose patients to stasis and infection.
  • Abdominal Trauma: Assessing for renal lacerations or urinary extravasation following blunt or penetrating injuries.
  • Flank Pain: Determining if obstruction is causing unilateral or bilateral hydronephrosis.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

Before scheduling an intravenous pyelogram, nurses must perform a thorough risk assessment to identify absolute and relative contraindications. The administration of iodinated contrast carries significant risks for specific patient populations.

Risk FactorClinical Implication
Allergy to Iodine/ShellfishHigh risk of anaphylactoid reactions or severe hypersensitivity.
Renal ImpairmentHigh risk of contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) due to reduced clearance.
PregnancyExposure to ionizing radiation poses teratogenic risks to the fetus.
Metformin TherapyRisk of lactic acidosis if renal function deteriorates post-contrast.

Screening for elevated serum creatinine levels is mandatory in patients with a history of diabetes or chronic kidney disease to prevent precipitating acute kidney injury.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Adequate bowel preparation is crucial for an IVP because fecal matter and gas obscure visualization of the kidneys and ureters on X-ray films. Nurses must administer prescribed cathartics, laxatives, or cleansing enemas the evening before the test. Fasting for several hours prior to the procedure minimizes bowel contents and reduces the risk of aspiration if a contrast reaction induces vomiting.

The nurse must obtain informed consent, ensuring the patient understands the procedure, potential discomforts like a transient flushing sensation upon dye injection, and inherent risks. Asking the patient to void immediately before the X-ray starts ensures the bladder is empty for the baseline scout film.

The IVP Procedure: Step-by-Step Overview

  1. Scout Film: An initial abdominal X-ray is taken without contrast to check patient positioning, bowel preparation, and identify any radiopaque stones already present.
  2. Contrast Injection: The radiologist or nurse administers the iodinated contrast medium intravenously over a few minutes.
  3. Timed Serial Imaging: X-ray films are exposed at specific intervals, typically at 1, 5, 10, and 15 minutes post-injection, to track the contrast as it fills the renal parenchyma, pelvicalyceal system, and ureters.
  4. Compression Application: Abdominal compression bands may be applied across the lower ureters to delay urine drainage and enhance filling of the renal collecting system.
  5. Post-Void Film: After the compression is released and the patient voids, a final film is taken to evaluate bladder emptying and residual urine volume.

Intra-Procedure and Post-Procedure Nursing Care

During the contrast injection, the nurse observes the patient closely for adverse reactions, reassuring them that a warm flush and a metallic taste in the mouth are normal transient sensations. After the imaging series concludes, nursing priorities shift to promoting safe recovery and contrast elimination.

Encouraging high fluid intake post-procedure helps flush the radiopaque dye out of the urinary tract quickly, protecting the kidneys from nephrotoxic damage. The nurse monitors vital signs, tracks urine output, and watches for delayed hypersensitivity reactions, which can occur hours after the injection. Patients are instructed to report any skin rashes, difficulty breathing, or decreased urine output immediately.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although most intravenous pyelogram procedures proceed without incident, nurses must remain vigilant for acute complications. Mild adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, and urticaria, which usually resolve without aggressive medical intervention. Severe, life-threatening anaphylactoid reactions can cause bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, severe hypotension, and cardiovascular collapse.

Emergency equipment, including oxygen, suction, and emergency drugs like adrenaline and antihistamines, must be immediately accessible in the imaging suite. If a severe reaction occurs, the nurse stops the contrast infusion immediately, maintains a patent airway, administers emergency medications per protocol, and notifies the medical team without delay.

Interpretation of IVP Results in Genito-Urinary Disorders

Interpreting IVP films involves analyzing the symmetry, size, and drainage patterns of both kidneys. Normal results demonstrate smooth renal outlines, prompt excretion of contrast into the pelvicalyceal system, and unobstructed ureteral passage into the bladder.

Pathological findings include filling defects, which suggest calculi, blood clots, or tumors that displace the contrast dye. Hydronephrosis appears as abnormal dilation of the renal pelvis and calyces, pointing toward a distal obstruction like a ureteral stricture or stone. Delayed or absent excretion of dye on one side indicates renal dysfunction or acute ureteral blockage in that specific kidney.
