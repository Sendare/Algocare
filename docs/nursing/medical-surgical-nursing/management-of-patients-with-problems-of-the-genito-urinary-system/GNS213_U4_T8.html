Nephrotic Conditions of the Kidney

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

Nephrotic conditions stem from damage to the glomerular capillary membrane, specifically the podocytes and the basement membrane. Normally, this membrane acts as a selective filter that blocks large serum proteins like albumin from entering the urinary space. When injury occurs, the electrical charge and structural integrity of the filter are lost, leading to massive protein leakage into the urine, known as proteinuria. The resulting drop in intravascular oncotic pressure shifts fluid from the blood vessels into the interstitial spaces, causing generalized edema. Simultaneously, the liver attempts to compensate for low blood protein by producing more lipids, leading to severe hyperlipidemia.

Classification and Etiology

Nephrotic syndrome is broadly categorized into primary, idiopathic forms originating within the kidney itself, and secondary forms resulting from systemic diseases.

  • Minimal Change Disease (MCD): The most common cause in children, characterized by normal-appearing glomeruli under light microscopy but foot process effacement on electron microscopy.
  • Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS): Scars develop in some, but not all, glomeruli; common in adults and often resistant to standard steroid therapy.
  • Membranous Nephropathy: Thickening of the glomerular basement membrane caused by immune complex deposition, frequently seen in older adults.
  • Secondary Causes: Systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, systemic lupus erythematosus, and certain infections like hepatitis B or malaria that damage the renal architecture over time.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on a 24-hour urine collection for total protein, comprehensive metabolic panels showing low albumin, and occasionally a renal biopsy to identify the specific histological subtype.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Treatment goals focus on reducing proteinuria, controlling edema, preventing complications, and addressing the underlying disease trigger. Corticosteroids, such as prednisone, serve as the first-line therapy to induce remission, particularly in minimal change disease. Patients who do not respond or experience frequent relapses may require calcineurin inhibitors or other immunosuppressive agents.

Diuretics like furosemide help manage peripheral edema, though they must be used cautiously to avoid intravascular volume depletion and acute kidney injury. ACE inhibitors or ARBs are frequently prescribed to lower intraglomerular pressure and reduce urinary protein loss.

Nutritional and Fluid Management

Dietary adjustments play a supportive role in managing fluid balance and metabolic disturbances. Historically, high-protein diets were recommended, but current evidence suggests a normal-protein diet with high biological value to avoid hyperfiltration injury in the remaining functional nephrons. Sodium restriction is critical to help control severe edema and enhance the effectiveness of diuretic therapy. Fluid restriction may be necessary if the patient experiences severe hyponatremia or refractory fluid overload. Additionally, because hyperlipidemia is common, a diet low in saturated fats is advised to mitigate long-term cardiovascular risks.

Complications and Nursing Interventions

Nephrotic syndrome exposes patients to several severe systemic complications that require vigilant nursing oversight. Due to the urinary loss of immunoglobulins, patients face a high risk of infection, particularly peritonitis and cellulitis. The loss of clotting regulatory proteins like antithrombin III creates a hypercoagulable state, elevating the risk of thromboembolism, especially renal vein thrombosis.

Nurses must monitor daily weights, accurately measure abdominal girth, and record strict fluid intake and output. Regular skin assessments are vital because edematous tissue is fragile and prone to pressure ulcers and breakdown. Monitoring for signs of infection or sudden chest pain associated with pulmonary embolism remains a core nursing responsibility.

Patient Education and Home Care

Preparing patients and their families for long-term self-management is essential for preventing recurrent relapses. Education should cover the signs of impending relapse, such as sudden weight gain or facial puffiness, allowing for early medical intervention. Patients on chronic corticosteroid therapy must understand the importance of taking medications exactly as prescribed and avoiding abrupt cessation. Families should be taught how to test urine for protein using dipsticks at home when requested by the physician. Guidance on infection prevention, including avoiding crowded places and maintaining proper hand hygiene, protects immunocompromised individuals during vulnerable phases.
