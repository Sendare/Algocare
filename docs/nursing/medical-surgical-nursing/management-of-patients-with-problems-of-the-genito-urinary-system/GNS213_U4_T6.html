Acute Renal Failure

Introduction and Definition of Acute Renal Failure

Acute Renal Failure, often referred to today as acute kidney injury, is the sudden loss of kidney function occurring over hours or days. This rapid decline leads to an inability of the kidneys to maintain fluid balance, electrolyte stability, and acid-base homeostasis. Nitrogenous waste products, notably urea and creatinine, accumulate rapidly in the bloodstream, a condition known as azotemia. Unlike chronic kidney disease, which develops over months and years, acute renal failure is potentially reversible if the underlying cause is identified and treated promptly. In Nigerian clinical settings, early recognition is vital because late presentation often complicates management and increases mortality rates.

Classification of Causes: Prerenal, Intrarenal, and Postrenal

Causes of acute renal failure are categorized into three main pathophysiological groups based on where the primary insult occurs:

  • Prerenal causes: Factors reducing blood flow to the kidneys, such as severe dehydration, hemorrhage, septic shock, or heart failure. The kidneys themselves are structurally intact initially.
  • Intrarenal causes: Direct damage to the kidney tissue, including acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, nephrotoxic drugs, or severe ischemia.
  • Postrenal causes: Obstruction of urine flow downstream from the kidneys, caused by conditions like benign prostatic hyperplasia, kidney stones, or tumors.

Pathophysiology and Stages

The disruption of renal function follows a distinct physiological pathway depending on the insult. Ischemia or toxins damage the renal tubular epithelial cells, causing them to slough off and obstruct the tubular lumen. This leads to a drop in the glomerular filtration rate. Clinically, acute renal failure progresses through four distinct phases:

  1. Onset phase: The initial triggering event occurs and lasts until cell injury happens.
  2. Oliguric phase: Urine output drops below 400 mL per day, and waste products accumulate rapidly.
  3. Diuretic phase: Urine output increases significantly as nephrons recover, but the tubules cannot yet concentrate urine effectively.
  4. Recovery phase: Glomerular filtration rate normalizes, and BUN and creatinine levels begin to stabilize.

Clinical Manifestations

The clinical picture of acute renal failure reflects systemic involvement due to fluid and waste accumulation. Patients often present with generalized edema, hypertension, and signs of uremia such as nausea, lethargy, and confusion. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hyperkalemia, pose immediate life-threatening risks by affecting cardiac conduction. Acidosis develops because the kidneys fail to excrete hydrogen ions, leading to Kussmaul breathing as the body attempts to compensate through the respiratory system.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing acute renal failure requires a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and imaging studies. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen rise steadily above baseline levels. Urinalysis helps differentiate causes by revealing casts, protein, or abnormal specific gravity. Renal ultrasound is frequently ordered early to rule out postrenal obstruction by checking for hydronephrosis. Fractional excretion of sodium is another useful diagnostic indicator to distinguish between prerenal azotemia and acute tubular necrosis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment focuses on identifying the root cause, correcting electrolyte imbalances, and managing fluid balance. Diuretics like furosemide may be administered to manage fluid overload, though they do not reverse the underlying renal damage. Hyperkalemia is treated urgently with intravenous regular insulin and glucose, calcium gluconate to protect the myocardium, or sodium polystyrene sulfonate. If conservative measures fail to control fluid overload, severe acidosis, or electrolyte toxicities, emergency renal replacement therapy such as hemodialysis becomes necessary.

Nursing Care and Fluid Management

Nursing management centers on strict intake and output monitoring, daily weight checks, and meticulous patient assessment. Fluid restriction is often necessary during the oliguric phase to prevent pulmonary edema and heart failure. Nurses must monitor vital signs closely, noting any trends in blood pressure and heart rhythm. Dietary counselling is initiated to restrict potassium, sodium, and protein intake while ensuring adequate caloric intake to prevent catabolism.
