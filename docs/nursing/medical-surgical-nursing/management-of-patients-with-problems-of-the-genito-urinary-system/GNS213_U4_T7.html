Chronic Renal Failure

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Chronic Renal Failure

Chronic Renal Failure, also known as Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), involves the progressive and irreversible loss of nephron function over months or years. When functioning nephrons are destroyed, the remaining healthy nephrons undergo hyperfiltration and hypertrophy to compensate. Eventually, this compensatory mechanism fails, leading to glomerular sclerosis and interstitial fibrosis. As the glomerular filtration rate declines, the kidneys lose their ability to regulate fluid and electrolyte balance, excrete nitrogenous wastes, and produce erythropoietin.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors in Nigerian Practice

  • Hypertension: Uncontrolled high blood pressure damages the delicate glomerular vessels over time.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Chronic hyperglycemia leads to diabetic nephropathy and microvascular damage.
  • Chronic Glomerulonephritis: Often linked to untreated streptococcal infections or autoimmune reactions.
  • Chronic Obstruction: Conditions like benign prostatic hyperplasia or untreated kidney stones cause backpressure.
  • Nephrotoxic Agents: Regular use of herbal concoctions, heavy metals, and prolonged non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Stages and Classification of Chronic Kidney Disease

Chronic Kidney Disease is staged from 1 to 5 based on the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) and the presence of kidney damage, such as persistent albuminuria.

Clinical Manifestations and Systemic Effects

As uremic toxins accumulate throughout the body, multiple organ systems begin to fail.

  • Fluid and Electrolyte: Fluid overload leads to peripheral edema and pulmonary congestion. Hyperkalemia develops as potassium excretion drops, threatening cardiac arrest.
  • Hematologic: Decreased erythropoietin production causes severe normocytic anemia, leading to chronic fatigue and pallor.
  • Cardiovascular: Hypertension worsens, and uremic pericarditis may occur due to toxin irritation.
  • Gastrointestinal: Uremic fetor, nausea, vomiting, and anorexia result from circulating urea breakdown by GI flora.
  • Neurological: Peripheral neuropathy, confusion, lethargy, and asterixis appear in advanced uremia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing chronic renal failure requires blood tests, imaging, and urinalysis to confirm permanent loss of function. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels rise significantly as filtration falls, though creatinine is a more reliable standalone indicator. The estimated GFR is calculated using serum creatinine along with age, sex, and sometimes weight. Urinalysis checks for persistent proteinuria or hematuria. Renal ultrasound typically reveals small, shrunken kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, differentiating chronic disease from acute kidney injury.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Medical therapy aims to slow disease progression, control symptoms, and manage complications. Blood pressure control is paramount, usually achieved using ACE inhibitors or ARBs, which reduce intraglomerular pressure. Dietary protein restriction lessens the accumulation of nitrogenous waste products. Diuretics manage fluid overload, while phosphate binders prevent hyperphosphatemia by binding dietary phosphorus in the gut. Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents and iron supplements correct anemia, and sodium bicarbonate treats metabolic acidosis.

Renal Replacement Therapy: Dialysis and Transplantation

When pharmacological management fails to control uremia and fluid balance in stage 5 CKD, renal replacement therapy becomes necessary. Hemodialysis filters blood outside the body using a machine and a specialized membrane called a dialyzer, requiring vascular access such as an arteriovenous fistula. Peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's own peritoneal membrane as a filter, infusing and draining dialysis fluid through an abdominal catheter. Kidney transplantation offers the best long-term outcome and quality of life, involving the surgical placement of a healthy donor kidney from a living or deceased donor, followed by lifelong immunosuppressive therapy.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for chronic renal failure focuses on monitoring fluid balance, preventing infection, and supporting dietary adherence. Nurses must accurately record daily weights, strict fluid intake and output, and vital signs to detect fluid overload early. Dietary education is critical: patients must restrict potassium, phosphorus, sodium, and protein intake according to their stage. Skin care is essential because uremic frost and dry skin cause severe itching. Finally, nurses provide psychological support, helping patients and their families cope with the lifestyle adjustments required for long-term management.
