Cardiac Trauma

Pathophysiology and Classification of Cardiac Injuries

Cardiac trauma is broadly divided into two main categories based on the nature of the mechanical force applied to the chest wall: blunt cardiac injury and penetrating cardiac trauma.

  • Blunt Cardiac Injury (BCI): Occurs most frequently during high-speed motor vehicle accidents, steering wheel impacts, or severe falls. The force can cause myocardial contusion, chamber rupture, or disruption of heart valves. A common misconception is that a normal ECG rules out BCI; however, myocardial contusion can present with subtle arrhythmias or troponin elevation hours after the event.
  • Penetrating Cardiac Trauma: Caused by sharp objects like knives or high-velocity projectiles such as bullets. These injuries usually create a direct communication between the pericardial space and the outside environment or cardiac chambers, leading to rapid blood loss and cardiac tamponade.

Clinical Manifestations and Beck's Triad

Recognizing cardiac trauma requires swift assessment of hemodynamic stability. The classic presentation of acute cardiac tamponade is known as Beck's triad, which consists of:

  1. Hypotension: Caused by restricted ventricular filling and decreased cardiac output.
  2. Distended neck veins: Resulting from elevated central venous pressure as blood backs up into the venous system.
  3. Muffled heart sounds: Caused by fluid accumulating within the rigid pericardial sac.

Patients may also exhibit Kussmaul's sign, an unexpected rise in jugular venous pressure during inspiration. In emergency departments across Nigeria, recognizing these signs promptly can differentiate life-threatening tamponade from simple tension pneumothorax.

Emergency Diagnostic Assessment

Rapid evaluation is critical when cardiac injury is suspected. The Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) exam is the bedside gold standard for detecting pericardial fluid. An ultrasound view of the subcostal area can quickly reveal blood in the pericardial space even before the patient enters the operating theatre.

Chest radiography may show a widened mediastinum or an enlarged, water-bottle-shaped heart shadow in chronic or subacute effusions, though it is often normal in acute penetrating wounds. Laboratory tests, including serial cardiac biomarkers and arterial blood gases, help gauge the extent of cellular hypoxia and myocardial stress.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation

Nursing care for patients with suspected cardiac trauma focuses on stabilizing hemodynamics and preparing for emergency surgery.

  • Secure two large-bore peripheral intravenous lines for rapid fluid administration, though excessive aggressive fluid loading can worsen bleeding in penetrating injuries.
  • Continuously monitor cardiac rhythm via ECG to detect life-threatening dysrhythmias like ventricular fibrillation or pulseless electrical activity.
  • Administer supplemental oxygen to maintain adequate tissue perfusion.
  • Prepare emergency equipment for pericardiocentesis or emergency department thoracotomy if directed by the surgical team.

Surgical Management and Post-Operative Complications

Definitive treatment for severe cardiac trauma requires surgical intervention, which may range from subxiphoid pericardial window to emergency thoracotomy or sternotomy. Surgeons suture myocardial lacerations directly, often using pledgets to prevent sutures from tearing through fragile heart muscle.

Post-operative nursing care requires vigilant monitoring in the intensive care unit. Nurses must watch for potential complications such as:

  • Post-pericardiotomy syndrome: Characterized by fever, chest pain, and a pericardial friction rub.
  • Low cardiac output syndrome: Resulting from myocardial stunning or residual injury.
  • Persistent arrhythmias: Requiring antiarrhythmic therapy or temporary pacing.
