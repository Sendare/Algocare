Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Introduction to Electrocardiography

An electrocardiogram, commonly abbreviated as ECG or EKG, is a graphical recording of the electrical activity of the heart over time. Clinical monitors and diagnostic machines capture these tiny electrical impulses from electrodes placed on the skin and translate them into waveforms on paper or a digital screen. Nigerian nursing students must recognize that an ECG does not measure the mechanical pumping action of the myocardium directly; rather, it reflects the electrical depolarizations and repolarizations that trigger those mechanical contractions.

Electrophysiological Principles of the Heart

Cardiac muscle cells maintain a resting membrane potential due to uneven ion distribution across the cell membrane, primarily sodium, potassium, and calcium. When stimulated, rapid influxes of sodium ions cause depolarization, changing the interior from negative to positive and prompting the heart muscle to contract. Repolarization follows as ions shift back via pumps and channels, returning the cell to its resting state. This electrical wave originates naturally at the sinoatrial node, known as the heart's primary pacemaker, before spreading systematically through the specialized conduction pathway.

Standard 12-Lead ECG Placement and Lead Configuration

A standard 12-lead ECG provides twelve different electrical viewpoints of the heart using only ten physical electrodes. These electrodes are categorized into limb leads and precordial chest leads.

  • Limb Leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF): Placed on the four extremities to view the heart in the vertical (frontal) plane.
  • Precordial Leads (V1 to V6): Placed across specific anatomical landmarks on the chest wall to view the heart in the horizontal plane.

Accurate placement is critical because even a slight shift in chest electrode position can mimic cardiac pathology or obscure true ischemic changes.

Anatomy of a Normal ECG Waveform

A single cardiac cycle on an ECG consists of distinct waves, segments, and intervals that correspond to specific phases of electrical activity.

  1. P wave: Represents atrial depolarization.
  2. PR interval: Measures the time taken for the impulse to travel from the atria through the AV node and down to the ventricles.
  3. QRS complex: Represents ventricular depolarization.
  4. ST segment: Shows the period between ventricular depolarization and the beginning of repolarization, normally flat or isoelectric.
  5. T wave: Represents ventricular repolarization.

Systematic Approach to ECG Interpretation

Interpreting an ECG strip requires a consistent, step-by-step method to avoid missing subtle abnormalities. Rushing straight to diagnosing an arrhythmia without a logical sequence leads to clinical errors.

  • Heart Rate: Determine if the rate is normal, tachycardic, or bradycardic.
  • Rhythm: Assess whether the R-R intervals are regular or irregular.
  • P Waves: Check for the presence of upright P waves preceding every QRS complex.
  • Intervals: Measure the PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval.
  • ST Segment and T Waves: Look for elevations, depressions, or inversions.

Common Cardiac Arrhythmias and Dysrhythmias

Arrhythmias occur when the normal electrical conduction system of the heart is disrupted, causing too fast, too slow, or irregular heartbeats. Sinus tachycardia features a normal waveform pattern at a rate above 100 beats per minute, whereas sinus bradycardia drops below 60 beats per minute. Atrial fibrillation shows a completely irregular baseline with no distinct P waves, placing patients at risk for blood stasis and clot formation within the atria. Ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation represent life-threatening emergencies requiring immediate intervention and possible defibrillation.

ECG Changes in Myocardial Infarction and Ischemia

When coronary blood flow is compromised, the affected myocardial tissue undergoes distinct electrical stages progressing from ischemia to injury and eventual infarction. Myocardial ischemia typically presents with T-wave inversion or ST-segment depression, indicating lack of oxygen. Acute injury is marked by prominent ST-segment elevation, a medical emergency often referred to as a STEMI. Old myocardial infarctions leave behind deep, wide Q waves that persist on the tracing permanently.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Obtaining a clean and accurate ECG recording requires careful preparation and technical skill from the nurse. Explain the painless procedure to the patient to reduce anxiety, which can cause muscle tremors and artifact interference. Ensure the skin is clean, dry, and free of excessive hair or oily lotions to promote good electrode contact. Instruct the patient to lie still and avoid talking while the machine captures the tracing.
