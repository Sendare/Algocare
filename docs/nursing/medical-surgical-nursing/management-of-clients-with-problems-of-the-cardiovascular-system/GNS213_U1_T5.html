Structural Cardiac Disorders

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Structural Cardiac Disorders

Structural cardiac disorders involve physical defects or abnormalities of the heart valves, walls, chambers, or great vessels. These anatomical flaws alter normal blood flow dynamics within the cardiovascular system. The heart responds to mechanical stress through remodeling, altering its shape, size, and muscle thickness to maintain cardiac output. Over time, this compensatory mechanism fails, leading to heart failure, arrhythmias, and diminished tissue perfusion.

Valvular Heart Diseases: Stenosis and Regurgitation

Valvular heart disease disrupts the unidirectional flow of blood through the cardiac chambers.

  • Stenosis: Valve leaflets thicken or fuse, narrowing the opening and restricting forward blood flow, which forces the heart to work harder.
  • Regurgitation: Incompetent valve leaflets fail to close completely, allowing blood to leak backward and causing volume overload in the preceding chamber.

Congenital Heart Defects

Congenital structural defects originate during embryonic development.

  • Atrial Septal Defect (ASD): An opening in the interatrial septum allows oxygenated blood to shunt from the left to the right atrium.
  • Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD): A hole in the interventricular septum causes high-pressure left-to-right shunting.
  • Tetralogy of Fallot: A complex anomaly consisting of pulmonary stenosis, ventricular septal defect, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy.

Cardiomyopathies

Cardiomyopathies are diseases of the heart muscle that impair structural integrity and mechanical function without primary coronary artery disease or hypertension.

  • Dilated Cardiomyopathy: Ventricular chambers enlarge and systolic pumping function weakens.
  • Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy: Myocardial walls thicken excessively, reducing internal chamber volume and filling capacity.
  • Restrictive Cardiomyopathy: Ventricular walls become rigid, impeding normal diastolic filling.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Structural cardiac defects manifest through signs of poor tissue perfusion and fluid volume overload. Nurses must assess for dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, peripheral edema, and jugular venous distension. Auscultation frequently reveals abnormal heart sounds, such as murmurs caused by turbulent blood flow across abnormal valves or shunts, along with extra sounds like S3 or S4 gallops.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Accurate diagnosis of structural cardiac disorders relies on advanced visualization tools.

  • Echocardiography: The gold standard imaging method using ultrasound to visualize valve structure, chamber size, and blood flow via Doppler.
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): Detects chamber enlargement, hypertrophy, and secondary arrhythmias.
  • Cardiac Catheterization: An invasive procedure that measures intracardiac pressures and visualizes anatomical defects directly using contrast dye.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical therapy for structural heart disorders aims to reduce workload, manage symptoms, and prevent complications. Diuretics relieve fluid overload and pulmonary congestion. ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers slow ventricular remodeling, reduce blood pressure, and improve survival rates. Anticoagulants are prescribed when conditions like mitral stenosis or atrial fibrillation increase the risk of systemic thromboembolism.

Surgical and Interventional Procedures

When medical management fails to control structural heart defects, surgical or catheter-based interventions become necessary. Valve repair preserves the patient's native valve using techniques like valvuloplasty or ring annuloplasty, whereas valve replacement substitutes the diseased valve with a mechanical or bioprosthetic valve. Percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty is often used to open stenotic valves without open-heart surgery.

Postoperative Nursing Care

Postoperative care following structural heart surgery requires rigorous hemodynamic monitoring in an intensive care setting. Nurses must monitor for complications such as bleeding, cardiac tamponade, arrhythmias, and infection. Pain management, early mobilization, respiratory exercises, and careful monitoring of intake and output are essential components of safe recovery and rehabilitation.
