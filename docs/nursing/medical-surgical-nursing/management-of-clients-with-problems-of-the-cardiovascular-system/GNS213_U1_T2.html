Assessment of Clients with Cardiovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cardiovascular Assessment

Assessing the cardiovascular system requires a systematic approach to uncover hidden heart diseases before they result in acute emergencies. Nurses in Nigerian hospitals frequently encounter patients presenting late with advanced heart failure or severe hypertension. A thorough evaluation combines subjective data from the patient's story with objective findings gathered through physical examination and diagnostic tools. Recognizing subtle changes in perfusion and oxygenation helps prevent rapid clinical deterioration on the ward.

Gathering the Health History

Taking a detailed health history provides crucial clues about underlying cardiac pathology. Nurses must skillfully interview the patient or relatives to uncover critical symptoms and risk factors.

  • Chest pain: Explore the onset, location, radiation, intensity, and precipitating factors using the old cart mnemonic.
  • Dyspnea: Determine if breathing difficulty occurs on exertion, when lying flat, or waking up suddenly at night.
  • Fatigue: Assess unusual tiredness that limits daily activities, often indicating low cardiac output.
  • Edema: Inquire about swelling in the feet, ankles, or abdomen, signaling fluid retention from right-sided heart failure.
  • Palpitations: Ask about fluttering sensations or rapid heartbeats that may indicate arrhythmias.

Physical Examination Techniques

Physical examination follows a strict sequence: inspection, palpation, and auscultation, differing slightly from the abdominal assessment order.

Inspection begins with the general appearance, looking for central cyanosis, clubbing of the fingers, and distended neck veins. Palpation focuses on feeling the pulses for rate, rhythm, and volume, alongside locating the point of maximal impulse on the chest wall. Auscultation requires a quiet environment to listen carefully for normal S1 and S2 heart sounds, as well as abnormal murmurs, gallops, or friction rubs using both the bell and diaphragm of the stethoscope.

Vital Signs and Hemodynamic Parameters

Vital signs are the frontline indicators of cardiovascular stability. Blood pressure measurement requires an appropriately sized cuff; a cuff that is too small yields falsely high readings, while an oversized cuff produces falsely low values. Pulse pressure, the difference between systolic and diastolic numbers, offers insight into arterial elasticity and stroke volume. Assessing orthostatic blood pressure changes helps detect hypovolemia or autonomic dysfunction when a patient transitions from lying to standing.

Diagnostic Investigations

Bedside and laboratory diagnostics confirm clinical suspicions and guide medical management. The standard 12-lead electrocardiogram captures electrical activity to identify ischemia, infarction, or arrhythmias. Cardiac biomarkers like troponin elevate rapidly when myocardial muscle cells sustain ischemic injury. Echocardiography uses ultrasound waves to visualize valve structures, chamber sizes, and ejection fraction, providing a clear picture of overall pump efficiency.

Documentation and Reporting in Clinical Practice

Accurate record-keeping ensures continuity of care across different shift handovers in busy Nigerian wards. Nurses must document objective data precisely, avoiding vague terms like 'heart sounds normal' without specifying if S1 and S2 were heard clearly or if murmurs were absent. Any sudden drop in blood pressure or spiking pulse rate demands immediate verbal reporting to the attending physician alongside prompt chart documentation.
