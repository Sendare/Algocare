Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Infectious cardiac disorders refer to inflammatory conditions of the heart muscle, inner lining, or outer sac caused by invading microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. These infections can damage the delicate structures of the heart, disrupt normal hemodynamic function, and lead to life-threatening complications like heart failure or systemic embolization if left untreated.

  • Endocarditis: Infection of the inner endocardial surface, typically involving the heart valves.
  • Myocarditis: Inflammation of the muscular middle layer of the heart wall, often viral in origin.
  • Pericarditis: Inflammation of the fluid-filled sac surrounding the heart, frequently following respiratory infections.

Infective Endocarditis: Etiology, Pathophysiology, and Clinical Manifestations

Infective endocarditis (IE) occurs when bloodborne microorganisms colonize the endocardial surface of the heart. The process usually begins with endothelial damage, which allows platelets and fibrin to form sterile microthrombi. Transient bacteremia—often introduced via dental procedures, intravenous drug use, or indwelling catheters—then seeds these thrombi, creating infected masses known as vegetations.

Vegetations disrupt normal valve closure and can break off into the bloodstream, causing septic emboli. Classic clinical manifestations include persistent fever, a new or changing cardiac murmur, petechiae, splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails, and painful subcutaneous nodules on the fingertips called Osler's nodes.

Myocarditis: Causes, Pathogenesis, and Clinical Presentation

Myocarditis involves an inflammatory destruction of the myocardial muscle fibers. While viral infections—particularly Coxsackievirus and influenza—are the leading global triggers, bacterial, fungal, and autoimmune reactions also contribute. In tropical regions, parasitic infections like Chagas disease remain important differential diagnoses.

The pathogenesis centers on direct viral injury followed by an immune-mediated response where the body's own immune cells attack infected myocytes. Patients present with fatigue, dyspnea, palpitations, and chest pain that mimics a myocardial infarction, though coronary arteries remain clear. Severe cases rapidly progress to dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure.

Pericarditis: Types, Underlying Causes, and Diagnostic Features

Pericarditis is the inflammation of the pericardial sac, which can be acute or chronic, and dry (fibrinous) or accompanied by effusion (fluid buildup). Viral infections are the most common cause, though autoimmune disorders, uremia, and post-myocardial infarction syndrome (Dressler's syndrome) also play major roles.

The hallmark clinical sign is sharp, pleuritic chest pain that worsens when lying flat and improves when sitting upright and leaning forward. Auscultation often reveals a high-pitched, scratching sound known as a pericardial friction rub. When fluid accumulation restricts ventricular filling, it leads to cardiac tamponade, a dangerous emergency marked by muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins, and hypotension.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation for Cardiac Infections

Thorough nursing assessment forms the backbone of early detection for cardiac infections. Nurses must monitor vital signs meticulously, watching for persistent low-grade fevers, tachycardia out of proportion to fever, and changes in blood pressure. Auscultation must focus on identifying new murmurs, gallops, or friction rubs.

Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on specific investigations:

  • Blood cultures: Essential for identifying the causative organism in endocarditis; drawn in multiple sets from different sites.
  • Echocardiography: Transthoracic or transesophageal ultrasound to visualize valve vegetations, chamber size, and fluid collections.
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): Detects conduction blocks in myocarditis or diffuse ST-segment elevations in pericarditis.
  • Laboratory markers: Elevated white blood cell count, ESR, and C-reactive protein indicate systemic inflammation.

Medical and Pharmacological Management of Cardiac Infections

Medical treatment focuses on eliminating the infectious agent, reducing inflammation, and supporting cardiac output. For infective endocarditis, prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy—often lasting 4 to 6 weeks—is mandatory to penetrate dense vegetations. Nurses manage central venous access lines carefully and monitor for adverse drug reactions.

For myocarditis and pericarditis, treatment often involves non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or colchicine to suppress inflammation. Corticosteroids are reserved for specific autoimmune or severe viral etiologies. If heart failure signs emerge, standard guideline-directed medical therapy including ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers is introduced cautiously under close hemodynamic monitoring.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

Surgery becomes necessary when medical management fails to control cardiac infections or when structural damage threatens life. Surgical indications include severe heart failure due to acute valve regurgitation, persistent infection despite appropriate antibiotics, large mobile vegetations carrying a high embolic risk, and cardiac tamponade requiring pericardial drainage.

Surgical procedures involve valve repair or replacement (using mechanical or tissue prostheses) and surgical drainage of the pericardial space via pericardiocentesis or a pericardial window. Post-operative nursing care requires intensive monitoring of hemodynamic parameters, management of chest tubes, strict aseptic technique for wound care, and early mobilization to prevent thromboembolic events.

Complications and Patient Education for Prevention in Nigerian Clinical Contexts

Cardiac infections carry severe risks, including permanent heart failure, stroke from septic emboli, renal impairment due to immune-complex glomerulonephritis, and recurrent infections. In the Nigerian healthcare context, late presentation due to socioeconomic factors and traditional medicine use often worsens outcomes.

Nurses play a vital role in patient education focused on secondary prevention. Patients with structural heart disease must understand the importance of antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental or surgical procedures, though guidelines now target only the highest-risk groups. Education must also emphasize completing full courses of prescribed medications, maintaining meticulous personal hygiene, and seeking early medical evaluation for unexplained persistent fevers.
