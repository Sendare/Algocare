Coronary Artery Disease

Introduction and Definition of Coronary Artery Disease

Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) is a condition where the major blood vessels supplying the heart muscle become damaged or diseased. The primary culprit is usually the buildup of cholesterol-containing plaques in these coronary arteries, a process that reduces blood flow and deprives the myocardium of vital oxygen and nutrients. When the heart muscle fails to receive enough oxygenated blood, patients experience pain or even tissue death. In Nigerian clinical practice, healthcare providers see CAD increasingly alongside traditional infectious diseases due to lifestyle transitions in urban areas. Understanding this condition is foundational for nurses managing adult patients with cardiovascular complaints.

Pathophysiology and Atherosclerosis Development

Atherosclerosis begins with subtle injury to the innermost layer of the arterial wall, the endothelium. Once injured, low-density lipoproteins (LDL) seep into the vessel wall and undergo oxidation. White blood cells, specifically monocytes, migrate into the tissue and transform into macrophages, gobbling up the oxidized cholesterol to become foam cells. These foam cells accumulate, forming a fatty streak visible in early vascular disease. Over time, smooth muscle cells proliferate, creating a fibrous cap over the lipid core. A stable plaque remains enclosed, but an unstable plaque can rupture, exposing thrombogenic material to the bloodstream and triggering an acute clot.

Risk Factors and Etiology

  • Non-modifiable risk factors: Age, biological sex, and family history of premature heart disease cannot be changed.
  • Modifiable risk factors: Hypertension, cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, obesity, and physical inactivity.
  • Emerging factors: Chronic systemic inflammation, elevated C-reactive protein, and high stress levels contribute significantly to endothelial damage.

Clinical Manifestations: Angina Pectoris and Acute Coronary Syndromes

Clinical presentations range from silent ischemia to sudden cardiac death. Stable angina manifests as predictable chest discomfort triggered by exertion and relieved by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin. Conversely, unstable angina occurs unpredictably at rest and signals an unstable plaque. Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI) involves prolonged ischemia resulting in irreversible myocardial necrosis. Patients typically describe a crushing pressure radiating to the left arm, jaw, or neck, accompanied by diaphoresis, nausea, and shortness of breath.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Confirming coronary artery disease requires a combination of electrical, biochemical, and imaging assessments. A 12-lead Electrocardiogram (ECG) detects ischemic changes such as ST-segment depression or elevation, and T-wave inversion. Cardiac biomarker blood tests, particularly troponin T and I, confirm myocardial necrosis during acute events. Stress testing evaluates heart function under physical or pharmacological workload. Coronary angiography remains the gold standard imaging test, injecting radiopaque contrast dye to visualize exact luminal narrowing.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy aims to reduce myocardial oxygen demand, increase supply, and prevent clot formation. Antiplatelet agents like aspirin prevent platelet aggregation on vulnerable plaques. Statins lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and stabilize plaque caps. Beta-blockers decrease heart rate and myocardial contractility, reducing oxygen consumption. Nitrates cause systemic vasodilation, decreasing preload and relieving angina. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors prevent ventricular remodeling after ischemic insults.

Surgical Interventions: PCI and CABG

When medical management fails or anatomical blockages are severe, revascularization is necessary. Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI) involves threading a catheter with a balloon through peripheral vessels to flatten the plaque, frequently followed by stent deployment to keep the vessel open. Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG) is an open-heart surgical procedure where harvested saphenous veins or internal mammary arteries are sewn around the blocked coronary segments, establishing an alternate pathway for blood flow.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Care Planning

Nurses play a critical role in continuous monitoring and stabilization. Assessment focuses on vital signs, pain characteristics, respiratory status, and peripheral perfusion. Nurses must monitor for signs of heart failure, such as dyspnea and crackles in lung bases. Care planning includes administering prescribed medications safely, managing acute pain promptly with oxygen and nitrates, and providing structured lifestyle counseling regarding diet, exercise, and smoking cessation before discharge.

Complications and Cardiac Rehabilitation

Long-term management extends beyond acute care to prevent recurrent events and restore quality of life. Common complications include heart failure, cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, and ventricular aneurysm. Cardiac rehabilitation is a medically supervised program designed to optimize physical, psychological, and social functioning. It encompasses progressive exercise training, dietary counseling, psychosocial support, and education on risk factor modification, empowering patients to manage their chronic condition effectively.
