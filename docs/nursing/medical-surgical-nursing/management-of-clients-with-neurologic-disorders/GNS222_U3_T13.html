Trauma of the Nervous System

Anatomy and Etiology of Nervous System Trauma

Nervous system trauma involves physical damage to the brain, spinal cord, or peripheral nerves. The central nervous system is heavily protected by the bony structures of the skull and vertebral column, alongside tough meningeal layers and cerebrospinal fluid. When external kinetic energy exceeds the protective capacity of these structures, neural tissue sustains injury.

  • Road traffic accidents: The leading cause of severe neurotrauma in Nigeria, frequently involving commercial motorcycles and buses without proper passenger restraints.
  • Falls: Common among the elderly population at home and construction workers on elevated sites.
  • Violence: Interpersonal altercations involving assaults with blunt or penetrating objects.
  • Sports injuries: Contact sports and diving into shallow water resulting in acute cervical spine impact.

Pathophysiology of Traumatic Brain and Spinal Cord Injuries

Neural tissue damage occurs in two distinct phases during a traumatic event. The primary injury happens at the exact moment of impact through shearing, tearing, or compression of axons and blood vessels. This mechanical disruption causes immediate, often irreversible cellular destruction.

Following the primary insult, a complex cascade of secondary injury mechanisms unfolds over hours and days. Ischemia develops due to localized vascular compromise and systemic hypotension. Intracellular calcium overload triggers destructive enzymatic activity, leading to free radical generation and lipid peroxidation. Edema accumulates rapidly within the rigid cranial vault or spinal canal, escalating tissue pressure and causing further ischemic damage.

Classification and Types of Nervous System Injuries

Injuries to the nervous system present in diverse forms depending on the force and location of the impact. Concussions involve a temporary loss of neurological function without structural damage visible on standard imaging. Contusions represent actual bruising of the brain or spinal parenchyma accompanied by localized hemorrhage.

  • Epidural hematoma: Arterial bleeding accumulating between the skull and dura mater, characterized by a brief lucid interval followed by rapid deterioration.
  • Subdural hematoma: Venous bleeding beneath the dura mater, presenting with slower, progressive neurological decline.
  • Complete spinal transection: Total loss of motor and sensory function below the level of the lesion.
  • Incomplete spinal injury: Preserved sensory or motor function below the primary site of damage.

Neurological Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Rapid, systematic assessment is vital to determine the severity of nervous system trauma and guide urgent interventions. The Glasgow Coma Scale evaluates eye opening, verbal response, and motor activity to grade consciousness levels. Pupillary reflexes offer quick insight into brainstem integrity and potential herniation syndromes.

Diagnostic imaging confirms the exact location and scope of structural pathology. Computed tomography scanning remains the gold standard for acute traumatic brain injury due to its speed in identifying fractures and hemorrhages. Magnetic resonance imaging provides superior detail for soft tissue damage, ligamentous injury, and spinal cord compression when the patient is clinically stable.

Emergency Medical and Surgical Management

Initial emergency management prioritizes stabilization using the airway, breathing, and circulation protocol while maintaining strict cervical spine immobilization. Hypoxia and hypotension severely worsen secondary brain injury and must be aggressively corrected. Intravenous fluids and vasopressors maintain adequate cerebral perfusion pressure.

Surgical intervention is required when space-occupying lesions threaten brainstem function. Decompressive craniectomy relieves life-threatening intracranial pressure by removing a portion of the skull. Spinal stabilization surgeries utilize hardware fixation and fusion to decompress neural elements and restore mechanical alignment.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Nursing care focuses on continuous monitoring, preventing complications, and supporting physiological recovery in critically ill trauma patients. Nurses track hourly neurological changes using standardized scoring tools to detect early signs of intracranial pressure spikes or neurological deterioration.

Airway clearance and respiratory support are paramount, especially for clients with high cervical injuries who experience diaphragmatic paralysis. Positioning the head at thirty degrees promotes venous drainage from the cranial vault. Meticulous skin care, passive range of motion exercises, and regular turning prevent pressure ulcers and contractures in immobile individuals.

Potential Complications and Long-Term Rehabilitation

Survivors of severe nervous system trauma face profound physical, cognitive, and psychosocial challenges. Autonomic dysreflexia is a life-threatening emergency in individuals with spinal cord injuries above the sixth thoracic vertebra, triggered by noxious stimuli such as a distended bladder or bowel impaction, leading to dangerous hypertension.

Long-term rehabilitation requires a multidisciplinary team approach involving physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists. The goal is to maximize residual function, promote independence, and adapt to permanent disabilities. Psychological support is crucial for both clients and their families as they navigate chronic lifestyle adjustments.
