Level of Consciousness Assessment

Introduction and Physiology of Consciousness

Consciousness relies on two main components: arousal and awareness. Arousal is the wakefulness state driven by the Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) located in the brainstem. Awareness involves higher-level cognitive functions managed by the cerebral cortex. Damage to either the brainstem or both cerebral hemispheres disrupts this delicate balance, resulting in altered states of consciousness.

Terminology of Altered States

Clinical documentation requires precise vocabulary to describe a client's mental status. Vague terms like "unresponsive" lack the clarity needed for effective handovers.

  • Confusion: Impaired ability to think clearly and rapidly with diminished attention span.
  • Lethargy: Severe drowsiness where the client is sluggish in speech and mental processes but arouses to stimulation.
  • Obtundation: Greater reduction in alertness, sleeping much of the time, and responding slowly to stimuli.
  • Stupor: Little spontaneous movement, remaining unresponsive unless subjected to painful or vigorous stimuli.
  • Coma: Complete failure of arousal where the client cannot be awakened even with maximal noxious stimuli.

The Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) evaluates neurological function across three distinct behavioral domains: eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. The highest possible score is 15, indicating full orientation, while the lowest score is 3, denoting deep coma or brain death.

Alternative Tools: AVPU and FOUR Score

While the GCS remains the gold standard for trauma and intensive care, other scales serve specific clinical settings.

  • AVPU Scale: A rapid screening tool used primarily in emergency triage. Stands for Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive.
  • FOUR Score: Full Outline of UnResponsiveness. Evaluates four components: eye responses, motor movements, brainstem reflexes, and respiration patterns. It provides better detail for intubated clients because it does not rely on verbal output.

Step-by-Step Clinical Assessment Technique

Perform neurological checks in a structured sequence to ensure accuracy and consistency across shifts. Begin with the least invasive stimulus and escalate only if necessary.

  1. Observe: Approach the client and note spontaneous posture, breathing patterns, and eye opening.
  2. Call: Speak the client's name in a normal tone of voice. If there is no reaction, repeat while gently touching the shoulder.
  3. Stimulate: If the client remains unresponsive, apply a safe noxious stimulus such as a trapezius squeeze or a supraorbital pressure.
  4. Test Reflexes: Check pupillary light reflex and corneal reflexes to assess brainstem integrity.

Interpreting and Documenting Findings

Accurate neurological documentation requires recording baseline data alongside serial assessments. Sudden drops in the GCS score by two or more points indicate acute intracranial changes, such as hemorrhage or edema, requiring immediate medical notification. Always note asymmetry in limb movement or pupil response, as localized neurological deficits point toward focal brain lesions rather than systemic causes.

Nursing Interventions for Altered Consciousness

Managing clients with depressed levels of consciousness focuses on protecting vital body functions. Maintain a patent airway by positioning the client on their side to prevent tongue drop and aspiration of oral secretions. Perform frequent position changes every two hours to prevent pressure injuries. Lubricate the eyes regularly with artificial tears if spontaneous blinking is absent to prevent corneal ulceration.
