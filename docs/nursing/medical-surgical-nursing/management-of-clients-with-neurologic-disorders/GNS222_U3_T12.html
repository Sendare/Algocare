Cerebrovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cerebrovascular Disorders and Cerebral Circulation

Cerebrovascular disorders encompass a group of conditions affecting the blood vessels and blood supply to the brain. The brain requires a constant, uninterrupted supply of oxygen and glucose to function. This metabolic demand is met by two main arterial pairs: the internal carotid arteries supplying the anterior circulation and the vertebral arteries merging into the basilar artery supplying the posterior circulation.

When blood flow is disrupted, brain tissue suffers ischemia rapidly. Neurons cannot store glucose or oxygen, leading to cellular energy failure within minutes. Understanding this vascular anatomy helps nurses pinpoint which area of the brain is damaged based on clinical signs.

Pathophysiology and Classification: Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic Strokes

Strokes are broadly categorized into two types: ischemic and hemorrhagic.

  • Ischemic Stroke: Caused by an obstruction within a blood vessel supplying blood to the brain. It accounts for the majority of cases and results from thrombosis or embolism.
  • Hemorrhagic Stroke: Caused by a weakened blood vessel that ruptures and bleeds into the surrounding brain tissue, often linked to chronic hypertension.

The damaged core area of the brain undergoes cell death, surrounded by the penumbra, a zone of minimally perfused, salvageable tissue that nursing and medical interventions aim to protect.

Risk Factors and Etiology in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria and across Sub-Saharan Africa, cerebrovascular disorders present with unique epidemiological trends. Uncontrolled hypertension remains the single greatest modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. Other significant contributors include diabetes mellitus, cardiac diseases such as rheumatic heart disease, heavy alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking.

Clinically, nurses frequently observe stroke affecting a younger demographic in Nigeria compared to Western nations. This trend is driven by poorly managed hypertension, lifestyle changes, and delayed access to screening and primary healthcare facilities.

Clinical Manifestations and Neurological Assessment

Clinical signs of a cerebrovascular accident depend heavily on the specific cerebral artery involved. Nurses use the FAST acronym (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call) for rapid community identification. Comprehensive hospital assessments include evaluating cranial nerves, motor strength, sensory function, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS).

  • Left Hemisphere Stroke: Results in right-sided hemiplegia, language deficits (aphasia), and cautious behavior.
  • Right Hemisphere Stroke: Results in left-sided hemiplegia, spatial-perceptual deficits, and impulsive behavior.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Rapid diagnostic imaging is mandatory to differentiate between ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes before any treatment begins.

  1. Non-contrast Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: The gold standard for initial evaluation because it quickly rules out intracranial hemorrhage.
  2. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Provides superior visualization of early or small ischemic strokes.
  3. Carotid Ultrasound and Echocardiography: Performed later to identify the underlying source of emboli.

Nurses must ensure patient stability during transport and monitor vital signs closely throughout these imaging procedures.

Acute Medical and Surgical Management

Management in the acute phase focuses on saving brain tissue and stabilizing vital organs. For eligible patients presenting within the strict time window, thrombolytic therapy (such as recombinant tissue plasminogen activator) is administered to dissolve clots. For large vessel occlusions, endovascular mechanical thrombectomy may be performed.

Blood pressure management requires extreme care. Hypertensive permissiveness is often allowed in acute ischemic stroke to maintain cerebral perfusion, whereas aggressive blood pressure reduction is vital in hemorrhagic stroke to stop ongoing bleeding.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation Strategies

Once the patient is medically stable, nursing care shifts toward preventing complications and promoting recovery. A thorough swallowing screening must occur before offering oral intake to prevent aspiration pneumonia. Positioning, regular repositioning every two hours, and passive range-of-motion exercises protect vulnerable joints and prevent pressure ulcers.

Rehabilitation involves physical, occupational, and speech therapists working in concert with the nurse. Encouraging independence in activities of daily living rebuilds patient confidence and functional capacity.

Complications and Secondary Prevention

Long-term management focuses on preventing a recurrent stroke and managing debilitating complications. Common secondary complications include deep vein thrombosis, contractures, depression, and post-stroke seizures.

Secondary prevention relies heavily on lifestyle modifications and strict pharmacological adherence: antiplatelet agents (like aspirin) or anticoagulants for ischemic strokes, strict blood pressure control, and diabetic management. Comprehensive patient and family education ensures lifestyle changes are sustained after discharge.
