Review of Related Anatomy and Physiology of the Nervous System

Introduction to the Nervous System and Its General Functions

The nervous system serves as the primary master control and communication network of the body. Working alongside the endocrine system, it maintains homeostasis by detecting changes, processing information, and orchestrating responses.

  • Sensory input: Receptors monitor internal and external environments, gathering data about temperature, pressure, and chemical changes.
  • Integration: The central nervous system processes this sensory information, analyzes it, and determines the appropriate response.
  • Motor output: Signals travel out from the control centers to effector organs—muscles and glands—to trigger physical movement or secretion.

Division of labor within this network separates structures into the central and peripheral components, each specialized for specific operational speeds and pathways.

Central Nervous System: Brain Anatomy and Functional Areas

The brain acts as the command center, divided into major regions with distinct responsibilities:

  • Cerebrum: The largest part, divided into left and right hemispheres, managing higher mental functions, sensory perception, and voluntary movement.
  • Cerebellum: Positioned inferior to the occipital lobes, coordinating smooth skeletal muscle movements, balance, and posture.
  • Brainstem: Comprising the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata, regulating vital autonomic functions like respiration, heart rate, and blood pressure.
  • Diencephalon: Containing the thalamus and hypothalamus, serving as a relay station for sensory input and a control center for endocrine and autonomic processes.

Spinal Cord Structure and Reflex Pathways

The spinal cord functions as a vital two-way conduction pathway between the brain and the body, while also serving as a major integration center for spinal reflexes. Protected by the vertebral column, its internal gray matter forms an H-shape containing cell bodies, while the surrounding white matter contains myelinated axon tracts ascending to the brain or descending to the body.

A reflex arc bypasses conscious brain processing to yield an immediate protective response. Sensory input travels through a dorsal root into the cord, synapses directly or via an interneuron, and sends a motor command out through the ventral root to a muscle.

Peripheral Nervous System: Somatic and Autonomic Divisions

The peripheral nervous system connects the central nervous system to limbs and organs. It splits functionally into somatic and autonomic branches.

The somatic nervous system governs conscious, voluntary actions by sending motor signals from the central nervous system to skeletal muscles. In contrast, the autonomic nervous system operates involuntarily, regulating visceral functions of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands.

The autonomic system further divides into the sympathetic division—which drives the fight-or-flight response during stress—and the parasympathetic division, which promotes rest-and-digest maintenance activities during calm periods.

Cellular Organization: Neurons and Neuroglial Cells

Nervous tissue consists of two principal cell types: neurons and neuroglia. Neurons are the structural units of the nervous system specialized for transmitting electrical signals. Each typically features a cell body, branching dendrites that receive inputs, and a single axon that transmits signals away.

Neuroglial cells, or glia, outnumber neurons and provide vital support, insulation, and nourishment. In the central nervous system, astrocytes maintain the blood-brain barrier, oligodendrocytes form myelin sheaths, microglia act as phagocytes, and ependymal cells line fluid-filled cavities. In the periphery, Schwann cells form myelin, and satellite cells cushion cell bodies.

Neurotransmission and Action Potential Generation

Communication within the nervous system relies on electrochemical impulses. At rest, a neuron maintains a negative electrical charge inside compared to the outside, known as the resting membrane potential.

When a stimulus reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open, causing a rapid influx of positive sodium ions that depolarizes the membrane. This spike is an action potential. Repolarization follows as potassium channels open and potassium ions rush out.

Once the action potential reaches the axon terminal, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These chemical messengers bind to receptors on the next cell, continuing or stopping the signal.

Protective Structures: Meninges, Ventricles, and Cerebrospinal Fluid

Delicate central nervous system tissue requires robust physical protection. Three connective tissue membranes called meninges encase the brain and spinal cord:

  1. Dura mater: The tough, fibrous outermost layer adhering closely to the skull and vertebral canal.
  2. Arachnoid mater: The web-like middle layer enclosing the subarachnoid space.
  3. Pia mater: The delicate innermost layer clinging directly to the surface of the brain and spinal cord.

Cerebrospinal fluid circulates through the subarachnoid space and internal brain ventricles, cushioning neural structures, supplying nutrients, and removing metabolic waste.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Assessment of Neurological Function

Nurses rely on baseline neurological assessments to detect subtle changes in patient status. Evaluating orientation, cranial nerve integrity, motor strength, and sensory response helps identify early signs of increased intracranial pressure or localized brain injury.

The Glasgow Coma Scale provides a standardized tool to monitor level of consciousness by scoring eye opening, verbal response, and motor performance. Any unexplained drop in score signals worsening cerebral function, requiring immediate clinical intervention and medical notification.
