Imaging Procedures

Introduction to Neurological Imaging Procedures

Neurological imaging has fundamentally transformed how clinicians diagnose and manage disorders of the central and peripheral nervous systems. Before these technologies existed, physicians relied heavily on clinical examination and invasive exploratory procedures to locate brain tumors, strokes, and structural anomalies. Modern diagnostic imaging allows non-invasive visualization of brain parenchyma, ventricular systems, and cerebral vasculature with high precision. Nurses must understand the foundational principles of these modalities to provide safe patient care, offer accurate pre-test education, and recognize potential contraindications before a patient is sent to the radiology department.

Skull and Spine Radiography

Plain radiography of the skull and vertebral column remains a quick, accessible baseline investigation, particularly in busy emergency settings across Nigeria.

  • Skull X-rays: Used primarily to detect traumatic bone fractures, erosions of the sella turcica, or calcified intracranial lesions.
  • Spine X-rays: Evaluates vertebral alignment, disc space narrowing, fractures, and spinal stability after trauma.

While computed tomography has largely replaced detailed skull radiography for acute head trauma, plain films are still valuable when advanced technology is unavailable or for rapid orthopedic spinal assessment.

Computed Tomography (CT) Scan in Neurology

A computed tomography scan combines a series of X-ray images taken from different angles to create cross-sectional views of the brain and spinal cord. In acute stroke management, a non-contrast CT is the gold standard initial test to quickly differentiate between ischemic stroke and acute intracranial hemorrhage before any thrombolytic therapy is considered.

Iodinated contrast media is frequently administered intravenously to enhance vascular structures or breakdown in the blood-brain barrier caused by tumors or abscesses. Nurses must check renal function laboratories, particularly serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, before contrast administration to prevent contrast-induced nephropathy.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Advanced Techniques

Magnetic resonance imaging uses powerful magnets and radiofrequency waves to generate remarkably detailed pictures of soft neurological tissues without ionizing radiation. It excels at identifying early ischemic strokes, demyelinating plaques in multiple sclerosis, and posterior fossa tumors.

  • Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI): Detects acute cellular injury within minutes of a stroke.
  • Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA): Visualizes blood vessels without necessarily requiring invasive catheter insertion.

Safety is paramount because the magnetic field is permanently active. Ferromagnetic metal objects, such as pacemakers, aneurysm clips, and certain metallic foreign bodies, can become lethal projectiles or malfunction inside the scanner room.

Cerebral Angiography and Vascular Imaging

Cerebral angiography is an invasive fluoroscopic procedure where a catheter is threaded from the femoral or radial artery up into the cerebral circulation, and radiopaque contrast is injected. It remains the diagnostic gold standard for mapping complex cerebrovascular anatomy, aneurysms, and arteriovenous malformations.

Following the procedure, the nurse maintains strict bed rest with the affected limb immobilized to prevent bleeding or hematoma formation at the puncture site. Frequent neurovascular checks—evaluating pedal or radial pulses, skin color, temperature, and capillary refill—are essential nursing responsibilities during the recovery period.

Functional Neuroimaging: PET and SPECT Scans

While structural imaging shows the anatomy of the brain, functional neuroimaging measures metabolic activity, blood flow, and neurochemical receptor binding.

  • Positron Emission Tomography (PET): Uses radiotracers that emit positrons to evaluate glucose metabolism in conditions like epilepsy, dementia, and brain tumors.
  • Single-Hemphoton Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT): Measures regional cerebral blood flow using gamma-emitting tracers, often utilized in seizure localization and ischemic evaluations.

Patients must understand that these scans involve radioactive tracers, though doses are typically low. Nurses instruct clients to avoid caffeine, alcohol, or sedatives before certain functional scans depending on institutional protocol.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive pre-procedure preparation minimizes complications and reduces client anxiety. The nurse must verify informed consent, confirm baseline neurological status, and screen thoroughly for allergies to iodine, shellfish, or latex if contrast is planned.

All metallic accessories, jewelry, hairpins, and hearing aids must be removed before entering imaging suites. Evaluating claustrophobia is also vital; patients undergoing lengthy MRI scans may require mild anxiolytic medication prescribed by the physician to successfully complete the imaging session.

Intra-Procedure Care and Client Safety

During the imaging procedure, maintaining client safety and immobility is critical for diagnostic success. Movement during CT or MRI scans causes motion artifacts that blur images and render them clinically useless.

Nurses working inside imaging departments must monitor vital signs, especially during procedures requiring sedation. For clients unable to follow commands, soft restraints or positioning aids may be necessary. Communication via intercom systems allows ongoing reassurance for anxious patients trapped inside narrow scanner bores.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Post-procedure care focuses on monitoring for delayed complications and assisting the body in eliminating contrast agents. Nurses encourage high fluid intake orally, provided the client has a stable gag reflex and swallowing capacity, to flush intravenous contrast out through the renal system.

Monitoring vital signs, neurological status, and puncture sites for bleeding remains standard protocol. If a patient received general anesthesia or heavy sedation, they must remain in a designated recovery area until fully awake, oriented, and vital signs return to baseline levels.
