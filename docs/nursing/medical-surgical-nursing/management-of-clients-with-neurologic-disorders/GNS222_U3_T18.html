Peripheral Nerve Disorders

Introduction to Peripheral Nerve Disorders

Peripheral nerve disorders, collectively known as peripheral neuropathies, involve damage or disease affecting the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. These disorders disrupt communication between the central nervous system and the rest of the body, leading to sensory deficits, muscle weakness, and autonomic dysfunction. In clinical practice across Nigeria, neuropathies frequently stem from systemic conditions like diabetes mellitus, infectious diseases such as leprosy or HIV, and nutritional deficiencies common in resource-limited settings. Understanding these conditions helps nurses identify early signs of nerve impairment before irreversible damage occurs.

Anatomy and Physiology of Peripheral Nerves

A peripheral nerve is a complex cordlike structure made up of bundles of nerve fibers called fascicles. Each individual nerve fiber consists of an axon surrounded by supporting Schwann cells. Many smaller axons are unmyelinated, while larger ones are wrapped in a protective myelin sheath that speeds up electrical impulse transmission. Surrounding these components are distinct connective tissue layers:

  • Epineurium: The outermost dense fibrous sheath enclosing the entire nerve trunk.
  • Perineurium: A protective sleeve surrounding each individual fascicle, maintaining the blood-nerve barrier.
  • Endoneurium: Loose connective tissue enclosing individual nerve fibers and their Schwann cells.

Classification and Etiology of Neuropathies

Peripheral neuropathies can be classified based on their distribution (mononeuropathy, polyneuropathy, or mononeuropathy multiplex) or their underlying cause. Systemic diseases, toxic exposures, and physical trauma are major contributors.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Nerve Injury

Nerve injuries are traditionally classified by severity into three main categories described by Sir Herbert Seddon: neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis.

  1. Neurapraxia: A mild temporary conduction block caused by local ischemia or mild compression. The axon remains intact, and recovery is complete within days or weeks.
  2. Axonotmesis: Injury where the axon is damaged, but the surrounding connective tissue sheaths remain intact. Wallerian degeneration occurs distal to the injury site, followed by slow axonal regeneration.
  3. Neurotmesis: Complete transection of both the axon and all supporting connective tissue structures. Spontaneous recovery is rare, and surgical repair is usually required.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Patients with peripheral nerve disorders present with a mix of sensory, motor, and autonomic symptoms depending on which nerve fibers are damaged. Sensory symptoms often start with a "stocking-glove" distribution, manifesting as numbness, tingling, paresthesia, or burning neuropathic pain. Motor involvement presents as muscle weakness, fasciculations, hyporeflexia, and eventual muscle wasting. Autonomic signs include orthostatic hypotension, anhidrosis, or bowel and bladder dysfunction. Nurses must perform thorough neurological assessments, checking light touch, pinprick sensation, and deep tendon reflexes.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Testing

Confirming a peripheral nerve disorder requires a combination of clinical history and specialized diagnostic tests. Nerve conduction studies measure the speed and strength of electrical signals as they travel through a nerve, helping differentiate between demyelinating and axonal injuries. Electromyography evaluates the electrical activity of muscles at rest and during contraction, revealing signs of denervation. Blood tests are also ordered to screen for underlying systemic causes, including fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, vitamin B12 levels, and infectious markers.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management of peripheral neuropathies focuses on treating the underlying cause, controlling symptoms, and preventing further nerve injury. For diabetic neuropathy, tight glycemic control is paramount. Pain management often requires adjunct medications because standard analgesics like paracetamol are frequently ineffective for neuropathic pain. Anticonvulsants such as gabapentin or pregabalin and tricyclic antidepressants like amitriptyline are standard first-line drugs used to calm erratic nerve firing. Topical treatments like capsaicin cream may also provide localized relief for painful areas.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation Strategies

Nurses play a vital role in preventing complications and promoting functional independence in patients with peripheral nerve disorders. Because sensory loss impairs the perception of pain, pressure, and temperature, patients are at high risk for skin breakdown and burns. Nursing interventions must focus on daily foot inspections, wearing properly fitted footwear, and avoiding prolonged pressure on vulnerable joints. Physical therapy collaboration is essential for maintaining joint mobility, preventing contractures, and implementing safe ambulation exercises.

Complications and Long-term Prognosis

Chronic peripheral nerve disorders can lead to severe long-term complications if left unmanaged. Chronic foot ulcers, secondary bone infections like osteomyelitis, and Charcot joint deformities often result from repetitive trauma on insensate limbs, occasionally necessitating amputation. Muscle atrophy and contractures further limit mobility and independence. The overall prognosis depends heavily on early diagnosis, aggressive control of the primary etiology, and strict adherence to protective self-care measures.
