Pathophysiology of Neurologic Disorders

Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms of Neuronal Injury

Neurons have a high metabolic rate and depend entirely on a continuous supply of oxygen and glucose. When this supply fails, a destructive cascade begins at the cellular level.

  • Excitotoxicity: Ischemia triggers excessive release of glutamate into the extracellular space. This overstimulates postsynaptic receptors, allowing massive amounts of calcium ions to flood into the neuron.
  • Calcium Overload: Intracellular calcium accumulation activates destructive enzymes including phospholipases, proteases, and endonucleases that actively digest the cell's own structural proteins and DNA.
  • Oxidative Stress: Damaged mitochondria produce high levels of reactive oxygen species, leading to lipid peroxidation and structural breakdown of the neuronal membrane.

A common clinical misconception is that neurons die immediately upon oxygen deprivation; rather, many undergo delayed cell death via apoptosis hours after the initial insult.

Mechanisms of Increased Intracranial Pressure

The skull is a rigid box containing three non-compressible volumes: brain tissue (80%), cerebrospinal fluid (10%), and blood (10%). The Monro-Kellie doctrine states that an increase in any one of these components must be offset by a decrease in the others to maintain normal intracranial pressure (ICP).

When compensatory mechanisms fail, ICP rises dangerously, threatening cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP). CPP is calculated as mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. As ICP approaches mean arterial pressure, blood flow to the brain ceases, leading to widespread ischemia, brain herniation, and irreversible neurological damage.

Pathophysiology of Cerebrovascular Disorders

Cerebrovascular accidents disrupt blood flow to localized regions of the brain. Ischemic strokes occur due to thrombosis or embolism, cutting off nutrients and triggering the ischemic penumbra—a zone of marginally perfused tissue surrounding the necrotic core that nurses strive to salvage.

Hemorrhagic strokes result from vessel rupture, causing direct mechanical compression of adjacent brain parenchyma from expanding hematomas, alongside secondary injury from toxic breakdown products of blood. Both stroke types ultimately cause cerebral edema, further escalating intracranial pressure.

Pathophysiology of Seizure Disorders

Seizures arise from a temporary imbalance between cerebral excitation and inhibition. Normally, gamma-aminobutyric acid acts as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, while glutamate drives excitation.

In epilepsy, a group of hyperexcitable neurons known as the epileptogenic focus fires abnormally synchronized high-frequency action potentials. If local inhibitory mechanisms fail, this electrical storm recruits surrounding healthy neurons, propagating the seizure across neural networks. Prolonged seizure activity, or status epilepticus, causes profound metabolic exhaustion, hyperthermia, and neuronal destruction due to sustained energy demand exceeding supply.

Degenerative Neurological Diseases and Proteinopathies

Neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease share a common underlying mechanism: protein misfolding and aggregation. These proteinopathies involve host proteins adopting abnormal three-dimensional shapes that resist normal cellular degradation.

  1. Alzheimer's Disease: Accumulation of extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau tangles disrupts synaptic communication and causes neuronal death.
  2. Parkinson's Disease: Misfolded alpha-synuclein forms intracellular inclusions called Lewy bodies, specifically destroying dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra.

These aggregates spread through neural networks via prion-like mechanisms, leading to progressive, region-specific functional decline.

Demyelinating and Inflammatory Conditions

Multiple sclerosis serves as the prototype for central nervous system demyelinating disorders. In this condition, autoreactive T-lymphocytes cross the blood-brain barrier and mistakenly attack myelin sheath components.

Inflammatory cell infiltration causes patches of demyelination, known as plaques, primarily in the white matter of the brain and spinal cord. The loss of myelin strips away the insulation required for rapid saltatory conduction, slowing or completely blocking electrical impulse propagation. Although oligodendrocytes attempt remyelination early in the disease, chronic inflammation eventually leads to axonal transection and permanent neurological deficit.

Nursing Implications in Monitoring Neurological Deterioration

Early identification of neurological decline allows for timely interventions that can prevent permanent brain injury. Nurses rely on structured assessment tools to detect subtle shifts in patient status before catastrophic changes occur.

  • Level of Consciousness: The earliest and most sensitive indicator of decreasing cerebral function is a change in orientation or alertness.
  • Pupillary Response: As intracranial pressure rises, compression of the third cranial nerve leads to sluggish or fixed, dilated pupils.
  • Cushing's Triad: A late, ominous sign of impending brain herniation characterized by widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations.

Waiting for Cushing's triad to appear before acting delays care dangerously, as it indicates the brainstem is already failing.
