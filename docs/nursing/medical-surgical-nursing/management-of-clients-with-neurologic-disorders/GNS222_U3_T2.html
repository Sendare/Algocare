Assessment of Neurological Function

Introduction to Neurological Assessment

A neurological assessment evaluates the integrity of the central and peripheral nervous systems. Nurses perform this examination during admission, routine shift changes, or whenever a patient shows signs of neurological decline. Establishing a baseline is the primary goal because early detection of subtle changes prevents catastrophic complications like brain herniation or irreversible ischemia. Systematic progression through distinct segments ensures no critical deficit is overlooked.

Health History and Symptom Analysis

Gathering a targeted health history clarifies current neurological complaints and contextualizes physical findings. Common chief complaints include headaches, dizziness, weakness, numbness, and sudden changes in vision or speech.

  • Onset: Determine if symptoms started abruptly, like in a stroke, or developed gradually over time.
  • Characteristics: Ask the patient to describe the exact sensation, such as sharp pain or a dull ache.
  • Preceding events: Identify any head trauma, recent infections, or environmental exposures.
  • Medication history: Check for adherence to anticonvulsants, antihypertensives, or anticoagulants.

Assessment of Mental Status and Level of Consciousness

Level of consciousness is the most sensitive indicator of neurological function. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) evaluates eye opening, verbal response, and motor response, yielding a score between 3 and 15. Mental status testing further evaluates orientation to person, place, and time, alongside memory and abstract thinking.

Evaluation of Cranial Nerves

Testing the twelve cranial nerves localizes specific brainstem or peripheral nerve lesions. Each nerve requires a distinct bedside test.

  • CN I (Olfactory): Test patency and identify familiar scents with eyes closed.
  • CN II (Optic): Assess visual acuity using a Snellen chart and peripheral vision.
  • CN III, IV, VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens): Check pupillary reaction and extraocular eye movements.
  • CN V (Trigeminal): Test facial sensation to light touch and motor strength of chewing muscles.
  • CN VII (Facial): Observe symmetry during smiling, frowning, and puffing cheeks.
  • CN VIII (Vestibulocochlear): Perform the whisper test for hearing acuity.
  • CN IX, X (Glossopharyngeal, Vagus): Assess the gag reflex and symmetrical palate elevation.
  • CN XI (Accessory): Test shoulder shrug and head rotation against resistance.
  • CN XII (Hypoglossal): Instruct the patient to stick out the tongue and check for midline deviation.

Motor Function and Coordination Assessment

Evaluating motor function involves inspecting muscle bulk, testing tone, and grading strength on a standard scale from 0 (no movement) to 5 (normal power against full resistance). Involuntary movements like tremors or fasciculations must be documented.

Coordination relies on cerebellar function. Tests include finger-to-nose movements, rapid alternating hand movements, and heel-to-shin sliding. The Romberg test assesses proprioception and balance; standing with feet together and eyes closed, a loss of balance indicates a positive sign.

Sensory Function Examination

Sensory pathways convey tactile, pain, temperature, and proprioceptive inputs to the cerebral cortex. The nurse tests superficial pain using a safe, disposable pin, and light touch using cotton wool, always comparing symmetrical body areas. Cortical sensory functions include stereognosis, the ability to identify a familiar object placed in the hand by touch alone, and graphesthesia, recognizing numbers traced on the palm.

Reflex Testing and Grading

Reflex arcs evaluate the communication between peripheral nerves and the spinal cord without requiring higher cerebral processing. Deep tendon reflexes—such as biceps, triceps, patellar, and achilles—are graded on a 0 to 4 scale, where 2+ is normal. Hyperreflexia (3+ or 4+) points to upper motor neuron lesions, while hyporeflexia (0 or 1+) indicates lower motor neuron pathology. The plantar response checks for the Babinski sign; dorsiflexion of the big toe in adults signifies central nervous system disease.

Diagnostic Tests and Advanced Neurological Monitoring

Bedside assessments are frequently supplemented by diagnostic imaging and invasive monitoring. Computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) visualize structural abnormalities, hemorrhages, or ischemic infarcts. Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring devices measure cerebral perfusion dynamics in critical care units. Nurses monitor ICP waveforms to ensure adequate cerebral perfusion pressure, preventing secondary brain injury.

Nursing Responsibilities and Documentation in Neurological Care

Clear, objective documentation ensures continuity of care across multidisciplinary teams. Nurses must record exact numerical scores, precise descriptive details, and the time of every assessment rather than vague terms like 'alert' or 'dull'. Prompt reporting of deteriorating trends to the medical team is a core nursing responsibility. Maintaining patient safety through fall precautions and seizure precautions remains paramount throughout the monitoring period.
