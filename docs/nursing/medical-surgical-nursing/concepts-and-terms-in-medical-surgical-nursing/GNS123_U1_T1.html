Concepts in Medical-Surgical Nursing

Introduction and Scope of Medical-Surgical Nursing

Medical-surgical nursing forms the backbone of adult healthcare in hospital settings. Nurses in this specialty care for adult patients with diverse, complex, and fluctuating health conditions, ranging from acute trauma to chronic disease exacerbations. Clinical competence requires integrating deep physiological knowledge with rapid assessment skills. You must recognize subtle clinical changes before they escalate into life-threatening emergencies. The practice environment spans general wards, specialized surgical units, and ambulatory care clinics across Nigeria.

The Nursing Process in Clinical Practice

The nursing process provides a systematic framework for clinical decision-making. It consists of five distinct phases: assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation.

  • Assessment: Gathering subjective and objective patient data through physical examination, history taking, and review of diagnostic results.
  • Diagnosis: Identifying actual or potential health problems based on analyzed data.
  • Planning: Setting measurable patient outcomes and selecting appropriate nursing interventions.
  • Implementation: Executing the planned interventions safely and documenting care accurately.
  • Evaluation: Determining whether patient outcomes have been met and modifying the plan if necessary.

Fluid, Electrolyte, and Acid-Base Regulation

Maintaining internal equilibrium is vital for cellular survival. Fluid imbalances manifest as extracellular volume deficits, such as dehydration from severe gastroenteritis, or volume excesses leading to pulmonary congestion. Electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and calcium regulate neuromuscular activity and fluid shifts. Acid-base balance relies on intricate compensatory mechanisms between the lungs and kidneys. Respiratory disorders alter carbon dioxide retention, while metabolic issues affect bicarbonate levels, shifting blood pH outside the normal range of 7.35 to 7.45.

Inflammation, Immunity, and Wound Healing

Tissue injury triggers an immediate inflammatory cascade designed to protect and repair the host. Acute inflammation brings neutrophils and macrophages to the site of trauma, causing localized heat, swelling, redness, and pain. Wound healing progresses through distinct phases: hemostasis and inflammation, proliferation with granulation tissue formation, and tissue remodeling. Chronic conditions like diabetes mellitus impair this sequence, predisposing patients to delayed wound closure and severe secondary infections.

Pain Assessment and Clinical Management

Pain is widely recognized as the fifth vital sign and demands regular, objective evaluation. Because pain is subjective, self-reporting using validated tools like numerical rating scales or visual analog scales is the gold standard. Pharmacological management utilizes the WHO analgesic ladder, moving from non-opioids like paracetamol to weak and strong opioids. Non-pharmacological interventions such as positioning, thermotherapy, and distraction complement drug therapy to enhance patient comfort and promote healing.

Perioperative Nursing Care

Perioperative nursing encompasses care delivered across three distinct phases: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative. Preoperative care focuses on baseline assessments, informed consent verification, and psychological preparation. Intraoperative care centers on patient safety, sterile technique maintenance, and physiological monitoring during anesthesia. Postoperative care in the post-anesthesia care unit involves monitoring for airway patency, bleeding, hemodynamic stability, and effective pain control.

Management of Shock and Multiorgan Dysfunction

Shock is a life-threatening clinical syndrome characterized by systemic tissue hypoperfusion and cellular hypoxia. Types include hypovolemic, cardiogenic, septic, and anaphylactic shock. If uncorrected, prolonged shock leads to cellular death, metabolic acidosis, and multiorgan dysfunction syndrome. Nursing management focuses on restoring effective intravascular volume, optimizing cardiac output, supporting oxygen delivery, and administering prescribed vasoactive medications under close hemodynamic monitoring.

Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care improves the quality of life for patients facing life-limiting illnesses by addressing physical, psychosocial, and spiritual distress. End-of-life care shifts the clinical focus entirely from curative treatment to comfort and dignity. Nurses manage distressing terminal symptoms such as dyspnea, pain, nausea, and delirium. Providing compassionate support to grieving family members remains an essential component of comprehensive nursing practice during the dying process.
