Assessment of Critically Ill Patients

Introduction to Critical Care Nursing and Patient Assessment

Critical care nursing demands rapid, systematic, and continuous evaluation of unstable patients. The primary goal in the intensive care unit is to identify life-threatening conditions before they cause irreversible organ failure. Assessment in this environment differs from general ward nursing due to the high frequency of monitoring and the presence of advanced life support technology. Nurses must balance machine data with hands-on physical findings. A structured approach prevents oversight during emergencies when multiple alarming monitors compete for attention.

Initial Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE approach provides a rapid framework to stabilize patients with immediate threats to life.

  • Airway: Check for patency, clear secretions, and ensure the airway is not obstructed by the tongue or foreign bodies.
  • Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, chest expansion, oxygen saturation, and breath sounds.
  • Circulation: Evaluate pulse rate and rhythm, capillary refill time, skin temperature, and blood pressure.
  • Disability: Quickly screen neurological status using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale and check blood glucose.
  • Exposure: Undress the patient briefly to examine for hidden injuries, rashes, or bleeding, while maintaining thermal control.

Comprehensive Secondary Survey and System-Wise Evaluation

Once immediate life threats are managed through the primary survey, a detailed secondary survey begins. This involves a head-to-toe physical examination and review of all body systems. Nurses inspect surgical incisions, check drainage tubes, review diagnostic imaging, and verify laboratory trends. Every invasive line, catheter, and wound dressing is checked for proper placement and signs of infection. This thorough review ensures subtle complications are caught before progressing to organ dysfunction.

Neurological Assessment and Consciousness Scales

Neurological evaluation in critical care tracks changes in brain function and detects rising intracranial pressure. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) evaluates eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A sudden drop in the GCS score warrants immediate medical notification. Nurses also assess pupillary light reflex, limb strength, and symmetry.

Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Cardiovascular assessment goes far beyond taking a routine blood pressure cuff reading. Hemodynamic monitoring tracks perfusion pressure, cardiac output, and fluid status. Nurses observe central venous pressure, arterial line waveforms, and peripheral pulses. Cold, clammy extremities and delayed capillary refill often signal poor tissue perfusion, even if blood pressure appears normal. Vasopressors and inotropes are frequently titrated based on these integrated cardiovascular parameters.

Respiratory Assessment and Airway Management

Respiratory failure is a frequent reason for intensive care admission. Nurses evaluate work of breathing by observing accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, and abnormal respiratory patterns. Auscultation reveals wheezes, crackles, or diminished breath sounds indicating obstruction or fluid overload. Pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases measure oxygenation and ventilation adequacy. For intubated patients, verifying endotracheal tube depth at the lip line and confirming bilateral chest rise prevents unrecognized mainstem intubation.

Renal, Gastrointestinal, and Fluid Balance Monitoring

Maintaining fluid balance is critical to prevent kidney injury and gut ischemia. Hourly urine output measurement via an indwelling catheter serves as the most sensitive indicator of renal perfusion. A drop below 0.5 mL/kg/hour signals potential acute kidney injury. Gastrointestinal assessment involves checking bowel sounds, monitoring abdominal distension, and tracking enteral or parenteral nutrition tolerance. Nurses also measure accurate fluid input and output totals every shift.

Pain, Agitation, and Delirium Assessment in the ICU

Patients in intensive care often experience severe discomfort, anxiety, and confusion due to illness and environment. Pain should be assessed regularly using behavioral scales if the patient is unable to communicate verbally. Sedation depth is measured using tools like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) to avoid oversedation. Delirium screening tools, such as the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU, help detect acute brain dysfunction early so contributing factors can be addressed.

Documentation, Communication, and Continuous Reassessment

Accurate charting and clear handovers complete the patient assessment cycle. Critical care settings change rapidly, making timely documentation vital for legal and clinical safety. Structured handover tools like SBAR ensure vital trends and pending concerns are communicated effectively between shifts. Because patient status fluctuates constantly, assessment is never a one-time task; it is an ongoing cycle of evaluating interventions and responding to new clinical data.
