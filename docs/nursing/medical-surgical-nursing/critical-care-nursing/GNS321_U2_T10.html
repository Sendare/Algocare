Management of Patients on Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction to Mechanical Ventilation and Indications

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy that physically assists or replaces spontaneous breathing. It is indicated when a patient cannot maintain adequate gas exchange independently due to respiratory failure, airway obstruction, severe brain injury, or exhaustion from work of breathing. Ventilators deliver air and oxygen under positive pressure into the patient's lungs through an artificial airway like an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy tube, or occasionally via a tight-fitting mask.

Common clinical scenarios requiring mechanical ventilation include:

  • Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS): Widespread lung inflammation leading to severe hypoxemia.
  • Status Epilepticus or Coma: Altered mental status compromising airway protection and respiratory drive.
  • Neuromuscular Diseases: Conditions like Guillain-Barré syndrome causing respiratory muscle paralysis.
  • Post-operative Recovery: Prolonged recovery from major cardiothoracic or neurosurgical procedures.

Ventilator Modes and Settings

Ventilator modes determine how the machine interacts with the patient's respiratory effort. Understanding these modes allows nurses to monitor patient synchrony and detect distress early.

  • Controlled Mandatory Ventilation (CMV): The ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. Used mostly in heavily sedated or paralyzed patients.
  • Assist-Control (AC): The ventilator delivers a preset volume or pressure whenever the patient initiates a breath, but guarantees a minimum breath rate if the patient is apneic.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): Delivers preset mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's spontaneous breaths, allowing the patient to breathe spontaneously between machine breaths.
  • Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV): Assists spontaneous breaths by delivering a constant pressure during inspiration, reducing the work of breathing through an artificial airway.

Basic ventilator settings prescribed by the physician include FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen), tidal volume (volume of air delivered per breath), respiratory rate, and PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure), which keeps alveoli open at the end of expiration.

Routine Nursing Care and Patient Monitoring

Caring for a mechanically ventilated patient requires continuous vigilance and systematic nursing assessments. The nurse must verify that ventilator alarms are always active and audible; never silence an alarm without investigating the cause.

Vital signs, oxygen saturation, and breathing patterns require frequent checks. The nurse assesses chest symmetry, auscultates breath sounds bilaterally to confirm correct endotracheal tube placement, and monitors arterial blood gas results. Sedation levels must be evaluated using validated scales like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) to prevent accidental extubation or patient distress. Providing meticulous oral care with chlorhexidine every two to four hours significantly reduces the risk of bacterial colonization and ventilator-associated pneumonia.

Airway Management and Endotracheal Suctioning

Maintaining a patent artificial airway is vital for effective ventilation and oxygenation. Endotracheal tubes and tracheostomy tubes bypass the upper airway's natural filtration and humidification, causing thick secretions to accumulate. Suctioning clears these secretions but poses risks such as hypoxia, mucosal trauma, and cardiac arrhythmias.

Guidelines for safe endotracheal suctioning:

  • Hyperoxygenation: Administer 100% oxygen for 30 to 60 seconds before and after the procedure to prevent desaturation.
  • Aseptic Technique: Wear sterile gloves and use sterile catheters to prevent introducing lower respiratory tract pathogens.
  • Limit Duration: Limit each suction pass to 10 to 15 seconds maximum.
  • Negative Pressure: Keep suction pressure between 80 and 120 mmHg to minimize mucosal damage.
  • Intermittent Suction: Apply suction only while withdrawing the catheter in a twisting motion, never during insertion.

Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Complications

Mechanical ventilation carries significant risks, including ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), barotrauma, oxygen toxicity, and stress ulcers. Nurses play a frontline role in implementing evidence-based care bundles to prevent these adverse events.

To prevent VAP, the head of the bed must be elevated between 30 and 45 degrees unless contraindicated. Daily sedation vacations and readiness assessments for extubation reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation. Peptic ulcer prophylaxis and deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis are standard supportive orders. Barotrauma, manifested as pneumothorax, can result from high positive pressure levels, requiring careful monitoring of peak inspiratory pressures.

Ventilator Weaning and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilation back to spontaneous breathing. The multidisciplinary team evaluates readiness daily using parameters such as stable hemodynamics, adequate oxygenation without high FiO2, and reversal of the underlying condition that led to intubation.

A common weaning method is a Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT), where the patient breathes with minimal or no ventilator support via a T-piece or low pressure support for 30 to 120 minutes. The nurse monitors closely for signs of weaning failure, which include tachypnea, tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, and oxygen desaturation. Once the patient passes the SBT and demonstrates an intact cough and gag reflex, the artificial airway is removed, followed by oxygen therapy and close monitoring for upper airway obstruction or respiratory fatigue.

Patient and Family Support in Critical Care

Being on a mechanical ventilator is a terrifying experience for patients who are conscious. The inability to speak due to the endotracheal tube causes severe anxiety and frustration. Nurses must establish alternative communication methods, such as communication boards, alphabet boards, writing pads, or hand gestures.

Family members experience immense emotional distress seeing their loved one connected to complex life-support machinery. Providing clear, compassionate updates, explaining equipment sounds simply, and encouraging gentle touch help alleviate fear. Involving families in non-clinical care tasks fosters a sense of participation and reassurance during a critical health crisis.
