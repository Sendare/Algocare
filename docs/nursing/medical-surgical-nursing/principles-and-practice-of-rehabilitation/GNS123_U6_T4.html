Specific Rehabilitation Situations

Introduction to Rehabilitation Nursing in Nigeria

Rehabilitation nursing focuses on restoring maximum health, function, and independence for patients dealing with long-term illnesses or physical disabilities. In the Nigerian healthcare landscape, this specialty faces unique hurdles. Overstretched public facilities, infrastructural deficits, and limited specialized rehabilitation centers mean that much of the burden falls back on family caregivers after hospital discharge. Cultural beliefs surrounding disability frequently complicate recovery, as some communities attribute physical impairments to spiritual factors rather than medical events. Nurses working in this environment must bridge clinical care with patient advocacy and community education. Understanding local resource limitations shapes how care plans are designed, ensuring goals remain realistic and achievable within the patient's actual living conditions.

Comprehensive Patient Assessment and Goal Setting

Effective rehabilitation starts with a detailed multi-system assessment evaluating physical, cognitive, emotional, and social capacities. Rather than focusing solely on the disease pathology, nurses assess what functional tasks the patient can still perform independently.

  • Functional Status: Evaluating activities of daily living using standardized tools like the Barthel Index.
  • Cognitive and Communicative Ability: Checking memory, orientation, and speech clarity to determine learning capacity.
  • Support Systems: Assessing home environment, caregiver availability, and financial constraints.
  • Psychological Readiness: Identifying signs of depression, denial, or anxiety that might hinder active participation.

Goals must follow the SMART principle—specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Involving the patient and their family in setting these milestones ensures motivation stays high throughout a grueling recovery process.

Stroke and Neurological Rehabilitation

Stroke survivors often present with hemiplegia, spasticity, aphasia, and unilateral neglect. Nursing care centers on protecting vulnerable limbs, preventing contractures, and retraining the brain through repetitive, task-oriented movements.

Positioning is critical. When the patient lies on the affected side, the shoulder must be aligned correctly to prevent subluxation. When on the unaffected side, pillows support the paralyzed arm and leg to maintain neutral alignment. Skin integrity requires constant vigilance because sensory deficits make patients blind to pressure injuries developing on paralyzed zones. Swallowing assessments must precede any oral intake to avoid fatal aspiration pneumonia. Nurses collaborate closely with speech therapists to re-establish safe swallowing techniques and communication strategies.

Spinal Cord Injury Management

Spinal cord injuries alter motor, sensory, and autonomic functions below the level of the lesion. Cervical injuries threaten respiratory function, demanding close observation of breathing patterns and vital capacity. A major physiological crisis in high-level injuries is autonomic dysreflexia—a medical emergency triggered by noxious stimuli like a distended bladder or impacted bowel. Symptoms include paroxysmal hypertension, severe pounding headache, profuse sweating above the injury site, and bradycardia. Immediate nursing action requires sitting the patient upright to lower blood pressure and searching for the triggering stimulus, such as checking for a kinked urinary catheter. Bowel and bladder retraining programs restore a degree of predictability, reducing the risk of urinary tract infections and severe autonomic episodes.

Musculoskeletal Rehabilitation and Amputation Care

Musculoskeletal rehabilitation aims to restore joint mobility, build muscle strength, and adapt patients to permanent structural losses such as limb amputations. For patients with amputations, stump care begins in the immediate post-operative phase. Nurses manage surgical drains, monitor for signs of infection, and apply elastic compression bandages in a figure-eight pattern to shape the residual limb for future prosthetic fitting. Phantom limb sensation—feeling like the missing limb is still attached—is normal and should be validated rather than dismissed, though true phantom limb pain requires medical management. Early prone lying prevents hip flexion contractures in below-knee and above-knee amputees, preserving the gait mechanics needed for walking aids.

Managing Chronic Respiratory and Cardiac Conditions

Pulmonary and cardiac rehabilitation focuses on building endurance while staying safely within the patient's cardiorespiratory limits. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or heart failure experience severe fatigue and shortness of breath that restrict daily activities. Nurses teach energy conservation techniques, such as pacing activities, breaking tasks into smaller steps, and coordinating breathing with physical exertion. Pursed-lip breathing helps keep airways open longer during expiration, trapping less air in the lungs. Exercise regimens are carefully monitored using pulse oximetry and heart rate tracking to prevent dangerous oxygen desaturation or cardiac strain.

Psychosocial Support and Coping Strategies

Physical disability triggers profound psychological distress, including grief, anger, anxiety, and clinical depression. Patients mourn the loss of their former bodily function and independence. Nurses provide therapeutic listening, allowing patients to express their frustrations without judgment. Encouraging peer support by connecting newly disabled patients with individuals who have successfully adapted to similar conditions offers tangible proof that meaningful life continues after trauma. Family counseling is equally vital, as caregiver burnout is high in settings where formal home-help services are scarce. Recognizing early warning signs of depression ensures timely psychiatric or psychological referrals.

Multidisciplinary Team Collaboration and Community Reintegration

Successful rehabilitation requires seamless teamwork among nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech pathologists, medical social workers, and physicians. Each discipline contributes specific expertise, but the nurse coordinates daily execution and monitors patient responses around the clock. Community reintegration bridges the hospital ward and the patient's home environment. Nurses assess home accessibility—checking for steps, narrow doorways, or absence of bathroom rails—and collaborate with social workers to source mobility aids like wheelchairs or crutches. Educating local communities reduces stigma and helps patients return to work, school, and social life with dignity.
