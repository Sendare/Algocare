Fits

Introduction to Fits and Epilepsy

A fit, medically termed a seizure, is a sudden, uncontrolled burst of electrical activity in the brain that causes temporary changes in behavior, movements, feelings, or level of consciousness. It is crucial to distinguish a single isolated fit from epilepsy, which is a chronic neurological condition diagnosed when a person experiences two or more unprovoked seizures separated by at least 24 hours. Seizures manifest differently depending on which part of the brain is involved and how widely the electrical disturbance spreads. In many Nigerian clinical settings, patients and families may use the term 'fit' to describe various convulsive episodes, making a careful nursing history essential to differentiate true epileptic seizures from non-epileptic events such as syncope or psychogenic attacks.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

At the cellular level, a seizure occurs when there is an imbalance between the brain's excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters. Normally, glutamate promotes neuronal excitation while gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) suppresses it to maintain calm electrical activity. During a seizure, a hypersynchronous discharge of neurons fires excessively because excitation overwhelms inhibition. This electrical storm can remain localized in one cerebral hemisphere or rapidly recruit widespread networks across the entire brain. Prolonged seizure activity stresses neuronal tissue by increasing metabolic demand, depleting oxygen and glucose, and eventually causing cellular injury if not interrupted.

Classification and Types of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on where they begin in the brain and how the patient behaves during the event.

  • Focal seizures: Start in a specific, localized area of one brain hemisphere. They may be focal aware (where consciousness is retained) or focal impaired awareness (where the patient is confused or unresponsive).
  • Generalized seizures: Engage both cerebral hemispheres simultaneously from the onset, immediately causing a loss of consciousness.
  • Generalized tonic-clonic seizures: The classic presentation involving a stiffening phase (tonic) followed by rhythmic jerking (clonic).
  • Absence seizures: Brief, sudden lapses in consciousness, lasting only a few seconds, commonly seen in children who appear to stare blankly.

Etiology and Common Triggers

While many cases of epilepsy are idiopathic, secondary seizures stem from identifiable structural, metabolic, or infectious insults to the central nervous system. In tropical regions like Nigeria, central nervous system infections such as cerebral malaria, meningitis, and neurocysticercosis are prevalent triggers. Other common causes include traumatic brain injury, perinatal hypoxia, stroke, and brain tumors. Furthermore, breakthrough seizures in diagnosed patients are frequently precipitated by specific triggers:

  • Missed medication doses: The single most common cause of recurrent hospital admissions for fits.
  • Sleep deprivation: Exhaustion drastically lowers the brain's seizure threshold.
  • Substance use and withdrawal: Alcohol and certain stimulants provoke electrical instability.
  • Metabolic derangements: Severe hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, and uremia.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluating a patient who has experienced a fit requires a systematic clinical approach. The nurse must gather a detailed eyewitness account covering the prodrome, the exact motor activity, eye deviation, tongue biting, and the post-ictal state. Diagnostic workups aim to identify the underlying cause and confirm electrical abnormalities. An electroencephalogram (EEG) records brain wave patterns to pinpoint seizure foci or generalized patterns. Neuroimaging, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), helps rule out structural lesions like tumors, strokes, or abscesses. Blood tests are routinely ordered to check electrolytes, blood glucose, and anti-seizure drug levels.

Acute Nursing Management During a Seizure

When a patient has an active generalized seizure in the ward, immediate safety is the nurse's primary responsibility. Do not panic, and never attempt to force objects or padded tongue depressors between clenched teeth, as this risks breaking teeth and causing airway obstruction. Gently ease the patient onto their side into the recovery position to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus and keep the airway clear. Clear the surrounding area of hard or sharp objects. Loosen tight clothing around the neck. Time the duration of the seizure carefully, administer prescribed emergency rescue medication if the fit lasts longer than five minutes, and stay with the patient until full consciousness returns during the post-ictal phase.

Pharmacological Treatment and Antiepileptic Drugs

Long-term management of epilepsy relies on antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) designed to raise the seizure threshold and stabilize neuronal membranes. Common agents include sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and levetiracetam. Nurses play a vital role in medication administration, monitoring for adverse drug reactions, and emphasizing adherence. Abrupt cessation of AEDs can trigger severe rebound seizures or status epilepticus. Therapeutic drug monitoring is often necessary for medications with narrow therapeutic windows, such as phenytoin, to ensure efficacy while avoiding toxicity. Patients must also be educated regarding potential side effects like drowsiness, gingival hyperplasia, or teratogenic risks in pregnancy.

Complications and Status Epilepticus

The most dangerous acute complication of epilepsy is status epilepticus, defined as a continuous seizure lasting longer than five minutes or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness in between. This medical emergency causes hyperthermia, hypoxia, systemic acidosis, and permanent neuronal death if prompt intervention fails. First-line management involves intravenous benzodiazepines like diazepam or lorazepam, followed by longer-acting anticonvulsants like intravenous phenytoin. Aside from status epilepticus, chronic epilepsy carries risks of physical injury from falls, sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), and significant psychosocial challenges due to social stigma.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Modifications

Comprehensive nursing care extends beyond hospital discharge through targeted patient education and lifestyle counseling. Patients must learn to identify and avoid individual seizure triggers, such as sleep deprivation, flashing lights, or alcohol. Practical safety precautions are essential, including advising against swimming alone, operating heavy machinery, or cooking over open fires where an unexpected fit could cause catastrophic burns. In many communities, cultural misconceptions surrounding epilepsy create severe social stigma; nurses must offer empathetic support and link families to community advocacy groups. Regular follow-up appointments and strict adherence to medication schedules remain the cornerstones of successful long-term seizure control.
