Epilepsy

Introduction and Definition of Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a chronic non-communicable neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures. A single seizure does not constitute epilepsy; the diagnosis typically requires at least two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a high probability of further seizures. It affects people of all ages worldwide, presenting significant challenges in resource-constrained healthcare environments like Nigeria due to social stigmas and limited access to anti-seizure medications.

Pathophysiology and Neurobiological Mechanisms

Seizures arise from a sudden, excessive, and synchronous discharge of cerebral neurons. The underlying mechanism involves an imbalance between central nervous system excitation and inhibition.

  • Excitatory neurotransmission: Primarily driven by glutamate acting on NMDA receptors, promoting depolarization.
  • Inhibitory neurotransmission: Mediated by gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which hyperpolarizes neurons to prevent hyperexcitability.
  • Ion channel dysfunction: Genetic or acquired defects in sodium, potassium, or calcium channels lower the seizure threshold.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors

Epilepsy can be categorized by its underlying cause, though many cases remain idiopathic. Identifying specific etiologies helps guide appropriate clinical management.

  • Structural causes: Brain tumors, stroke, traumatic brain injuries, and congenital malformations.
  • Infectious causes: Central nervous system infections such as neurocysticercosis, cerebral malaria, meningitis, and viral encephalitis.
  • Genetic factors: Inherited channelopathies and mutations affecting neuronal development.
  • Metabolic disturbances: Severe hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalances, and uremia.

Classification of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on where they begin in the brain and how they manifest clinically.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Clinical presentation varies widely depending on the seizure type and brain region involved. Nurses must observe and document precise semiology during an episode. A typical generalized tonic-clonic seizure includes a tonic phase of muscle stiffening, followed by a clonic phase of rhythmic jerking, and a post-ictal phase characterized by deep sleep, confusion, headache, and temporary weakness known as Todd's paresis.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Monitoring

Accurate diagnosis relies heavily on a detailed clinical history combined with neurodiagnostic testing.

  • Electroencephalogram (EEG): The cornerstone test that records abnormal electrical discharges and helps classify seizure types.
  • Neuroimaging: Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) to identify structural lesions, tumors, or scars.
  • Laboratory investigations: Electrolyte panels, blood glucose tests, and toxicological screens to rule out acute provoked seizures.

Pharmacological and Surgical Management

Long-term management centers on controlling seizures while minimizing medication side effects. Anti-Seizure Medications (ASMs) such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, sodium valproate, and phenobarbitone are first-line therapies. Selection depends on seizure classification, patient age, and comorbid conditions. For drug-resistant epilepsy, surgical resection of the epileptogenic focus, corpus callosotomy, or vagus nerve stimulation may be considered.

Nursing Care Plan and Status Epilepticus Protocols

Status epilepticus is a medical emergency defined as continuous seizure activity lasting longer than five minutes or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness between them. Immediate nursing priorities include maintaining a patent airway, providing supplemental oxygen, and administering fast-acting rescue medications like intravenous diazepam or lorazepam. Nurses must pad side rails, refrain from placing objects inside the patient's mouth during a seizure, and continuously monitor vital signs.

Patient Education, Safety, and Psychosocial Support

Living with epilepsy extends beyond physical seizure control. Patient education focuses on consistent adherence to ASMs, avoiding known triggers such as sleep deprivation and alcohol, and adopting lifestyle precautions like avoiding swimming alone or operating heavy machinery until seizure-free. Addressing psychosocial concerns is vital, especially countering cultural misconceptions and stigma prevalent in many communities that often isolate affected individuals.
