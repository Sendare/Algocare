Convulsions

Introduction and Definition of Convulsions

A convulsion is a medical emergency characterized by sudden, violent, and involuntary contractions of a group of muscles. It is a clinical sign rather than a specific disease, often presenting as part of a neurological disturbance. During a convulsive episode, the patient typically experiences rapid, rhythmic shaking of the limbs and body caused by uncontrolled electrical discharges in the brain. While the terms seizure and convulsion are frequently interchanged by the public, nurses must recognize that not all seizures involve physical convulsions. Understanding this distinction ensures accurate documentation and appropriate initial response in clinical practice.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Neurological Mechanisms

Convulsions originate from abnormal, excessive, and synchronized electrical activity within the cerebral cortex. Normally, the brain maintains a delicate balance between neuronal excitation, driven by neurotransmitters like glutamate, and inhibition, regulated by gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). When this balance fails, hyperexcitable neurons fire rapidly in clusters. This runaway electrical storm recruits surrounding normal brain tissue, spreading the signal across cerebral hemispheres and triggering the physical motor manifestations seen at the bedside.

Classification and Types of Convulsive Seizures

  • Generalized onset motor seizures: These affect both cerebral hemispheres from the start, manifesting as bilateral tonic-clonic movements where the body stiffens and then jerks.
  • Focal onset motor seizures: These originate within neuronal networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere, causing localized shaking or posturing in specific muscle groups.
  • Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures: These begin locally in one brain region and quickly spread to involve both hemispheres, culminating in widespread convulsions.

Etiology and Common Triggers

Convulsions can stem from primary neurological disorders like epilepsy or secondary systemic disturbances. Identifying the underlying trigger is vital for effective nursing management in emergency and ward settings.

  • High fever: Particularly common in young children, resulting in febrile convulsions.
  • Metabolic derangements: Severe hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, and hypocalcemia alter neuronal membrane stability.
  • Central nervous system infections: Meningitis and cerebral malaria cause severe inflammation and irritation.
  • Substance withdrawal or toxicity: Sudden cessation of alcohol or exposure to certain poisons lowers the seizure threshold.
  • Trauma and hypoxia: Head injuries and acute oxygen deprivation disrupt normal brain function.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

A thorough patient assessment during and after a convulsion provides crucial diagnostic clues. The clinical picture typically unfolds in phases: the pre-ictal aura or warning sign, the ictal phase featuring tonic muscle stiffening followed by clonic rhythmic jerking, and the post-ictal phase characterized by profound lethargy, confusion, and deep sleep. Nurses must observe and document exact timings, the direction of eye deviation, symmetrical or asymmetrical limb movements, and the duration of post-ictal recovery.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Diagnosing the root cause of a convulsion requires a combination of laboratory tests and neuroimaging. Blood investigations check for electrolyte imbalances, blood glucose levels, and signs of systemic infection or organ failure. Lumbar punctures are performed if central nervous system infection is suspected. Electroencephalography (EEG) remains the cornerstone investigation, recording abnormal electrical brain wave patterns. Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans help identify structural abnormalities, tumors, or previous stroke damage.

Acute Nursing Management and Emergency Care

During an active convulsion, the primary nursing goal is maintaining patient safety and ensuring a patent airway. Never attempt to force objects between clenched teeth, as this risks dental damage and airway obstruction. Gently ease the patient to the floor, clear the surrounding area of dangerous objects, and turn them onto their side into the recovery position to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Loosen tight clothing around the neck, administer supplemental oxygen if cyanosis appears, and time the seizure meticulously.

Pharmacological Treatment and Patient Education

Emergency pharmacological management relies on fast-acting anticonvulsants, most commonly intravenous or rectal benzodiazepines such as diazepam or lorazepam, to halt continuous firing. Long-term management involves maintenance anti-seizure medications like phenytoin, carbamazepine, or sodium valproate. Nurses must educate patients and caregivers on adherence, warning signs of toxicity, and avoiding triggers like sleep deprivation and flashing lights. Emphasizing that medication must not be stopped abruptly prevents rebound status epilepticus.

Complications and Status Epilepticus

Status epilepticus is a life-threatening medical emergency defined as continuous seizure activity lasting longer than five minutes, or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness in between. Prolonged muscle contractions lead to severe hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hypoxia, which can cause permanent brain damage or death. Management requires aggressive airway support, continuous cardiac monitoring, and rapid escalation of intravenous anti-seizure therapies in an intensive care setting.
