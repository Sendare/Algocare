Acquired Coagulation Disorders

Introduction to Acquired Coagulation Disorders

Acquired coagulation disorders develop later in life due to underlying medical conditions, nutritional deficits, or external exposures, unlike inherited conditions such as hemophilia. In Nigerian healthcare settings, nurses frequently encounter these bleeding tendencies resulting from severe infections, advanced liver disease, obstetric complications, and malnutrition. Recognizing these disorders early prevents catastrophic hemorrhages in vulnerable patients.

Pathophysiology and Normal Hemostasis Overview

Hemostasis stops bleeding through a precise sequence of events involving blood vessels, platelets, and clotting factors.

  • Vasoconstriction: Damaged blood vessels immediately constrict to reduce blood flow.
  • Primary Hemostasis: Platelets adhere to the exposed subendothelium and aggregate to form a loose plug.
  • Secondary Hemostasis: The coagulation cascade activates clotting factors, culminating in fibrin mesh formation that stabilizes the platelet plug.
  • Fibrinolysis: The body eventually breaks down the clot once tissue repair is complete.

Etiology and Common Types of Acquired Coagulopathies

Acquired bleeding disorders arise from diverse clinical triggers that deplete or inhibit clotting factors. Common etiologies include:

  • Liver Disease: The liver synthesizes most coagulation factors; hepatic failure directly reduces factor production.
  • Vitamin K Deficiency: Inadequate dietary intake or malabsorption prevents hepatic synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, and X.
  • Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): Widespread systemic inflammation triggers massive, uncontrolled clotting and subsequent factor depletion.
  • Massive Transfusion Protocol: Rapid infusion of packed red blood cells without adequate plasma replacement dilutes existing platelets and clotting factors.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) in Clinical Practice

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation is a complex, life-threatening syndrome triggered by severe systemic conditions such as sepsis, severe obstetric complications like abruptio placentae, and major trauma. The underlying mechanism involves runaway activation of the clotting cascade, leading to microvascular thrombosis throughout the body. As platelets and clotting factors become completely exhausted, the patient paradoxically experiences severe hemorrhage from surgical sites, venipuncture punctures, and mucous membranes. Nursing care requires constant monitoring of vital signs and rapid administration of prescribed blood components.

Vitamin K Deficiency and Liver-Related Coagulopathies

Vitamin K is a fat-soluble nutrient essential for the gamma-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. In adults, deficiency occurs through prolonged antibiotic therapy that eradicates gut flora, fat malabsorption syndromes, or poor nutritional intake. Liver dysfunction independently impairs coagulation because hepatocytes cannot process available precursors into functional clotting proteins. Parenteral administration of vitamin K corrects nutritional deficiencies within hours, but fails to restore clotting ability if severe hepatocellular destruction is present.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Nurses must recognize subtle and overt signs of bleeding during routine patient assessments. Common physical findings include petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis, epistaxis, and bleeding from intravenous sites or surgical wounds. Gastrointestinal bleeding manifests as melena or hematemesis, while internal bleeding causes falling blood pressure, tachycardia, and altered mental status. Thorough assessment requires checking mucous membranes, inspecting skin integrity, and monitoring urine output for hematuria.

Medical Management and Blood Product Administration

Treating acquired coagulation disorders focuses on correcting the underlying cause and replacing deficient blood components. Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) provides missing clotting factors, while platelet concentrates address severe thrombocytopenia. Cryoprecipitate supplies specific factors like fibrinogen. Nurses must verify blood products carefully, monitor transfusion rates, and watch closely for transfusion reactions or circulatory overload, especially in patients with compromised cardiac or renal status.

Nursing Care Plan, Complications, and Patient Education

Nurses coordinate comprehensive care for patients with bleeding disorders by implementing strict safety precautions. Interventions include using soft toothbrushes, avoiding intramuscular injections, and minimizing venipunctures. Nurses monitor for life-threatening complications such as intracranial hemorrhage or hypovolemic shock. Patient and family education focuses on recognizing early warning signs of bleeding, avoiding aspirin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and keeping follow-up clinic appointments.
