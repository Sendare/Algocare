Health History in Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Health History in Haematological Nursing

Taking a health history for a patient with a blood disorder requires patience and thoroughness. Blood is everywhere in the body, so a problem in the haematological system can show up in almost any organ. Nurses must look beyond the obvious physical signs and dig deep into the patient's past and daily life. A good interview builds trust and uncovers subtle clues that standard lab tests might miss.

Biographical Data and Demographic Factors

Certain blood disorders target specific populations due to genetics, geography, or environment. Age, sex, occupation, and ethnic background form the bedrock of demographic profiling.

  • Age: Congenital disorders like sickle cell disease present in early childhood, whereas acute myeloid leukaemia is more common in older adults.
  • Sex: Inherited conditions like haemophilia are X-linked, predominantly affecting males.
  • Ethnicity: Sickle cell trait and disease have a high prevalence among populations of African descent.
  • Occupation: Exposure to industrial benzene or agricultural chemicals increases the risk of bone marrow suppression.

Chief Complaint and History of Present Illness

Patients with blood conditions often report vague symptoms that develop slowly. Chronic fatigue is the hallmark complaint of anaemia, driven by low tissue oxygenation.

When exploring the chief complaint, use the OLDCARTS mnemonic to capture the onset, location, duration, characteristics, aggravating factors, relieving factors, treatment, and severity. Differentiate between acute bleeding episodes, such as epistaxis or menorrhagia, and chronic issues like persistent low-grade fevers linked to malignancies.

Past Medical, Surgical, and Family History

A detailed past medical history reveals previous transfusions, blood-borne infections, or chronic renal disease that could impact erythropoietin production. Surgical history is equally critical; check for complications like excessive hemorrhage following tooth extractions or prior surgeries.

Because many blood disorders run in families, construct a three-generation pedigree. Inquire specifically about unexplained early deaths, bleeding tendencies, or chronic anemias among blood relatives.

Medication History and Toxic Exposures

Many drugs alter blood counts and clotting function. Ask about prescription medicines, over-the-counter painkillers, and herbal preparations. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs inhibit platelet aggregation and can trigger gastrointestinal bleeds. Long-term use of certain antibiotics or anti-seizure medications can cause bone marrow suppression.

Inquire about home and work environments. Chronic exposure to organic solvents, pesticides, or radiation poses a severe threat to haematopoietic stem cells.

Psychosocial, Nutritional, and Lifestyle History

Dietary habits directly impact blood health. Inquire about iron, folate, and vitamin B12 intake, especially in vegetarian or food-insecure populations. Alcohol abuse interferes with folate metabolism and suppresses bone marrow activity. Assess the patient's support systems, coping mechanisms, and financial status, as chronic blood disorders require expensive, lifelong management.
