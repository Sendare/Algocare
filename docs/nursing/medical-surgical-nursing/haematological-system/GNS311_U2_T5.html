Diagnostic Evaluation of Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Haematological Diagnostics

Haematological diagnostics involves evaluating blood components, bone marrow, and the lymphatic system to identify disorders such as anaemia, leukemias, and bleeding dysfunctions. A structured diagnostic approach ensures clinicians move from broad clinical suspicion down to specific cellular and molecular abnormalities. In Nigerian clinical practice, where infectious conditions like malaria frequently mimic or trigger blood abnormalities, a careful diagnostic framework prevents misdiagnosis. Diagnostic modalities range from basic bedside assessments to complex genetic profiling.

Comprehensive Patient History and Physical Examination

A detailed history provides vital clues before laboratory tubes are ever drawn. The nurse or clinician must inquire about chronic fatigue, bleeding tendencies, recurrent infections, occupational exposures, and family history of inherited blood disorders. Physical examination focuses on signs reflecting bone marrow failure or excessive cell destruction.

  • Pallor: Indicates possible anaemia or reduced tissue oxygenation.
  • Jaundice: Suggests haemolytic anaemia due to excessive red blood cell breakdown.
  • Lymphadenopathy: Points toward infection, lymphoma, or chronic lymphocytic leukaemia.
  • Splenomegaly: Reveals extramedullary haematopoiesis or increased destruction of blood elements.

Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Peripheral Blood Smear

The Complete Blood Count is the cornerstone of haematological evaluation, measuring red blood cells, white blood cells, haemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelets. Indices such as Mean Corpuscular Volume help classify anemias into microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic categories. When the CBC yields abnormal results, a peripheral blood smear is examined under a microscope. This allows pathologists and trained scientists to observe cell morphology directly, identifying sickle cells, blast cells, or schistocytes.

Bone Marrow Aspiration and Biopsy Procedures

When peripheral blood tests remain inconclusive, bone marrow examination provides direct insight into haematopoiesis. Aspiration extracts fluid marrow for cellular cytology and flow cytometry, while the trephine biopsy obtains a solid core tissue sample to assess architecture and cellularity. The posterior superior iliac spine is the preferred site in adults. Nurses play a critical role in explaining the procedure, obtaining informed consent, positioning the patient laterally, monitoring vital signs, and managing post-procedure pain and bleeding risks at the puncture site.

Coagulation and Haemostasis Tests

Evaluating the clotting system requires specific laboratory assays to detect bleeding disorders or monitor anticoagulant therapy.

  • Prothrombin Time (PT) and INR: Measures the extrinsic coagulation pathway, commonly used to monitor warfarin therapy.
  • Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT): Evaluates the intrinsic and common pathways, useful for monitoring heparin therapy.
  • Fibrinogen Assay: Quantifies the primary clotting protein converted to fibrin during clot formation.
  • Bleeding Time / Platelet Function Tests: Assess primary haemostasis and the ability of platelets to form an initial plug.

Specialized Laboratory and Genetic Testing

Advanced diagnostics utilize molecular and immunological techniques to pinpoint specific blood cancers and inherited hemoglobinopathies. Flow cytometry identifies specific surface antigens on white blood cells to classify leukemias and lymphomas accurately. Cytogenetic analysis, including karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), detects chromosomal translocations like the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukemia. Haemoglobin electrophoresis remains essential in West Africa for diagnosing sickle cell disease and other hemoglobin variants.

Radiological and Imaging Studies

Imaging modalities assist in staging haematological malignancies and locating areas of extramedullary disease or bone destruction. Plain radiographs reveal lytic bone lesions in multiple myeloma. Computed tomography scans evaluate lymph node enlargement in the chest, abdomen, and pelvis for lymphoma staging. Magnetic resonance imaging offers detailed views of bone marrow infiltration. Positron emission tomography scans help differentiate active tumor tissue from necrotic or fibrotic scar tissue following treatment.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care

Nurses coordinate the diagnostic journey, ensuring patient safety, accurate specimen collection, and emotional reassurance. Proper labeling and timely transport of blood and marrow samples prevent erroneous laboratory results. Following invasive procedures like bone marrow biopsy, nurses monitor vital signs and inspect puncture sites for persistent oozing or hematoma formation. Patient education is vital, helping individuals understand preparation requirements for fasting tests or managing anxiety regarding diagnostic procedures.
