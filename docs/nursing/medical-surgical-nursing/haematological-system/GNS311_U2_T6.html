Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Anaemia

Anaemia is a pathological condition characterised by a reduction in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, resulting from a decrease in the concentration of circulating haemoglobin, red blood cell mass, or packed cell volume below normal reference ranges for age and sex.

At the cellular level, inadequate tissue oxygenation triggers compensatory physiological mechanisms. The sympathetic nervous system increases heart rate and stroke volume to enhance cardiac output. Simultaneously, the kidneys increase erythropoietin production to stimulate bone marrow erythropoiesis. When these compensatory mechanisms fail to meet systemic oxygen demands, clinical symptoms of tissue hypoxia emerge.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia

Classifying anaemia helps identify the root cause, usually categorizing them by red blood cell size and haemoglobin content using indices like Mean Corpuscular Volume.

  • Microcytic Hypochromic Anaemia: Characterized by small, pale red blood cells, commonly caused by iron deficiency or thalassemia.
  • Normocytic Normochromic Anaemia: Cells are normal in size and color, frequently resulting from acute blood loss, chronic disease, or haemolysis.
  • Macrocytic Megaloblastic Anaemia: Features abnormally large red blood cells due to impaired DNA synthesis, typically caused by vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Clinical features of anaemia vary depending on the severity and the speed of onset. Patients often exhibit generalized weakness, chronic fatigue, and exercise intolerance as early signs.

Pallor of the skin, nail beds, and conjunctivae reflects reduced blood flow and lower haemoglobin levels in superficial tissues. Cardiovascular signs include tachycardia, palpitations, and systolic flow murmurs as the heart pumps faster to compensate for low oxygen levels. Severe cases can present with shortness of breath on exertion, dizziness, and koilonychia in chronic iron deficiency.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Evaluations

Laboratory testing confirms the diagnosis of anaemia and guides targeted intervention.

  • Full Blood Count (FBC): Measures haemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, and red blood cell indices.
  • Serum Ferritin: Reflects total body iron stores and helps isolate iron deficiency from other causes.
  • Serum Iron and Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC): Evaluates circulating iron levels and the capacity of transferrin to bind iron.
  • Vitamin B12 and Folate Assays: Identifies megaloblastic causes when MCV is elevated.
  • Stool Microscopy: Checks for occult blood loss due to parasites or gastrointestinal bleeding.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management focuses on correcting the underlying cause, restoring adequate haemoglobin levels, and replenishing nutritional stores.

For iron deficiency anaemia, oral iron supplements like ferrous sulfate are standard, though parenteral iron may be used if absorption is poor. Megaloblastic anaemia requires vitamin B12 injections or oral folic acid replacement. In severe or acute symptomatic anaemia, packed red blood cell transfusions are administered cautiously to avoid circulatory overload. Addressing chronic infections or hookworm infestations is equally vital in endemic regions to stop ongoing blood loss.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Nursing care centers on managing symptoms, promoting safety, and ensuring therapeutic adherence. Nurses monitor vital signs closely for signs of hemodynamic instability or worsening respiratory effort.

Activity intolerance is managed by scheduling nursing activities around rest periods to conserve patient energy. For patients taking oral iron, nurses educate them on expected side effects like dark-colored stools and advise taking supplements with vitamin C to enhance absorption while avoiding milk or antacids that inhibit uptake. Safety precautions are instituted for patients experiencing dizziness or orthostatic hypotension.

Complications and Patient Education in Anaemia

Untreated chronic anaemia can lead to severe complications, most notably high-output cardiac failure due to the heart constantly overworking to compensate for hypoxia. Pregnant women with severe anaemia face higher risks of postpartum haemorrhage, low birth weight, and increased maternal mortality.

Patient education must emphasize dietary modifications, including the consumption of local iron-rich foods such as dark green leafy vegetables, beans, and lean meats. Adherence to prescribed medication schedules and completion of the full course of treatment are reinforced to prevent relapse.
