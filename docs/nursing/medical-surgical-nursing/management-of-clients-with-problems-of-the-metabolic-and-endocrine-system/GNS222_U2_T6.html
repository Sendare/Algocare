Disorders of the Gallbladder

Anatomy and Physiology of the Gallbladder

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped organ situated in the fossa on the visceral surface of the right lobe of the liver. It functions primarily to store and concentrate bile produced by the hepatocytes.

  • Fundus: The rounded, distal portion that projects below the inferior border of the liver.
  • Body: The main central section that contacts the visceral surface of the liver.
  • Neck: The tapered, narrow region that continues into the cystic duct.
  • Cystic Duct: Connects the gallbladder neck to the common hepatic duct, forming the common bile duct.

Bile consists of water, bile salts, cholesterol, bilirubin, and electrolytes. During fasting periods, the sphincter of Oddi remains closed, forcing hepatic bile backward into the gallbladder. Here, the mucosa absorbs water and inorganic electrolytes, concentrating the remaining bile components up to tenfold. When dietary fats enter the duodenum, enteroendocrine cells release cholecystokinin, stimulating gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi to release bile into the small intestine for fat emulsification.

Pathophysiology and Types of Gallbladder Disorders

Gallbladder disease primarily stems from structural or functional disruptions in bile flow and composition.

  • Cholelithiasis: The presence of solid stones within the gallbladder or biliary tree, formed due to an imbalance in the chemical constituents of bile, notably cholesterol and calcium bilirubinate.
  • Cholecystitis: Acute or chronic inflammation of the gallbladder wall, frequently precipitated by mechanical obstruction of the cystic duct by a gallstone.
  • Choledocholithiasis: The presence of one or more gallstones in the common bile duct, leading to biliary stasis and potential ascending cholangitis.
  • Biliary Dyskinesia: Motor dysfunction of the sphincter of Oddi or gallbladder smooth muscle in the absence of demonstrable stones.

Precipitation of cholesterol occurs when bile becomes supersaturated, leading to crystal nucleation, aggregation, and eventual stone growth. If a stone lodges persistently in the cystic duct, trapped secretions increase intraluminal pressure, compromising mucosal blood flow and inviting bacterial invasion.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Gallbladder pathology develops through complex interactions of metabolic, genetic, and environmental influences. Understanding patient demographics helps identify individuals at elevated risk.

Risk CategorySpecific Factors
DemographicFemale gender, advancing age, Native American or Hispanic descent
MetabolicRapid weight loss, total parenteral nutrition, obesity, pregnancy
FamilialFamily history of gallstones, inherited lipid metabolism disorders
MedicalDiabetes mellitus, Crohn disease, terminal ileal resection

Oestrogen enhances cholesterol secretion into bile, while progesterone slows gallbladder motility, explaining the increased risk during pregnancy and with hormonal replacement therapy. Rapid weight loss mobilizes hepatic fat stores, increasing biliary cholesterol saturation.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic incidental findings to severe abdominal emergencies. The classic hallmark of gallbladder disease is biliary colic, characterized by steady, severe pain in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium that may radiate to the right shoulder or scapula.

  • Biliary Colic: Sudden onset of pain lasting 30 minutes to several hours, frequently triggered by fatty meals.
  • Murphy Sign: Cessation of inspiration during deep palpation of the right upper quadrant due to pain, indicative of acute cholecystitis.
  • Jaundice: Yellowish discoloration of the skin and sclera, pointing toward common bile duct obstruction.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Frequently accompanying symptoms due to visceral autonomic stimulation.

Fever, chills, and leukocytosis suggest progression from simple biliary colic to acute cholecystitis or cholangitis. Nurses must perform thorough abdominal assessments, noting tenderness, guarding, and bowel sounds.

Diagnostic Investigations

Confirming gallbladder pathology relies on a combination of laboratory blood tests and imaging modalities.

  • Abdominal Ultrasound: The primary, non-invasive imaging test of choice to identify gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening, and pericholecystic fluid.
  • HIDA Scan: Nuclear medicine scan assessing cystic duct patency and gallbladder emptying function.
  • Liver Function Tests: Serum bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and transaminases, which elevate during biliary tract obstruction.
  • Complete Blood Count: Elevated white blood cell count indicates acute inflammation or infection.

When ultrasound findings remain inconclusive regarding ductal stones, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography may be performed to visualize the biliary tree directly.

Medical and Dietary Management

Non-surgical management focuses on pain control, hydration, infection prevention, and dietary modification. During acute flare-ups, patients are kept nil per os to rest the gastrointestinal tract and minimize cholecystokinin release.

  • Analgesia: Administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or opioids for severe biliary colic.
  • Antibiotic Therapy: Broad-spectrum intravenous antimicrobials if secondary bacterial infection or cholecystitis is suspected.
  • Bile Acid Therapy: Oral ursodeoxycholic acid may dissolve small cholesterol stones over many months in selected surgical candidates.
  • Dietary Modification: Low-fat nutritional plans to prevent postprandial gallbladder spasms once acute symptoms resolve.

Medical dissolution has a high recurrence rate and is reserved for patients who cannot tolerate surgical procedures.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

Surgical removal of the gallbladder, known as a cholecystectomy, remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic gallbladder disease.

  • Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy: The gold-standard minimally invasive approach, utilizing small abdominal ports, associated with shorter recovery times and reduced postoperative pain.
  • Open Cholecystectomy: A traditional approach through a subcostal incision, utilized when severe inflammation, adhesions, or complications preclude the laparoscopic route.
  • Choledochostomy: Exploration of the common bile duct with T-tube placement if ductal stones are found during surgery.

Nursing care involves monitoring vital signs, assessing surgical incision sites for bleeding or infection, managing postoperative pain, and monitoring bowel sounds and passage of flatus. For patients with a T-tube, nurses monitor and record the volume, color, and consistency of bile drainage.

Complications and Patient Education

Untreated or severe gallbladder disease can result in life-threatening systemic complications.

  • Empyema: Accumulation of purulent material within a blocked, inflamed gallbladder.
  • Gangrene and Perforation: Ischemia of the gallbladder wall leading to tissue death and bile leakage into the peritoneal cavity.
  • Pancreatitis: Obstruction of the ampulla of Vater by a passing gallstone, triggering pancreatic enzyme activation.

Discharge education should emphasize proper wound care, recognizing signs of infection like fever or worsening pain, and adopting a gradual return to a low-fat diet. Patients must understand that while bile flows directly into the intestine after cholecystectomy, avoiding excessively greasy meals prevents transient post-operative diarrhea.
