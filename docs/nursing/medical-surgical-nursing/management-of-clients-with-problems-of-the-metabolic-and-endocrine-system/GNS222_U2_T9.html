Pituitary Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pituitary Gland

Often called the master gland, the pituitary sits snugly within the sella turcica at the base of the brain. It hangs from the hypothalamus by a thin stalk called the infundibulum. The gland consists of two distinct lobes: the anterior lobe, known as the adenohypophysis, and the posterior lobe, known as the neurohypophysis.

The anterior pituitary produces and secretes hormones in response to releasing factors from the hypothalamus. These hormones include:

  • Growth Hormone (GH): Stimulates body growth and cell reproduction.
  • Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH): Stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroid hormones.
  • Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH): Stimulates the adrenal cortex to produce cortisol.
  • Prolactin: Stimulates milk production in the mammary glands.
  • Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) and Luteinizing Hormone (LH): Regulate sex hormones and reproductive processes.

The posterior pituitary does not synthesize hormones. Instead, it stores and releases hormones produced by the hypothalamus:

  • Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH): Controls water reabsorption in the renal tubules.
  • Oxytocin: Triggers uterine contractions during labor and milk ejection during breastfeeding.

Hyperpituitarism: Acromegaly and Cushing's Disease

Hyperpituitarism typically stems from a benign pituitary adenoma that secretes excess hormones. When an overproduction of growth hormone occurs after epiphyseal plate closure, it results in acromegaly. Patients present with enlargement of the hands, feet, facial bones, and tongue, along with joint pain and organomegaly.

Excessive secretion of ACTH drives Cushing's disease, leading to chronic hypercortisolemia. Classic physical manifestations include central obesity, a buffalo hump, purple striae on the abdomen, moon facies, and secondary hypertension. Both conditions alter metabolic processes and require careful endocrinological management to prevent long-term cardiovascular and structural complications.

Hypopituitarism and Panhypopituitarism

Hypopituitarism involves the deficiency of one or more pituitary hormones, whereas panhypopituitarism represents a total failure of the gland affecting all hormonal outputs. Common causes include pituitary tumors, surgical removal, radiation therapy, ischemic necrosis from postpartum hemorrhage known as Sheehan's syndrome, and head trauma.

The clinical presentation depends on which hormones are missing. A lack of TSH and ACTH creates profound secondary hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency, presenting with severe fatigue, hypotension, and hypoglycemia. Gonadotropin deficiency leads to amenorrhea, loss of libido, and testicular atrophy. Nurses must recognize these overlapping symptoms quickly, as acute adrenal crisis can become life-threatening without prompt hormone replacement.

Diabetes Insipidus and SIADH

Disorders of the posterior pituitary frequently revolve around water balance disturbances involving antidiuretic hormone.

  • Diabetes Insipidus (DI): Caused by a deficiency of ADH or an inability of the kidneys to respond to it. Patients excrete massive amounts of dilute urine, resulting in severe hypernatremia, polyuria, and constant thirst.
  • Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH): Characterized by excessive, unsuppressed release of ADH. Water is retained within the vascular system, causing dilutional hyponatremia, concentrated urine, and low urine output.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Assessing a patient with a suspected pituitary disorder requires a meticulous head-to-toe physical examination and a comprehensive health history. Nurses monitor vital signs closely, noting patterns of blood pressure changes, unexpected weight fluctuations, and visual disturbances resulting from tumor pressure on the optic chiasm.

Diagnostic evaluations commonly include:

  • Serum Hormone Levels: Measuring baseline cortisol, thyroid hormones, growth hormone, and prolactin.
  • Stimulation and Suppression Tests: Using pharmacological agents to provoke or suppress hormone release to confirm hyperfunction or hypofunction.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The imaging gold standard for visualizing pituitary microadenomas and macroadenomas.
  • Visual Field Testing: Assessing peripheral vision loss caused by optic nerve compression.

Medical and Surgical Management

Medical treatment focuses on correcting hormone imbalances through pharmacotherapy, such as giving synthetic hormone replacements or dopamine agonists to shrink prolactinomas. When tumors are large or fail medical therapy, surgical intervention becomes necessary.

The preferred surgical approach is transsphenoidal hypophysectomy, where the surgeon accesses the pituitary gland through the nasal cavity and sphenoid sinus, avoiding an open craniotomy. Post-operative care requires frequent neurological checks, monitoring for cerebrospinal fluid leaks, and assessing for sudden diabetes insipidus caused by surgical manipulation of the posterior pituitary.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Developing a nursing care plan for pituitary disorders centers on maintaining fluid balance, supporting hormone replacement compliance, and preventing post-operative complications. Nurses instruct patients who undergo pituitary surgery to avoid blowing their nose, coughing forcefully, or straining during bowel movements to protect the surgical graft site.

Lifelong hormone replacement education is vital for patients with permanent hypopituitarism. Patients and their families must understand how to administer medications correctly, recognize symptoms of acute hormone deficiency or excess, and wear medical alert identification at all times to ensure safety during emergencies.
