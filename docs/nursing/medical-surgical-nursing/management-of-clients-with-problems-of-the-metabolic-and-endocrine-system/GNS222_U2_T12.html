Adrenal Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Adrenal Glands

The adrenal glands are small, triangular endocrine organs located superior to each kidney. Anatomically, each gland divides into two distinct parts: the outer adrenal cortex and the inner adrenal medulla.

  • Adrenal Cortex: Produces steroid hormones categorized into three types. The zona glomerulosa secretes mineralocorticoids, primarily aldosterone, which regulates sodium and potassium balance. The zona fasciculata produces glucocorticoids, mainly cortisol, influencing metabolism and stress response. The zona reticularis synthesizes androgens.
  • Adrenal Medulla: Functions as part of the sympathetic nervous system and secretes catecholamines, specifically epinephrine and norepinephrine, driving the fight-or-flight response.

Negative feedback loops involving the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary regulate cortical hormone production through the release of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH).

Addison's Disease and Adrenal Crisis

Addison's disease stems from primary adrenocortical insufficiency, where the adrenal glands destroy or damage themselves, failing to produce sufficient cortisol and aldosterone. Autoimmune destruction remains the leading global cause, while chronic infections such as tuberculosis account for many cases in Nigeria.

Patients present with progressive fatigue, muscle weakness, weight loss, and characteristic hyperpigmentation of the skin and mucous membranes, resulting from elevated ACTH levels stimulating melanocytes.

An adrenal crisis is a life-threatening medical emergency triggered by sudden stress, infection, or abrupt withdrawal of corticosteroid therapy. Symptoms include severe hypotension, shock, vomiting, and vascular collapse. Immediate intravenous administration of hydrocortisone and normal saline is required to prevent death.

Cushing's Syndrome and Cushing's Disease

Cushing's syndrome reflects prolonged exposure to excess circulating glucocorticoids. When a pituitary adenoma overproduces ACTH, driving the adrenal glands to over-secrete cortisol, clinicians specifically term it Cushing's disease. Exogenous administration of corticosteroid medications for chronic inflammatory conditions remains the most frequent non-pituitary cause.

Classic clinical manifestations include:

  • Central obesity with a thin trunk and slender extremities
  • Moon face and a dorsocervical fat pad known as a "buffalo hump"
  • Purple abdominal striae, thin fragile skin, and easy bruising
  • Secondary hypertension and hyperglycemia

Pheochromocytoma

A pheochromocytoma is a rare, usually benign catecholamine-secreting tumor that arises from the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. By relentlessly releasing excessive amounts of epinephrine and norepinephrine, the tumor triggers severe paroxysmal hypertension.

The classic triad of symptoms includes:

  1. Severe, episodic headache
  2. Profuse diaphoresis (sweating)
  3. Tachycardia with palpitations

Diagnosing this condition requires measuring urinary or plasma fractionated metanephrines. A major clinical hazard is precipitating a hypertensive crisis; therefore, healthcare providers must never palpate the abdomen of a patient suspected of having a pheochromocytoma, as physical manipulation can release a massive surge of catecholamines.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Accurately diagnosing adrenal disorders relies on targeted hormonal assays and imaging studies.

  • Screening for Cushing's: Utilizes a late-night salivary cortisol test, a 24-hour urinary free cortisol collection, or an overnight dexamethasone suppression test. Normal suppression fails to occur in patients with Cushing's syndrome.
  • Screening for Addison's Disease: Involves measuring early morning serum cortisol and plasma ACTH levels, followed by an ACTH stimulation test. A failure of cortisol levels to rise after synthetic ACTH administration confirms primary adrenal insufficiency.
  • Screening for Pheochromocytoma: Relies on testing for elevated plasma or 24-hour urinary metanephrines.
  • Imaging: Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen helps visualize adrenal mass lesions or pituitary causes.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment strategies vary depending on whether the gland is overactive or underactive.

  • Hypofunction (Addison's): Requires lifelong hormone replacement therapy. Patients take daily oral hydrocortisone (split into morning and afternoon doses to mimic diurnal rhythms) and fludrocortisone for mineralocorticoid replacement. Dosage must increase during physical stress or illness.
  • Hyperfunction (Cushing's/Pheochromocytoma): Surgical removal of the offending tumor is the definitive treatment. For Cushing's, this involves transsphenoidal removal of a pituitary adenoma or an adrenalectomy. For pheochromocytoma, surgical excision is preceded by several weeks of alpha-adrenergic blockade (e.g., phenoxybenzamine) to control blood pressure and prevent intraoperative hypertensive crises.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education in the Nigerian Clinical Context

Nursing care for patients with adrenal disorders requires vigilant monitoring and practical patient education, keeping local healthcare challenges in mind.

Key nursing responsibilities include:

  • Hemodynamic Monitoring: Assessing blood pressure and pulse frequently to detect early signs of crisis or hypertensive emergencies.
  • Electrolyte Tracking: Monitoring serum sodium and potassium levels, as imbalances can trigger lethal cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Sick-Day Rules Education: Instructing patients with Addison's disease to double or triple their oral hydrocortisone dose during minor illnesses or febrile states, and teaching them how to administer emergency intramuscular hydrocortisone when oral intake is impossible.
  • Resource Accessibility: In resource-limited settings like Nigeria, ensuring patients have reliable access to affordable maintenance medications and emphasizing the danger of abruptly stopping corticosteroid therapy.
