Ethical and Human Rights Issues in End-of-Life Care

Introduction to End-of-Life Care and Nursing Ethics

End-of-life care focuses on maintaining comfort, dignity, and quality of life for individuals facing a terminal illness. Nursing ethics provide the moral framework that guides clinical decisions when cure is no longer possible and the goal shifts entirely to palliative comfort. Nurses often bridge the gap between medical interventions and human dignity, advocating for patients who may be too weak or distressed to speak for themselves. Ethical dilemmas arise frequently in Nigerian clinical practice due to limited resources, cultural expectations, and differing views on death and dying among healthcare teams and families.

Core Ethical Principles in Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Clinical decision-making at the bedside rests upon four foundational bioethical pillars:

  • Autonomy: Respecting the patient's right to make independent choices about their own medical care.
  • Beneficence: Acting in the best interest of the patient to promote wellbeing and alleviate suffering.
  • Non-maleficence: Ensuring that nursing and medical interventions do not cause unnecessary harm or pain.
  • Justice: Fair and equitable distribution of healthcare resources and nursing attention regardless of social status.

Patient Human Rights and Self-Determination

Every individual retains fundamental human rights until the moment of death, including the right to bodily integrity, privacy, and freedom from cruel or degrading treatment. Self-determination means a competent person has the legal and moral authority to accept or reject medical interventions based on their personal values. Nurses must protect these rights even when family members or medical staff pressure the patient to conform to traditional or institutional routines.

Informed Consent, Refusal, and Advance Directives

Informed consent requires that patients receive clear information regarding diagnosis, prognosis, risks, and alternatives before agreeing to a procedure. Conversely, refusal of treatment is an extension of informed consent. Advance directives—such as living wills or durable powers of attorney—allow individuals to state their preferences before losing the capacity to communicate. In many West African settings, formal written advance directives are rare, making verbal conversations and family dynamics critical during health crises.

Distinguishing Euthanasia, Assisted Suicide, and Terminal Sedation

Clarity around terminology prevents ethical confusion in end-of-life care.

  • Active Euthanasia: A clinician directly administers a lethal substance to end a patient's life, usually at their request. This remains illegal in Nigeria and most parts of the world.
  • Physician-Assisted Suicide: A clinician provides the means for a patient to end their own life, such as prescribing a lethal dose of medication.
  • Terminal Sedation: Administering high-dose sedatives to relieve intractable distress in dying patients, even if it may indirectly shorten life. This is ethically and legally distinct from euthanasia because the primary intent is symptom relief.

Withholding Versus Withdrawing Life-Sustaining Treatment

Ethical and legal frameworks frequently differentiate between not starting a burdensome intervention and stopping one that has already begun. Withholding treatment means choosing not to initiate an intervention like mechanical ventilation or dialysis when it offers no clinical benefit. Withdrawing treatment means stopping an intervention that is already running because it has become futile or overly burdensome. Ethically, both carry the same weight; however, psychological barriers for nurses and families are often higher during withdrawal because stopping a machine feels more active than deciding not to start it.

Role of Family, Surrogates, and Conflict Resolution

In many Nigerian communities, medical decision-making is a communal family affair rather than an isolated individual choice. When a patient loses capacity, a surrogate decision-maker is expected to speak on their behalf. Conflicts often emerge when family members disagree among themselves, or when family wishes conflict with the professional judgment of the nursing and medical team. Effective conflict resolution requires active listening, family conferences, clear communication about prognosis, and respect for cultural values.

Cultural, Religious, and Legal Contexts in Nigerian Healthcare

End-of-life care in Nigeria is deeply influenced by diverse ethnic traditions, Islamic and Christian beliefs, and statutory laws. Many Nigerians view death not just as a biological event, but as a transition to ancestral realms or the afterlife, making spiritual rituals paramount. Legally, the Nigerian Criminal Code prohibits mercy killing and assisted suicide. Nurses must navigate these boundaries delicately, honoring spiritual practices like prayer or traditional rites while operating strictly within legal boundaries.

Moral Distress and Support Strategies for Nurses

Moral distress occurs when a nurse knows the ethically correct action to take but is constrained by institutional rules, hierarchical structures, or resource scarcity from doing so. Repeatedly caring for dying patients with aggressive, futile interventions can lead to emotional exhaustion, burnout, and quitting the profession. Establishing debriefing sessions, ethics committees in hospitals, and peer support networks helps nurses process difficult cases and maintain psychological resilience.
