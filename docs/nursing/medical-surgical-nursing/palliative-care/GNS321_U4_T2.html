Concepts of Palliative Care

Introduction and Definition of Palliative Care

Palliative care is an active, total approach to patient care when disease is no longer responsive to curative treatment. The primary goal is achieving the best possible quality of life for both the patient and their loved ones. Control of pain and other distressing symptoms takes center stage, but psychological, social, and spiritual support receive equal attention. Contrary to common belief, this care model is not reserved solely for the final hours or days of life. It can and should be integrated early in the course of any serious, life-limiting illness, running concurrently with disease-modifying therapies.

Core Principles and Philosophy of Palliative Care

  • Affirmation of life: Views dying as a normal process that neither hastens nor postpones death.
  • Symptom relief: Integrates psychological and spiritual aspects of patient care.
  • Support system: Offers a support network to help patients live as actively as possible until death.
  • Family support: Assists families in coping during the patient's illness and their own subsequent bereavement.

Palliative Care Versus Curative Treatment

Primary GoalEradicate disease and restore healthMaximize comfort and quality of life

FocusDisease pathology and organ systemsTotal person and family unit

In clinical practice, these two approaches frequently overlap. A patient might receive chemotherapy to shrink a tumor while simultaneously receiving palliative interventions for nausea and pain.

The Total Pain Concept

Pain in advanced illness is rarely just a physical sensation. Dame Cicely Saunders introduced the concept of total pain to describe suffering that encompasses multiple intersecting domains:

  1. Physical: Tissue damage, fatigue, organ failure, and direct disease symptoms.
  2. Psychological: Anxiety, depression, fear of the unknown, and loss of control.
  3. Social: Financial strain, role changes within the family, and isolation.
  4. Spiritual: Loss of meaning, existential questioning, and struggles with faith.

A nurse cannot treat total pain with analgesics alone. Addressing the physical component alone will fail if the patient is terrified of dying alone or worried about their children's school fees.

The Multidisciplinary Palliative Care Team

No single healthcare professional can address every facet of a dying patient's suffering. Effective palliative care relies on a coordinated team. Doctors manage disease progression and complex medication titration. Nurses coordinate daily care, monitor symptoms, and spend the most time at the bedside. Social workers navigate financial burdens, discharge planning, and family counseling. Pharmacists ensure precise dosing and medication availability. Chaplains or religious leaders provide spiritual guidance tailored to the patient's faith, which holds immense cultural importance in Nigeria.

Patient and Family-Centered Communication

Clear, compassionate communication builds trust and reduces anxiety for patients and their families. Nurses must assess what the patient and family understand about the diagnosis before sharing new information. Active listening involves allowing silence, observing non-verbal cues, and validating emotional outbursts without trying to instantly fix them. In many Nigerian settings, extended family members often wish to shield the patient from a poor prognosis. The nurse must navigate this delicate balance by respecting family dynamics while honoring the patient's right to know and participate in their own care decisions.

End-of-Life Care and Ethical Considerations in the Nigerian Context

Caring for patients in their final days requires reverence, symptom control, and respect for cultural and religious traditions. Nurses manage physical signs such as noisy breathing, terminal restlessness, and skin changes while ensuring comfort. In Nigeria, spiritual and religious rituals surrounding death carry profound weight for both Christians and Muslims. Ethical dilemmas often arise around requests for life prolongation versus allowing a peaceful, natural death. Nurses advocate for comfort measures, pain relief even if it hastens drowsiness, and an environment where family can gather according to local customs.
