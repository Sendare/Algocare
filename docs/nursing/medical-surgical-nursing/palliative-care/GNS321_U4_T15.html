Management of Symptoms Other Than Pain

Introduction to Non-Pain Symptom Management

Palliative care extends far beyond pain relief. Patients facing life-limiting illnesses often struggle with a heavy burden of physical and psychological symptoms that severely diminish their quality of life. Non-pain symptoms can be just as distressing, if not more so, than physical pain. Comprehensive care requires equal attention to these secondary complaints, which frequently arise from disease progression or treatment side effects. Addressing these issues early prevents needless suffering and supports both the patient and their family through the illness trajectory.

Assessment and Holistic Evaluation

Effective symptom management starts with a thorough, systematic assessment. Nurses must evaluate each symptom's onset, duration, severity, and triggering factors. Because palliative issues are multidimensional, evaluation goes beyond physical parameters. Emotional, social, and spiritual dimensions deeply influence how a patient experiences symptoms like fatigue or breathlessness. Regular use of validated screening tools ensures that subtle changes are caught early before they overwhelm the patient's coping reserves.

Management of Gastrointestinal Symptoms

Gastrointestinal disturbances are common and debilitating in advanced illnesses. Nausea, vomiting, and constipation frequently stem from medications like opioids, metabolic imbalances, or direct tumor involvement.

  • Nausea and Vomiting: Requires identifying the underlying trigger, whether vestibular, chemical, or mechanical, to select the correct antiemetic.
  • Constipation: Often preventable with a bowel regimen initiated concurrently with opioid therapy, combining stool softeners and stimulants.

Proactive management prevents severe complications such as bowel obstruction or delirium.

Respiratory Support: Dyspnea and Cough

Dyspnea, or the subjective feeling of breathlessness, is terrifying for patients and families alike. Non-pharmacological interventions form the cornerstone of comfort: positioning the patient upright, using a bedside fan to stimulate trigeminal facial receptors, and teaching pursed-lip breathing techniques. Low-dose systemic opioids remain the gold standard for pharmacological reduction of the sensation of breathlessness when underlying causes cannot be reversed. A persistent cough can also exhaust a weakened patient, requiring humidification and appropriate antitussives.

Addressing Constitutional Symptoms: Fatigue and Cachexia

Constitutional symptoms like profound fatigue, anorexia, and cachexia accompany many advanced systemic diseases. Cancer cachexia, characterized by progressive muscle wasting, cannot be reversed simply by forcing food intake. Metabolic shifts drive this breakdown, and aggressive artificial nutrition often causes more harm than comfort. Nurses should educate families that loss of appetite is a natural part of the dying process. Focusing on comfort feeding, meticulous mouth care, and managing olfactory triggers brings greater relief than forced meals.

Interventions for Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Psychological distress, anxiety, depression, and delirium frequently complicate end-of-life care. Delirium requires urgent investigation for reversible causes such as infection, constipation, or urinary retention, alongside calming environmental modifications. Existential distress and situational anxiety respond well to empathetic listening, psychological support, and sometimes short-acting anxiolytics. Acknowledging a patient's fears without dismissing them fosters trust and emotional stability during a vulnerable time.

Skin Integrity, Pruritus, and Pressure Injuries

Maintaining skin integrity is a vital component of comfort care. Pruritus, or severe itching, can stem from hepatic or renal failure and responds poorly to standard antihistamines, often requiring targeted agents like gabapentin or specific binders. Pressure injuries develop rapidly in immobile, cachectic patients. Prevention relies on regular repositioning, specialized pressure-relieving mattresses, and keeping the skin clean and dry. When wounds are unavoidable, the goal shifts to odor management, pain control, and preventing infection rather than aggressive healing.

Multidisciplinary Collaborative Care

No single healthcare professional can manage the complex web of non-pain symptoms alone. Effective palliative care relies heavily on a coordinated multidisciplinary team including physicians, nurses, pharmacists, social workers, and spiritual care providers. Each discipline contributes unique expertise to address the multifaceted needs of the patient and family. Clear communication and regular team meetings ensure that care plans remain cohesive, reducing the risk of conflicting interventions and ensuring a dignified, comfortable experience.
