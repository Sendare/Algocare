Fracture

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A fracture is a complete or partial break in the continuity of bone tissue. When the physical force exerted on a bone is stronger than the bone itself, it cracks or breaks. In clinical practice, this disruption can range from a hairline crack caused by minor stress to a complete shattering of the bone resulting from high-velocity trauma.

Classification and Types of Fractures

  • Closed (Simple): The bone breaks, but the overlying skin remains intact with no external wound.
  • Open (Compound): The broken bone punctures through the skin, exposing the bone to the external environment and high risk of infection.
  • Greenstick: An incomplete fracture where the bone bends and cracks, commonly seen in young children with pliable bones.
  • Comminuted: The bone shatters into three or more pieces at the fracture site.
  • Transverse: The break occurs in a straight line right across the bone shaft.

Causes and Risk Factors

Most fractures stem from acute traumatic events such as motor vehicle accidents, falls from a height, or sports injuries. However, pathological fractures occur in bones already weakened by underlying disease processes.

Key risk factors include:

  • Advanced age, which naturally decreases bone density.
  • Osteoporosis and other metabolic bone diseases.
  • Nutritional deficiencies, particularly calcium and vitamin D.
  • Long-term corticosteroid therapy.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Assessing a fractured extremity requires a systematic approach. Classic signs include immediate localized pain, swelling, bruising, and an obvious deformity of the limb. Patients frequently report an inability to bear weight or use the affected body part. Crepitus, a grating sound or feeling caused by broken bone ends rubbing together, may be present but should never be actively searched for during examination because it can cause further soft tissue damage.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

Bone healing is a dynamic physiological regeneration process divided into distinct overlapping phases.

  1. Hematoma Formation: Blood vessels tear at the fracture site, forming a blood clot within the first 72 hours.
  2. Fibrocartilaginous Callus: Fibroblasts and chondroblasts invade the clot, creating a soft bridge of cartilage and fibrous tissue.
  3. Bony Callus Formation: Osteoblasts deposit spongy bone, replacing the soft callus with a hard bony bridge over several weeks.
  4. Remodeling: Osteoclasts absorb excess bone while osteoblasts lay down compact bone, restoring the original bone contour.

Diagnostic Investigations

Plain radiography, commonly known as an X-ray, remains the primary diagnostic tool to confirm a fracture, identify its exact location, and classify its pattern. At least two views at right angles to each other are standard practice. Computed tomography (CT) scans may be ordered for complex fractures involving joints or spinal elements. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is exceptionally useful for detecting occult or stress fractures that do not appear on standard X-rays.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management aims to achieve reduction, immobilization, and rehabilitation. Reduction can be closed through manual manipulation or open via surgical incision. Immobilization is maintained using casts, traction, or internal fixation devices like plates, screws, and intramedullary nails. External fixation frames are frequently utilized for severe open fractures with extensive soft tissue damage.

Nursing Care and Patient Management

Comprehensive nursing care centers on frequent neurovascular assessments of the affected limb. The nurse regularly checks capillary refill, skin temperature, peripheral pulses, motor movement, and sensory perception. Pain management and meticulous pin site care for patients with external fixators are vital routine duties. Elevation of the injured limb controls swelling, and early mobilization exercises prevent deep vein thrombosis and muscle atrophy.

Complications of Fractures

Fracture complications can be acute or delayed. Acute complications include compartment syndrome, where excessive pressure within a fascial compartment compromises tissue perfusion, and fat embolism syndrome, often following long bone fractures. Delayed complications feature malunion, where bones heal in a faulty position, and nonunion, where the fracture fails to heal entirely after many months. Infection, particularly osteomyelitis, is a constant threat in open fractures.
