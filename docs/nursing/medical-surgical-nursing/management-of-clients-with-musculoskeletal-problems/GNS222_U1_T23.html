Complications of Musculoskeletal Problems

Introduction and Classification of Musculoskeletal Complications

Musculoskeletal injuries, particularly fractures and severe soft tissue trauma, frequently trigger cascading systemic and local complications. These disruptions demand vigilant nursing oversight because patient deterioration can happen rapidly. Clinicians generally divide these adverse events into two major groups: early complications that arise within hours or days of the initial trauma, and late complications that emerge weeks, months, or even years later.

  • Early complications: Life-threatening systemic conditions such as hypovolemic shock, fat embolism syndrome, compartment syndrome, and acute infections.
  • Late complications: Structural and functional healing failures such as avascular necrosis, delayed union, malunion, and nonunion.

Hypovolemic Shock and Hemorrhage

Hemorrhage is a primary early threat in major orthopedic trauma, especially involving the pelvis and femur. The highly vascular nature of spongy bone and the surrounding muscle tissue means a single closed femur fracture can easily cause the loss of one to two liters of blood into the thigh.

As blood volume drops, tissue perfusion fails. Tachycardia, tachypnea, cool clammy skin, and falling blood pressure serve as classic warning signs. Nurses must monitor vital signs strictly, track urine output via an indwelling catheter, and replace lost fluids and blood products promptly to prevent irreversible multi-organ failure.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

Fat embolism syndrome occurs when fat globules enter the bloodstream from the yellow bone marrow of fractured long bones. These globules travel to the lungs and other organs, causing microvascular occlusion and ischemia.

  • Respiratory distress: Tachypnea, dyspnea, and hypoxemia often present within 24 to 72 hours.
  • Neurological changes: Confusion, restlessness, and lethargy stem from cerebral hypoxia.
  • Petechial rash: A distinct, non-blanching rash appears on the chest, neck, and axilla due to small vessel occlusion, serving as a hallmark diagnostic sign.

Compartment Syndrome

Compartment syndrome develops when increased pressure within a confined fascial space compromises circulation and nerve function. Because fascia cannot stretch, swelling from bleeding or edema traps pressure inside the muscle compartment.

Early recognition centers on the classic five Ps: pain out of proportion to the injury that resists opioid medication, pallor, paresthesia (tingling), pulselessness (a very late sign), and paralysis. Nurses must never elevate an affected limb above heart level, as elevation reduces arterial pressure and worsens ischemia. Emergency fasciotomy is required to relieve the trapped pressure.

Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism

Venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability—known collectively as Virchow's triad—put orthopedic patients at high risk for deep vein thrombosis (DVT). When a thrombus dislodges, it travels through the venous system into the pulmonary circulation, causing a pulmonary embolism.

Prevention includes early ambulation, sequential compression devices, and anticoagulant prophylaxis like low-molecular-weight heparin. Nurses assess for unilateral calf swelling, tenderness, and warmth. A pulmonary embolism presents with sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, and apprehension.

Osteomyelitis and Wound Infections

Infection remains a constant threat, particularly with open fractures or following orthopedic surgery. Bacteria introduced at the injury site can colonize bone tissue, causing osteomyelitis, a severe and difficult-to-treat bone infection.

Patients develop local signs including persistent pain, localized swelling, redness, warmth, and purulent drainage, often accompanied by systemic fever and malaise. Management requires wound cultures, aggressive intravenous antibiotic therapy, and surgical debridement of necrotic bone tissue.

Avascular Necrosis

Avascular necrosis (AVN) happens when bone tissue loses its blood supply, leading to cellular death and structural collapse of the bone. This complication frequently occurs in femoral neck fractures or following prolonged corticosteroid therapy.

As the bone matrix dies, it loses structural integrity, causing gradual pain and loss of joint function months after the initial injury. Diagnostic imaging like MRI or bone scans confirms the diagnosis, and management often requires joint replacement surgery.

Delayed Union, Malunion, and Nonunion

Fracture healing can deviate from the normal timeline and alignment path, resulting in three distinct mechanical complications:

  • Delayed union: Healing takes significantly longer than the expected timeframe but eventually occurs.
  • Malunion: The bone heals in an abnormal or deformed position, which can impair function and biomechanics.
  • Nonunion: The fracture fails to heal altogether, often forming a false joint or pseudarthrosis, requiring bone grafting or electrical stimulation.

Nursing Assessment and Management

Comprehensive nursing care for musculoskeletal injuries requires systematic neurovascular assessments. Clinicians evaluate color, temperature, capillary refill, pulses, sensation, and motor movement in the affected extremity every few hours.

Nurses manage pain aggressively, maintain proper traction or cast alignment, encourage deep breathing exercises to prevent pulmonary stasis, and monitor laboratory values for signs of infection or anemia. Prompt reporting of subtle changes prevents minor issues from escalating into permanent disabilities.
