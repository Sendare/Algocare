Nursing Process Management of Musculoskeletal Problems

Musculoskeletal Assessment and Health History

Assessing the musculoskeletal system requires a systematic approach combining subjective data collection with objective physical examination. The nurse begins by gathering a thorough health history, focusing on the chief complaint, mechanism of injury, and the onset of symptoms such as pain, swelling, stiffness, or deformity.

  • Chief Complaint: Clarify the primary reason for seeking care, noting the exact location and duration of discomfort.
  • Pain Characteristics: Assess pain using standardized scales, determining whether it is sharp, dull, aching, or radiating.
  • Functional Status: Evaluate how the musculoskeletal issue affects activities of daily living, including bathing, dressing, and walking.
  • Past Medical History: Inquire about previous fractures, osteoporosis, arthritis, or chronic systemic illnesses like diabetes that impair healing.
  • Lifestyle and Occupation: Document dietary calcium intake, smoking status, alcohol use, and occupational hazards involving repetitive strain or heavy lifting.

Physical Examination Techniques

The physical examination follows a strict sequence: inspection, palpation, and range of motion testing. Nurses inspect the skin and tissues over muscles and joints for symmetry, swelling, color changes, and visible deformities. Palpation helps detect localized tenderness, warmth, crepitus, and muscle tone abnormalities.

Range of motion is assessed both actively, where the patient moves the joint independently, and passively, where the nurse gently moves the joint through its motion arc. Goniometers are frequently utilized to measure exact joint angles when stiffness or contractures are suspected. Muscle strength is graded on a standardized scale from zero to five, comparing bilateral extremities for symmetry.

Diagnostic and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnostic tests confirm structural abnormalities, while laboratory studies uncover systemic causes of musculoskeletal dysfunction. Common investigations include:

  • X-rays: Visualize bone fractures, joint dislocation, and bone density changes.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Provides detailed images of soft tissues, including ligaments, tendons, cartilage, and muscles.
  • Bone Scans: Detect metastatic cancer, osteomyelitis, and stress fractures using radioactive tracers.
  • Serum Calcium and Phosphorus: Evaluate bone metabolism and mineral balance.
  • C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR): Markers that elevate in inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or bone infections.

Nursing Diagnoses and Care Planning

Once assessment data is gathered, the nurse formulates accurate nursing diagnoses that reflect the patient's physical and psychological responses to musculoskeletal impairment. Common problems include impaired physical mobility, acute pain, risk for peripheral neurovascular dysfunction, and deficient knowledge regarding long-term recovery.

Care planning involves establishing realistic, measurable, patient-centered goals. For instance, a goal for a patient with a tibia fracture might state that the patient will report pain below a level of three on a ten-point scale within forty-eight hours. Interventions are then tailored to achieve these milestones safely.

Management of Immobilization, Traction, and Casts

Immobilization devices such as casts, splints, and traction maintain bone alignment during healing but introduce risks of skin breakdown and neurovascular compromise.

Nurses perform regular neurovascular checks—evaluating pulse, pallor, pain, paresthesia, and paralysis distal to the injury site. Casts must be handled with palms while drying to prevent indentation points that can cause pressure ulcers. Traction ropes must hang freely, and weights must never rest on the floor or bed frame. Pin sites for skeletal traction require strict sterile cleaning protocols to prevent osteomyelitis.

Pain Management and Mobility Promotion

Managing musculoskeletal pain involves a multimodal approach combining pharmacological therapy with non-pharmacological comfort measures. Analgesics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and muscle relaxants are administered as prescribed. Repositioning, cold therapy for acute swelling, and heat application for chronic muscle spasms complement drug therapy.

Promoting mobility safely requires utilizing assistive devices like crutches, walkers, or canes. Nurses ensure patients demonstrate proper technique: advancing the assistive device and the affected limb together, followed by the unaffected limb. Early mobilization prevents complications of bed rest, including deep vein thrombosis, atelectasis, and muscle atrophy.

Patient Education and Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation aims to restore the patient to their optimal level of function and independence. Comprehensive patient education ensures continuity of care after discharge.

Nurses instruct patients on home care for casts or surgical incisions, recognizing signs of infection, and managing assistive devices safely on stairs and uneven surfaces. Nutritional counseling emphasizes adequate protein, calcium, and vitamin D intake to support bone matrix formation and tissue healing. Physical therapy referrals are coordinated to guide progressive strengthening exercises.

Evaluation of Care Outcomes

The final phase of the nursing process involves evaluating whether patient goals have been met. The nurse compares current assessment findings against baseline data collected during admission.

Evaluation criteria include pain levels within acceptable limits, intact neurovascular status, absence of complications such as pressure injuries or venous thromboembolism, and the patient's demonstrated ability to perform self-care and use mobility aids. If outcomes are not met, the care plan is revised to address persistent challenges.
