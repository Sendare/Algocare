CT Scan in Musculoskeletal Disorders

Introduction to Computed Tomography in Orthopedics

A computed tomography (CT) scan uses rotating X-ray equipment and computer processing to create cross-sectional images of the body. In musculoskeletal nursing, this diagnostic tool provides superior visualization of bone architecture compared to standard radiography. While plain X-rays offer a two-dimensional shadow of skeletal structures, CT scans generate detailed, slice-by-slice views that eliminate overlapping soft tissues. Nurses caring for patients with orthopedic trauma, complex fractures, or degenerative bone diseases must understand how these scans guide surgical planning and evaluate healing. Recognizing the capabilities of CT imaging helps clinicians anticipate patient needs and address anxieties related to diagnostic equipment.

Indications for Skeletal CT Imaging

Clinicians order musculoskeletal CT scans when plain radiographs fail to provide sufficient detail for diagnosis or treatment planning. Common indications include complex articular fractures, occult spinal injuries, osteomyelitis, and bone neoplasms.

  • Complex Fractures: Visualizing intra-articular extension in tibial plateau or calcaneal breaks.
  • Spinal Trauma: Assessing cortical integrity and fragment retropulsion in vertebral compression fractures.
  • Bone Tumors: Determining the exact matrix, cortical destruction, and soft-tissue extension of primary or metastatic lesions.
  • Surgical Planning: Measuring bone stock and dimensions for hardware placement or joint arthroplasty.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive nursing assessment before a CT scan ensures patient safety and diagnostic accuracy. Nurses must screen for allergies, particularly to iodine and shellfish if intravenous contrast media is requested. Renal function assessment through serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels is mandatory to prevent contrast-induced nephropathy, especially in elderly patients or those with pre-existing kidney disease. Pregnancy screening protects female patients of childbearing age from unnecessary radiation exposure. Finally, nurses educate patients about the procedure, explaining the necessity of lying completely still on a hard table while the machine makes mechanical buzzing sounds.

Intra-Procedure Care and Contrast Management

During the scan, maintaining patient positioning is critical to prevent motion artifacts that distort images. If intravenous contrast is ordered, the nurse ensures secure peripheral access, often utilizing a large-bore cannula in the antecubital fossa. Patients frequently experience a transient warm flushing sensation, a metallic taste in the mouth, or mild nausea when the contrast agent is injected. Nurses monitor the access site closely for signs of extravasation, such as swelling, pain, or coolness around the catheter. Immediate cessation of the injection and local intervention are required if contrast leaks into the subcutaneous tissues.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Post-procedure care centers on monitoring for delayed hypersensitivity reactions and renal impairment. Nurses encourage high fluid intake, either orally or intravenously, to facilitate rapid renal clearance of the contrast agent. Delayed allergic reactions can occur hours after the scan, presenting as pruritus, urticaria, or mild respiratory wheezing. Patients with diabetes who take metformin require special instructions regarding medication cessation and resumption to prevent lactic acidosis when combined with iodinated contrast. Assessing vital signs and urinary output completes the safe recovery protocol.

Radiation Safety and Patient Advocacy

Diagnostic CT imaging exposes patients to ionizing radiation, requiring nurses to advocate for justification and optimization. The principle of ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) guides radiologic technologists in minimizing radiation dose without compromising image quality. Nurses educate concerned patients, balancing the established risks of radiation against the vital diagnostic benefits of uncovering serious musculoskeletal pathology. Documenting cumulative radiation exposure in patient records helps prevent unnecessary duplicate imaging studies during ongoing orthopedic care.
