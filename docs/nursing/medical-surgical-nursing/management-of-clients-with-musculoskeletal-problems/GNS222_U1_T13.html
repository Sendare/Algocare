Degenerative Joint Diseases

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Degenerative Joint Diseases

Degenerative joint disease, commonly known as osteoarthritis, is a non-inflammatory, progressive disorder of synovial joints. The primary pathological process involves the gradual erosion of articular cartilage. As the smooth cartilage wears away, the underlying subchondral bone is exposed, leading to friction during joint movement. In response to this mechanical stress and micro-damage, the bone attempts to repair itself, resulting in abnormal bone outgrowths called osteophytes or spurs at the joint margins.

Over time, the synovial membrane may experience mild inflammation due to cartilage debris floating in the joint space. Joint capsule thickening and ligament laxity further contribute to joint instability and decreased range of motion. Unlike systemic inflammatory arthropathies, degenerative joint disease typically affects weight-bearing joints such as the knees, hips, and lumbar spine, alongside the distal interphalangeal joints of the hands.

Etiology and Risk Factors

  • Age: Advancing age is the single biggest risk factor due to cumulative wear and tear on joints and decreased cartilage repair capacity.
  • Obesity: Excess body weight places continuous mechanical stress on weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips, accelerating cartilage breakdown.
  • Joint trauma: Previous fractures, ligament tears, or repetitive occupational injuries permanently alter joint mechanics.
  • Genetics: Inherited defects in collagen production can predispose individuals to early-onset joint degeneration.
  • Gender: Women over the age of fifty are statistically more prone to developing severe knee and hand osteoarthritis.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Patients presenting with degenerative joint disease typically report deep, aching joint pain that worsens with physical activity and improves with rest. In advanced stages, pain may occur even at rest or during sleep. Joint stiffness is another hallmark sign, particularly in the morning or after a period of inactivity, though this morning stiffness usually resolves within thirty minutes, distinguishing it from inflammatory arthritis.

Physical examination often reveals reduced active and passive range of motion, joint tenderness upon palpation, and audible or palpable crepitus—a grating sound or sensation caused by bone ends rubbing together. Clients may exhibit visible joint enlargement, such as Heberden's nodes on the distal interphalangeal joints or Bouchard's nodes on the proximal interphalangeal joints. Muscle atrophy from disuse surrounding the affected joint is also common.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosis of degenerative joint disease relies heavily on clinical history and physical examination, confirmed by diagnostic imaging.

Laboratory blood tests such as complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein are typically normal or within baseline ranges. These tests are primarily used to rule out systemic inflammatory or infectious joint disorders.

Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy for degenerative joint disease aims to control pain and improve functional ability rather than halt disease progression.

Topical agents, including capsaicin cream and topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, are often the first-line recommendation for localized joints like the knee, minimizing systemic side effects. Oral analgesics such as acetaminophen may be used, though nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are more effective for managing inflammatory flares. Clinicians must monitor patients on long-term oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for gastrointestinal bleeding and renal impairment, frequently co-prescribing proton pump inhibitors.

Intra-articular injections of corticosteroids offer temporary relief during acute pain flares. Hyaluronic acid injections may also be administered to restore joint fluid viscosity and provide mechanical cushioning.

Non-Pharmacological and Rehabilitative Care

Non-pharmacological strategies form the foundation of long-term degenerative joint disease management. Weight reduction is paramount for overweight clients to decrease mechanical joint loading. Physical therapy focuses on tailored exercise regimens, combining low-impact aerobic conditioning like swimming or cycling with strengthening exercises for muscles surrounding the affected joint, such as the quadriceps for knee osteoarthritis.

Assistive devices such as canes, walkers, and knee braces reduce physical strain during ambulation. Occupational therapists teach joint protection techniques, energy conservation methods, and adaptive equipment use for daily tasks. Thermal modalities, including warm baths or heating pads, help relax stiff muscles, while cold packs can assist during acute inflammatory flares.

Surgical Interventions and Perioperative Nursing Care

When conservative measures fail to control debilitating pain and functional loss, surgical options are considered. Arthroscopic debridement may be performed to flush out joint debris and smooth damaged cartilage surfaces, though its benefits are often temporary. Joint arthroplasty, or total joint replacement, is the definitive surgical treatment for severe hip and knee degeneration.

Perioperative nursing care focuses on preventing common complications. Postoperatively, nurses must monitor neurovascular status in the affected extremity, manage pain effectively to allow early ambulation, and prevent venous thromboembolism through mechanical compression devices and prescribed anticoagulants. For hip replacements, nurses must also enforce movement precautions, such as avoiding excessive hip flexion and crossing the legs, to prevent surgical dislocation.

Nursing Diagnoses, Planning, and Patient Education

  • Chronic Pain: Related to joint degeneration and friction, managed through analgesics, positioning, and heat therapy.
  • Impaired Physical Mobility: Related to joint pain and stiffness, addressed through structured exercise plans and assistive devices.
  • Deficient Knowledge: Regarding disease self-management, weight control, and joint protection strategies.

Nurses collaborate with multidisciplinary teams to create sustainable home care plans. Education centers on pacing daily activities, maintaining ideal body weight, recognizing signs of medication toxicity, and understanding when to follow up with healthcare providers.
