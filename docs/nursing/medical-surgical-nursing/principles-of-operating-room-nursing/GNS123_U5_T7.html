Swab Counting

Introduction and Definition of Surgical Swabs

Surgical swabs, often called gauze sponges, are essential absorbent materials used extensively during operative procedures. Their primary functions include absorbing blood and bodily fluids, retracting delicate tissues, and maintaining a clear surgical field. In Nigerian operating theatres, these items are treated as critical accountable objects because failing to track them can lead to catastrophic post-operative complications. Every single swab introduced into the sterile field possesses a radiopaque marker, a critical safety feature that ensures visibility under X-ray imaging should a retention incident occur.

Types of Surgical Swabs and Sponges

  • Laparotomy sponges: Large, soft absorbent pads used for packing off bowels and soaking up major fluid pools in abdominal surgeries.
  • Gauze swabs: Smaller woven sponges utilized for routine superficial wiping, blunt dissection, and localized bleeding control.
  • Tonsil sponges: Tiny, tightly rolled cotton balls held on forceps during ear, nose, and throat procedures.
  • Neurological patties (pancoats): Delicate, cottonoid strips designed to protect sensitive brain and spinal cord tissues while keeping the field dry.

Roles and Responsibilities of the Scrub Nurse and Circulating Nurse

Accurate swab counting is a dual responsibility shared between the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse. teamwork directly prevents retained surgical items.

  • Scrub Nurse: Manages the sterile field, tracks items currently in use, and hands supplies to the surgeon.
  • Circulating Nurse: Manages the unsterile environment, records numbers on the white board, and tracks items discarded from the wound.

Standard Protocols for Swab Counting Procedures

Every swab count follows a strict chronological sequence to minimize human error.

  1. Initial count: Performed before the surgery begins while all items are still packaged and easily separated.
  2. First intraoperative count: Conducted as the surgical team begins closing deep cavities or organ spaces.
  3. Second intraoperative count: Executed when closure of the subcutaneous tissue layer starts.
  4. Final count: Performed during skin closure to confirm absolute baseline parity with the opening count.

Handling Discrepancies and Missing Swabs

When a count does not match, panic must be replaced with a systematic search protocol. The scrub nurse immediately informs the surgeon, and all progress on wound closure halts. Both nurses recount the entire sterile field, check the floor, inspect the linen laundry hampers, and review the waste disposal buckets. If the item remains unaccounted for, the circulating nurse organizes an intraoperative X-ray. Legal documentation in the patient case notes and incident report forms must accurately reflect the search steps taken.

Prevention of Retained Surgical Items

Retained surgical items (RSIs) represent preventable medical errors that cause severe patient morbidity, legal liability, and loss of professional trust. Preventive strategies rely on strict adherence to institutional protocols rather than memory alone. Never use surgical sponges for dressing wounds, as this practice confuses counts. Maintain organized, clutter-free instrument tables. Implement standardized communication loops between incoming and outgoing nursing shifts during prolonged surgeries.
