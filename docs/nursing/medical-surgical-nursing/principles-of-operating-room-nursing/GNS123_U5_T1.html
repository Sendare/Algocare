Operating Room Structure

Introduction to Operating Room Environment and Design

The operating room (OR) environment requires a carefully planned layout to prevent surgical site infections and ensure smooth patient flow. Architectural design dictates that clean and contaminated materials never cross paths. The suite sits away from high-traffic hospital corridors to reduce microbial contamination.

Key architectural features include:

  • Proximity to support services: Immediate access to the central sterile supply department and post-anesthesia care unit.
  • Seamless surfaces: Walls, floors, and ceilings use non-porous materials that withstand frequent chemical disinfection.
  • Controlled access: Entry points feature interlocked doors to prevent simultaneous opening and loss of room pressure.

Zoning Areas of the Operating Theatre

Surgical suites are divided into distinct zones based on cleanliness levels and attire requirements. Moving from the outer hospital to the sterile core requires progressive changes in clothing and protective gear.

  • Unrestricted Zone: Street clothes are permitted here. This area includes the main reception, dressing rooms, and administrative offices.
  • Semi-restricted Zone: Scrub suits and hair covers are mandatory. This zone houses corridors, equipment storage, and instrument processing rooms.
  • Restricted Zone: Masks, eye protection, and full sterile attire are required. This includes the actual operating theatres and sterile core areas where open instruments are exposed.

Ventilation, Air Handling, and Environmental Controls

Air handling systems in the OR control airborne contaminants and maintain thermal comfort for the surgical team and patient. Positive pressure ventilation ensures that air flows outward from the sterile operating theatre into the semi-restricted corridors whenever doors open.

High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filters remove microscopic particles and bacteria from incoming air. Air changes occur at least twenty times per hour, with a set percentage of fresh outdoor air mixed in. Temperature remains between 18 and 22 degrees Celsius, while humidity stays controlled between 30 and 60 percent to discourage bacterial growth and prevent static electricity hazards.

Surgical Suite Equipment and Infrastructure

The operating table sits at the center of the room, surrounded by specialized furniture, electrical pendants, and monitoring units. Medical gas pipelines supply oxygen, nitrous oxide, and medical air directly through wall outlets or ceiling booms, eliminating tangled hoses on the floor.

Surgical lights provide shadow-free illumination over the operative site and feature sterilizable handles for intraoperative adjustment by the scrub team. Electrosurgical units, suction apparatuses, and patient monitors connect to dedicated electrical circuits equipped with isolated power systems to protect against electrical shock and equipment failure.

Infection Control Protocols and Aseptic Technique

Maintaining sterility is the cornerstone of safe surgery. The surgical scrub removes transient flora and reduces resident skin flora on the hands and arms of the sterile team using antiseptic solutions.

Personnel must adhere strictly to the boundaries of the sterile field. Items dropped below waist level or touching non-sterile surfaces are immediately discarded. Regular environmental cleaning occurs between every case, supplemented by terminal cleaning at the end of each operating day to eliminate pathogen accumulation.

Safety Hazards and Emergency Preparedness in the OR

The operating room contains multiple hazards including surgical smoke, radiation from imaging equipment, sharp instruments, and flammable agents. Surgical smoke evacuation systems capture hazardous aerosols and toxic gases generated by energy devices like electrocautery.

Fire safety relies on the management of the surgical fire triad: ignition sources, oxidizers, and fuel. Operating rooms maintain backup power generators that kick in within seconds of a primary electrical failure. Emergency protocols require all staff to know the locations of fire alarms, emergency shut-off valves for medical gases, and crash carts.

The Role of the Perioperative Nursing Team

Perioperative nursing is divided into distinct roles that work in tandem to ensure patient safety and surgical efficiency. The scrub nurse or surgical technologist works within the sterile field, managing instruments, sutures, and anticipating the needs of the surgeon.

The circulating nurse operates outside the sterile field, managing overall room safety, documentation, patient positioning, and coordination with anesthesia. Both roles share joint responsibility for rigorous sponge, sharp, and instrument counts before and after the procedure to prevent retained surgical items.
