Scrubbing

Introduction to Surgical Scrubbing

Surgical scrubbing is a specialized mechanical and chemical cleansing process performed before any operation. Its primary purpose is to significantly reduce the resident microbial flora and completely remove transient microorganisms from the hands and forearms of the surgical team. Unlike routine handwashing, a surgical scrub requires a specific duration and technique to ensure that the skin is as sterile as safely possible. This practice prevents the introduction of pathogens into the surgical site, protecting the patient from healthcare-associated infections.

Preparation and Attire Before Scrubbing

Before beginning the surgical scrub, personal preparation is critical to maintain sterility. Scrub personnel must remove all jewelry, including rings, watches, and bracelets, because these items harbor bacteria and can puncture surgical gloves. Fingernails must be short, clean, and free of artificial nails or polish. Standard operating room attire must be worn correctly: hair must be completely covered by a surgical cap, masks must fit snugly over both the nose and mouth, and protective eyewear should be in place to prevent splashes.

Selection and Use of Antiseptic Agents

The effectiveness of a surgical scrub relies heavily on the chosen antiseptic solution. Chlorhexidine gluconate and povidone-iodine are the most widely utilized agents in clinical practice.

  • Chlorhexidine Gluconate (4%): Provides rapid microbial reduction and offers a persistent residual effect that inhibits bacterial growth for hours after application.
  • Povidone-Iodine (10%): Acts effectively through the gradual release of free iodine, though its residual activity is shorter compared to chlorhexidine and can be inactivated by organic matter like blood.

Step-by-Step Surgical Scrub Techniques

Surgical scrubs can be performed using either a counted-stroke method or a timed method, depending on institutional policy. Water temperature should be warm to open skin pores without causing excessive irritation. Hands must always be held higher than the elbows throughout the procedure to ensure water and soap flow downward toward the hands, away from clean arms. Each finger, the palm, the back of the hand, and the forearm must be scrubbed systematically. Thorough rinsing follows the exact same directional rule, moving from fingertips down to the elbows.

Post-Scrub Protocol and Entering the Sterile Field

Once the scrubbing and rinsing phases are complete, the nurse must enter the operating theatre without contaminating the scrubbed areas. Hands and arms should never touch any non-sterile surfaces or clothing. Drying is accomplished using a sterile towel, taking care to use separate ends of the towel for each arm using an aseptic blot technique. Gowning and gloving follow immediately while maintaining strict distance from unsterile equipment before approaching the sterile instrument table.

Common Errors and Troubleshooting in Surgical Scrubbing

Even experienced personnel can make errors that compromise sterility. Common mistakes include splashing water onto scrub attire, dropping hands below waist level, failing to scrub all four sides of each finger, and touching unsterile objects while moving into the theatre. If contamination occurs at any point during or after the scrub, the process must be restarted immediately. Vigilance and self-correction are the best defenses against accidental contamination in the operating environment.
