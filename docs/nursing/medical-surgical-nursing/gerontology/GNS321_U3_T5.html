Nursing Care of the Elderly

Introduction to Gerontological Nursing and Aging

Gerontological nursing focuses on the care of older adults, promoting health, preventing illness, and managing chronic conditions. Aging is a natural, lifelong developmental process characterized by gradual physiological decline and cumulative environmental exposures. Unlike geriatrics, which is a medical specialty focusing on disease pathology in the aged, gerontological nursing emphasizes holistic care, quality of life, and the preservation of functional independence. Nurses practicing in this field must differentiate between normal age-related changes, such as mild presbyopia, and pathological processes like cataracts. Dispelling common myths about aging—such as assuming depression and cognitive decline are inevitable parts of growing old—is a core professional responsibility in Nigerian clinical settings.

Physiological Changes in the Elderly

Aging affects every organ system, altering baseline health status and responses to illness.

  • Cardiovascular: Decreased cardiac output, arterial stiffening, and slower heart rate recovery.
  • Respiratory: Reduced chest wall compliance, weakened respiratory muscles, and diminished ciliary clearance.
  • Renal: Decreased glomerular filtration rate and reduced nephron units, impacting drug excretion.
  • Integumentary: Thinning epidermis, loss of subcutaneous fat, and reduced sweat gland activity.
  • Musculoskeletal: Sarcopenia, loss of bone mineral density, and joint cartilage degeneration.

These shifts mean older adults present differently during illness; for instance, pneumonia may manifest as confusion or lethargy rather than a high fever and productive cough.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment

A Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary diagnostic process designed to determine an older adult's medical, psychosocial, and functional capabilities. Unlike a standard medical examination that focuses primarily on disease diagnosis, the CGA integrates physical health with environmental and mental evaluations.

  • Functional Status: Assesses Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) such as bathing and feeding, and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs) like managing finances and medication.
  • Mental Health: Evaluates cognitive function and mood using validated screening tools.
  • Social Circumstances: Examines available support systems, caregiver burden, and living arrangements.
  • Environmental Safety: Identifies home hazards that increase vulnerability to injury.

Common Health Problems and Geriatric Syndromes

Geriatric syndromes are multifactorial health conditions common in older adults that do not fit into discrete disease categories. They are typically precipitated by an interplay of multiple underlying impairments.

  • Frailty: A state of increased vulnerability resulting from age-associated declines in physiological reserve across multiple systems.
  • Incontinence: Involuntary loss of urine or stool, severely impacting social interaction and skin integrity.
  • Pressure Injuries: Localized damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue caused by prolonged pressure and shear forces.
  • Delirium: An acute, fluctuating confusional state that signals an underlying physiological disturbance, such as a urinary tract infection or electrolyte imbalance.

Pharmacotherapy and Medication Safety in Older Adults

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics change significantly with age. Reduced renal clearance and hepatic metabolism prolong drug half-lives, increasing toxicity risks. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—heightens the incidence of adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, and prescribing cascades. Nurses must routinely review medication regimens using screening criteria such as the Beers Criteria to identify potentially inappropriate medications. Patient education must be tailored to accommodate sensory deficits, utilizing large print labels and clear, simplified dosing schedules to encourage therapeutic adherence.

Nutritional Care and Hydration Management

Proper nutrition and hydration are vital for maintaining tissue integrity, immune function, and strength in older adults. Age-related changes like diminished taste buds, poor dentition, decreased gastric acid secretion, and slower intestinal motility often lead to poor oral intake and malnutrition. Additionally, a blunted thirst mechanism puts older adults at high risk for dehydration, which can quickly precipitate acute confusion or kidney injury. Nursing interventions include offering nutrient-dense foods, assisting with feeding patiently, ensuring easy access to fresh water, and monitoring body weight trends closely.

Mobility, Fall Prevention, and Safety

Falls are a major cause of morbidity, loss of independence, and mortality among older adults. Fall risk is multifactorial, stemming from muscle weakness, impaired balance, poor vision, neurological deficits, and environmental hazards such as loose rugs or inadequate lighting. Medications such as sedatives, antihypertensives, and psychotropics further elevate fall risk by causing dizziness or orthostatic hypotension. Prevention strategies involve conducting systematic fall risk screenings, implementing tailored exercise programs to improve lower limb strength, securing the physical environment, and ensuring assistive devices are properly fitted and used.

Psychosocial and Mental Health Care

Psychosocial health in later life is heavily influenced by life transitions, including retirement, bereavement, chronic illness, and loss of independence. Depression is prevalent among older adults but is frequently misdiagnosed as normal aging or masked by somatic complaints like fatigue and pain. Loneliness and social isolation also negatively affect mental and physical well-being. Nurses provide essential support by practicing active listening, encouraging participation in community activities, facilitating family involvement, and screening routinely for depressive symptoms and suicide risk.

End-of-Life Care and Palliative Support

Palliative care and end-of-life management aim to optimize comfort and dignity for older adults facing life-limiting illnesses. This approach focuses on symptom relief—such as managing pain, dyspnea, and nausea—rather than curative treatment. Nurses play a pivotal role in facilitating advance care planning, discussing patient and family preferences regarding life-sustaining interventions, and offering emotional and spiritual support. Ensuring a peaceful, dignified death requires clear interprofessional communication, cultural sensitivity, and compassionate presence for both the patient and their grieving family members.
