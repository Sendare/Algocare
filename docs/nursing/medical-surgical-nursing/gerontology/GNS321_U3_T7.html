Roles of Various Groups in Elderly Care

Introduction to Gerontological Care Teams

Caring for older adults requires a multidisciplinary approach because aging brings complex, overlapping physical, psychological, and social challenges. A single discipline cannot adequately address the multifaceted needs of an aging client who might suffer from chronic illnesses, polypharmacy, and mobility decline simultaneously. The gerontological care team brings together diverse medical and social professionals to pool their expertise, ensuring comprehensive assessments and individualized care plans. In Nigerian healthcare settings, this team often bridges the gap between formal hospital structures and community-based family networks, striving to maintain the highest possible quality of life for the elderly.

Primary Roles of Registered Nurses and Nursing Assistants

  • Registered Nurses (RNs): Coordinate overall patient care, perform head-to-toe clinical assessments, monitor chronic conditions, manage complex medication schedules, and educate both patients and their families.
  • Nursing Assistants / Auxiliary Nurses: Assist clients with activities of daily living, including bathing, feeding, toileting, mobility support, and recording basic vital signs.
  • Wound and Skin Care: Both groups monitor for pressure ulcers, implement turning schedules, and maintain skin integrity in immobile seniors.
  • Advocacy: Nurses act as the primary link between the elderly client, the family, and the rest of the medical team, reporting subtle behavioral or physical changes before they escalate.

Contributions of Physicians and Geriatricians

Medical management in elderly care centers around diagnosing acute illnesses, managing chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes, and overseeing overall treatment trajectories. General practitioners handle routine medical needs, while geriatricians specialize specifically in the physiological changes of aging, atypical disease presentations, and geriatric syndromes like delirium and falls. A key medical responsibility is polypharmacy management—regularly reviewing and rationalizing drug regimens to prevent adverse interactions and toxicity in aging organs with reduced clearance rates. Physicians also establish advance care directives and determine medical prognoses in consultation with families.

The Role of Allied Health Professionals: Physiotherapy, Occupational Therapy, and Pharmacy

Allied health professionals target specific functional and therapeutic aspects of aging:

  • Physiotherapists: Focus on mobility, balance retraining, gait stability, and post-stroke or post-fracture rehabilitation to prevent falls.
  • Occupational Therapists: Evaluate the home and living environment, recommending adaptive devices and structural modifications so clients can perform daily tasks independently.
  • Pharmacists: Dispense medications, screen for drug-drug interactions, and counsel older adults and caregivers on correct timing and administration routes.

Social Workers and Case Managers in Elderly Support

Social workers and case managers address the psychosocial, financial, and environmental determinants of health that heavily influence elderly well-being. They connect families with community resources, financial aid, and long-term care placement options. When abuse, neglect, or caregiver burnout is suspected, social workers conduct safety assessments and coordinate protective interventions. Case managers streamline communication between various clinical departments and insurance or payment schemes, ensuring that discharged patients land in a safe, sustainable living environment.

Family Caregivers and Informal Support Systems

In many cultures, particularly across Nigeria, family members form the foundational backbone of elder care. Informal caregivers provide round-the-clock personal assistance, emotional companionship, and financial support without formal remuneration. However, this heavy burden often leads to physical exhaustion, depression, and social isolation known as caregiver burnout. Nurses and other team members must actively assess family caregivers for signs of fatigue, offer respite care guidance, and provide practical training on safe lifting and chronic disease management.

Interprofessional Collaboration and Communication

Effective gerontological care depends on seamless communication among doctors, nurses, therapists, social workers, and family members. Miscommunication can lead to dangerous medication errors, conflicting care instructions, and fragmented client experiences. Regular team meetings, standardized handoff tools, and shared electronic or paper health records help keep everyone aligned. When team members respect each other's distinct scopes of practice, the older adult receives holistic, safe, and dignified care.
