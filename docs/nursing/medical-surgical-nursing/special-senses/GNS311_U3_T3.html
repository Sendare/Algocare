Conditions Affecting the Eye

Anatomy and Physiology Review of the Eye

The human eye functions much like a camera, focusing light to create clear images. Light enters through the transparent cornea, passes through the pupil whose size is regulated by the iris, and is further focused by the lens onto the retina.

  • Cornea: The clear outer dome that bends incoming light rays.
  • Iris: The colored muscular ring that controls the amount of light entering by adjusting the pupil.
  • Lens: A flexible structure that changes shape to focus near or distant objects through accommodation.
  • Retina: The innermost sensory layer containing photoreceptors (rods for dim light, cones for color and detail) that convert light into electrical impulses.
  • Optic Nerve (Cranial Nerve II): Transmits visual impulses from the retina to the brain's occipital lobe for interpretation.

A common misconception is that the aqueous humor and vitreous humor are identical; aqueous humor circulates in the anterior segment nourishing nearby tissues, while vitreous humor is a gel-like substance filling the posterior segment to maintain eye shape.

Refractive Errors and Common Visual Impairments

Refractive errors occur when the eye cannot clearly bend light to focus it properly on the retina. These structural imperfections cause blurred vision and affect millions of people globally.

  • Myopia (Nearsightedness): The eyeball is too long or the cornea is too steeply curved, causing light to focus in front of the retina. Distant objects appear blurry.
  • Hyperopia (Farsightedness): The eyeball is too short or the cornea is too flat, causing light to focus behind the retina. Near objects are difficult to see clearly.
  • Astigmatism: The cornea or lens has an irregular, uneven curve, creating distorted or blurred images at any distance.
  • Presbyopia: The natural lens loses its elasticity with aging, making close-up reading difficult.

Corrective lenses, contact lenses, or refractive surgeries like LASIK alter the pathway of light to compensate for these anatomical variations.

Inflammatory and Infectious Eye Conditions

Infections and inflammation of the eye structures can cause pain, discharge, and potential vision loss if left untreated.

  • Conjunctivitis: Inflammation of the conjunctiva, often called pink eye. It can be viral, bacterial, or allergic. Bacterial conjunctivitis typically presents with purulent discharge, while allergic conjunctivitis causes intense itching.
  • Keratitis: Inflammation or infection of the cornea, frequently linked to improper contact lens wear or herpes simplex virus.
  • Uveitis: Inflammation of the middle layer of the eye (uvea), which includes the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. It often presents with deep eye ache, photophobia, and blurred vision.

Nurses must emphasize hand hygiene and proper contact lens care to prevent these conditions. When administering eye drops, nurses teach patients to avoid touching the tip of the bottle to the eye to prevent cross-contamination.

Glaucoma: Pathophysiology, Types, and Nursing Care

Glaucoma is a group of eye conditions characterized by progressive damage to the optic nerve, frequently associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) caused by impaired drainage of aqueous humor.

Pharmacological management includes beta-blockers (like timolol) to decrease aqueous humor production and prostaglandin analogues (like latanoprost) to increase outflow. Nurses must stress lifelong medication adherence because lost vision cannot be restored.

Cataracts and Age-Related Macular Degeneration

Both cataracts and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are major causes of visual impairment in older adults, though they affect different parts of the eye.

  • Cataracts: The crystalline lens becomes clouded or opaque, scattering light instead of focusing it clearly. Patients report faded colors, blurred vision, and glare from headlights at night. Treatment involves surgical removal of the cloudy lens and placement of an intraocular lens.
  • Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD): Degeneration of the macula, the part of the retina responsible for sharp, central vision.
  • Dry AMD: Gradual breakdown of light-sensitive cells, leading to slow central vision blur.
  • Wet AMD: Abnormal blood vessels grow under the macula, leaking fluid and causing rapid, severe central vision loss.

Nurses help patients adapt by recommending magnifying lenses, bright lighting, and large-print reading materials.

Ocular Trauma and Emergency Management

Eye injuries require swift, careful assessment to prevent permanent vision loss. Trauma ranges from superficial corneal abrasions to penetrating glob injuries.

  1. Chemical Burns: Irrigate the eye immediately and continuously with copious amounts of normal saline or clean water for at least 15 to 30 minutes before doing anything else.
  2. Foreign Bodies: Never attempt to remove an embedded object. Cover the eye lightly with a rigid shield to prevent pressure on the globe.
  3. Penetrating Injuries: Stabilize any protruding object in place. Do not apply pressure or patch tightly, as this can squeeze out internal eye structures.

Nurses should assess visual acuity immediately if the situation permits, record the mechanism of injury, and prepare the patient for emergency ophthalmology evaluation.

Postoperative Care and Patient Education

Surgical procedures like cataract extraction or glaucoma trabeculectomy require meticulous postoperative care to promote healing and prevent complications like infection or increased intraocular pressure.

Postoperative instructions focus on avoiding activities that raise intraocular pressure:

  • No bending over from the waist.
  • No heavy lifting (greater than 5 kilograms).
  • Avoid straining during bowel movements; prescribe stool softeners.
  • Wear an eye shield at night to prevent accidental rubbing.

Nurses must teach patients how to instill prescribed antibiotic and anti-inflammatory eye drops using sterile technique. Any sudden increase in pain, decrease in vision, or worsening redness must be reported to the surgeon immediately.
