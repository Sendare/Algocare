Review of the Nursing Process

Introduction to the Nursing Process

The nursing process is a systematic, rational method of planning and providing individualized nursing care. It serves as the foundation of professional nursing practice, guiding clinicians through a logical sequence to address patient needs.

  • Assessment: Systematic collection and verification of data regarding the patient's health status.
  • Diagnosis: Clinical judgment concerning human responses to health conditions or life processes.
  • Planning: Establishment of priorities, measurable goals, and specific interventions.
  • Implementation: Execution of the planned nursing actions.
  • Evaluation: Determination of the patient's progress toward goal achievement and plan revision.

This framework ensures care is organized, efficient, and centered on the unique requirements of each patient rather than relying on guesswork.

Assessment: Data Collection and Validation

Assessment involves gathering information about a patient to build a comprehensive health profile. Data is categorized into two distinct types:

  • Subjective data: Information reported by the patient, such as pain levels, nausea, or anxiety.
  • Objective data: Measurable and observable findings gathered through physical examination, vital signs, and laboratory results.

After collection, nurses must validate the data by cross-checking cues to ensure accuracy before forming clinical conclusions. Missing or inaccurate information at this stage compromises every subsequent phase of patient care.

Nursing Diagnosis: Formulating Clinical Judgments

A nursing diagnosis identifies an actual or potential health problem that nurses are licensed and competent to treat. It differs distinctly from a medical diagnosis, which focuses on identifying a specific disease or pathology.

Standardized language provided by bodies like NANDA-International helps maintain clarity among healthcare professionals. A well-constructed actual nursing diagnosis typically follows the PES format:

  • Problem (nursing diagnosis label)
  • Etiology (related factors or probable cause)
  • Signs and symptoms (defining characteristics or clinical evidence observed during assessment)

Planning: Setting Priorities and Expected Outcomes

During the planning phase, nurses prioritize identified nursing diagnoses using frameworks such as Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, placing life-threatening physiological needs above psychological concerns.

After establishing priorities, the nurse writes expected outcomes or goals. Effective goals must be patient-centered and SMART:

  • Specific
  • Measurable
  • Achievable
  • Relevant
  • Time-bound

Vague goals like "patient will feel better" make it impossible to determine if nursing care has been effective.

Implementation: Executing Nursing Interventions

Implementation is the action phase where the established care plan is put into motion. Nurses perform independent, interdependent, and dependent nursing interventions to help the patient reach their goals.

  • Independent interventions: Actions nurses can initiate based on their own clinical judgment and scope of practice, such as repositioning a bedridden patient.
  • Dependent interventions: Actions that require a physician's or advanced practice provider's order, such as administering prescribed medications or starting an intravenous infusion.

Before carrying out any intervention, the nurse must reassess the patient to ensure the action remains appropriate for their current clinical status.

Evaluation: Assessing Patient Outcomes and Revising the Plan

Evaluation is an ongoing, systematic review that measures how well the patient has achieved the expected outcomes specified in the care plan. The nurse compares current clinical data against the target goals.

  • If goals are met, the nursing diagnosis may be resolved and closed.
  • If goals are partially met or unmet, the nurse must investigate why, modifying interventions, adjusting timelines, or re-evaluating the accuracy of the original diagnosis.

Evaluation prevents stagnant nursing care, ensuring that clinical actions actively adapt to the changing realities of the patient's recovery process.

Documentation and Communication

Clear documentation provides a legal and clinical record of the nursing process in action. If an intervention or assessment is not documented, it is legally considered never to have been done.

Written notes and verbal handovers must be objective, timely, and concise. Nurses use formats like SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) or DAR (Data, Action, Response) to structure nursing notes. Effective handover communication ensures safe transitions of care between shifts, preventing critical patient details from getting lost.
