Review of Anatomy and Physiology of the Body Defence System

Introduction and Overview of the Body Defence System

The human body faces constant exposure to foreign invaders like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. To survive, the body relies on a coordinated defense network known as the immune system. This system differentiates between self and non-self cells, neutralizing anything that threatens homeostasis. It operates in overlapping layers, starting with physical barriers and escalating to highly specialized cellular responses if a pathogen breaches the outer defenses.

Anatomy of Primary and Secondary Lymphoid Organs

Lymphoid organs are categorized by their role in lymphocyte development and activation.

  • Primary Lymphoid Organs: The red bone marrow and thymus. These sites are where lymphocytes are formed and mature into immunocompetent cells. B-cells mature in the bone marrow, while T-cells migrate to and mature in the thymus.
  • Secondary Lymphoid Organs: The lymph nodes, spleen, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), and tonsils. These structures trap pathogens and provide the environment where mature lymphocytes encounter antigens and mount an immune response.

Cells of the Immune System and Their Functions

White blood cells, or leukocytes, drive the immune response. They originate from multipotent hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow.

  • Neutrophils: Highly active phagocytes that arrive first at the site of acute bacterial infection, forming the bulk of pus.
  • Macrophages: Versatile phagocytes derived from monocytes that engulf debris and present antigens to T-cells.
  • Lymphocytes: Comprise T-cells, B-cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, responsible for adaptive immunity and destroying virus-infected cells.
  • Eosinophils and Basophils: Combat parasitic infections and mediate allergic reactions through the release of inflammatory granules.

Innate Immunity: Barriers and Non-Specific Responses

Innate immunity is present from birth and acts immediately upon exposure to a pathogen without requiring prior sensitization.

Adaptive Immunity: Humoral and Cell-Mediated Pathways

Adaptive immunity develops over time and targets specific pathogens with high precision, leaving behind immunological memory.

  1. Humoral Immunity: Mediated by B-lymphocytes that mature into plasma cells. These cells secrete antibodies into the bloodstream and lymph to bind extracellular pathogens, neutralizing them or marking them for destruction.
  2. Cell-Mediated Immunity: Mediated by T-lymphocytes. Helper T-cells coordinate the response by releasing cytokines, while cytotoxic T-cells directly destroy host cells infected by viruses or mutated into cancerous growths.

The Complement System and Inflammatory Response

The complement system consists of a group of plasma proteins that circulate in an inactive state. When triggered by infection, they undergo a cascade reaction. This cascade enhances inflammation, attracts phagocytes through chemotaxis, coats pathogens to promote phagocytosis through opsonization, and forms membrane attack complexes that punch holes in bacterial cell membranes, causing them to lyse.

Inflammation itself is a localized vascular and cellular response. Vasodilation increases blood flow, bringing white blood cells to the injured tissue, resulting in the classic signs of redness, heat, swelling, and pain.

Physiological Regulation and Immune Tolerance

Immune tolerance is the ability of the immune system to recognize self-antigens and refrain from attacking the body's own tissues. Central tolerance eliminates self-reactive T and B cells during their maturation in primary organs. Peripheral tolerance suppresses any self-reactive cells that escape into circulation.

When these regulatory mechanisms fail, the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy host cells, resulting in autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis.

Clinical Relevance for Nursing Practice in Nigeria

Understanding immunological principles helps nurses manage infectious diseases prevalent in tropical regions, such as malaria, typhoid, and tuberculosis. Nurses monitor leukocyte differentials to evaluate infection severity. Immunization programs, such as the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in Nigeria, rely on inducing adaptive immunity and memory cell formation.

Compromised immunity from malnutrition, HIV, or immunosuppressive therapy increases patient vulnerability to opportunistic infections, requiring strict adherence to aseptic techniques during clinical procedures.
