Preventing Infection in the Hospital

Understanding Nosocomial Infections

Nosocomial infections, also known as hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), are infections that patients contract while receiving treatment in a healthcare facility. These infections were neither present nor incubating at the time of admission. Common sites include the urinary tract, surgical wounds, and the respiratory system. They significantly increase patient morbidity, prolong hospital stays, and raise healthcare costs across Nigerian hospitals. Pathogens often spread through contaminated hands of healthcare workers, medical devices, or the hospital environment.

The Chain of Infection in Healthcare

Infection transmission follows a continuous six-link chain. Breaking any single link stops the spread of disease.

  • Infectious Agent: Pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
  • Reservoir: Where the pathogen lives and multiplies, such as humans, equipment, or water.
  • Portal of Exit: How the pathogen leaves the reservoir, like respiratory droplets or blood.
  • Mode of Transmission: Direct contact, indirect contact, droplets, or airborne travel.
  • Portal of Entry: The route the pathogen enters a new host, including broken skin or mucous membranes.
  • Susceptible Host: A person lacking immunity or resistance to fight off the invading organism.

Standard Precautions for All Patients

Standard precautions form the foundational level of infection control. Nurses must apply them to every patient, regardless of their diagnosed or suspected infection status. These measures assume that every bodily fluid, secretion, and excretion—except sweat—may contain transmissible infectious agents.

Key components include:

  • Hand hygiene: Performed before and after every patient contact.
  • Personal protective equipment: Used whenever exposure to blood or fluids is anticipated.
  • Respiratory hygiene: Covering coughs and sneezes.
  • Safe injection practices: Preventing needle-stick injuries and reusing syringes.
  • Environmental cleaning: Ensuring shared equipment is properly disinfected.

Transmission-Based Precautions

When standard precautions alone are insufficient, nurses implement transmission-based precautions tailored to the specific route of pathogen spread.

  • Contact Precautions: Used for infections spread by direct or indirect contact with the patient or their environment, such as multi-resistant organisms. Requires gowns and gloves.
  • Droplet Precautions: Used for pathogens transmitted through large respiratory droplets generated during coughing or talking, such as influenza. Requires surgical masks.
  • Airborne Precautions: Used for tiny droplet nuclei that remain suspended in the air over long distances, such as tuberculosis. Requires N95 respirators and negative pressure rooms.

Proper Hand Hygiene Techniques and Timing

Hand hygiene is the single most effective measure to prevent cross-contamination in clinical settings. Nurses must perform hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water.

The World Health Organization outlines five key moments for hand hygiene: before touching a patient, before clean or aseptic procedures, after body fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient, and after touching patient surroundings.

Use of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and infectious materials. Proper sequencing during donning and doffing prevents self-contamination.

  • Donning order: Put on the gown first, followed by the mask or respirator, goggles or face shield, and finally gloves.
  • Doffing order: Remove gloves first, as they are the most contaminated, followed by goggles or face shield, gown, and mask last.

Gloves must never be washed or reused. Nurses should perform hand hygiene immediately after removing any piece of personal protective equipment.

Aseptic Technique and Catheter Care

Aseptic technique involves practices designed to keep clients and procedures free from microorganisms. It is critical during surgical procedures, wound dressings, and urinary catheter insertions. Catheter-associated urinary tract infections are among the most common hospital-acquired infections.

Prevention strategies include:

  • Inserting indwelling catheters only when strictly necessary using sterile technique.
  • Maintaining a closed drainage system at all times.
  • Keeping the collection bag below the level of the bladder to prevent backflow.
  • Performing regular perineal hygiene without using harsh antiseptics.

Environmental Sanitation and Waste Management

A clean hospital environment reduces the reservoir for infectious pathogens. Hospital surfaces, bedrails, and bedside tables must undergo routine disinfection using appropriate chlorine or chemical solutions.

Proper waste segregation protects healthcare workers and waste handlers:

  • General waste: Non-hazardous items like paper and food wrappers go into black bins.
  • Infectious waste: Items contaminated with blood or body fluids go into yellow infectious bins.
  • Sharps waste: Needles and scalpels go into puncture-resistant safety boxes without recapping.

The Role of the Nurse in Surveillance and Reporting

Nurses act as the first line of defense in tracking and stopping infection outbreaks. Surveillance involves monitoring patients for signs of infection, reviewing laboratory results, and tracking antibiotic usage patterns. When an infection cluster occurs, nurses must report the incident to the hospital infection control committee.

Accurate documentation of wound drainage, vital signs, and antibiotic administration times ensures that interprofessional teams can adjust care plans promptly. Early detection prevents localized infections from escalating into systemic sepsis.
