Family Planning

Introduction and Maternal-Child Benefits

Family planning allows individuals and couples to anticipate and attain their desired number of children, alongside the spacing and timing of their births. This practice is achieved through the use of contraceptive methods and the treatment of involuntary infertility.

Promoting birth spacing directly improves health outcomes. Pregnancies spaced less than two years apart carry a higher risk of infant mortality, premature birth, and low birth weight. Mothers also benefit significantly, as adequate recovery time reduces the incidence of maternal depletion syndrome, uterine rupture, and postpartum hemorrhage. In regions with high maternal mortality rates like Nigeria, empowering women with family planning choices serves as a foundational preventive health intervention.

Natural Family Planning and Fertility Awareness

Natural methods rely on identifying the fertile days of the menstrual cycle through biological signs. These techniques require high motivation, regular cycles, and consistent record-keeping.

  • Calendar Rhythm Method: Tracking cycle lengths over several months to estimate ovulation, avoiding unprotected sex during calculated fertile windows.
  • Cervical Mucus Method: Observing changes in vaginal secretions, which become clear, stretchy, and slippery like raw egg white around ovulation.
  • Basal Body Temperature Method: Measuring resting body temperature every morning before rising; a slight sustained rise indicates ovulation has occurred.
  • Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM): A temporary method based on exclusive breastfeeding, which suppresses ovulation during the first six months postpartum.

Barrier Methods and Dual Protection

Barrier contraceptives physically block sperm from reaching the ovum. They include male and female condoms, diaphragms, and cervical caps.

Condoms hold a unique status in public health because they provide dual protection—preventing both unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV. Nurses must emphasize proper usage, such as checking expiration dates, avoiding oil-based lubricants with latex, and ensuring correct withdrawal techniques after ejaculation to prevent spills.

Hormonal Contraceptives

Hormonal methods use synthetic estrogen, progestin, or progestin alone to suppress ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, and alter the endometrium.

  • Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs): Daily pills containing estrogen and progestin; contraindicated in smokers over 35 and women with a history of thromboembolism.
  • Progestin-Only Pills (POPs): Safe for breastfeeding mothers and those with estrogen contraindications, but must be taken at the exact same time daily.
  • Injectables: Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) given intramuscularly or subcutaneously every three months.
  • Implants: Small rods inserted under the skin of the upper arm providing long-acting progestin release for three to five years.

Intrauterine Devices and Permanent Methods

Long-Acting Reversible Contraception (LARC) and permanent procedures offer high efficacy rates with minimal daily user compliance.

Intrauterine devices (IUDs) include copper-bearing devices, which impair sperm motility through a local inflammatory reaction, and hormonal IUDs, which thicken cervical mucus and thin the uterine lining. Both types offer years of continuous protection.

Surgical sterilization permanently blocks gamete transport. Tubal ligation seals the fallopian tubes in females, while a vasectomy seizes and cuts the vas deferens in males. Nurses must ensure clients understand that surgical sterilization is irreversible and requires thorough informed consent.

Client Assessment and Counseling

Effective contraceptive counseling requires a client-centered, non-judgmental approach. Nurses use the Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (MEC) guidelines provided by the World Health Organization to screen for contraindications.

Assessment involves taking a thorough obstetric, medical, and social history, checking blood pressure, and discussing personal reproductive goals. Counseling must address potential side effects before method initiation to reduce premature discontinuation. For example, informing a client about temporary irregular bleeding with implants prevents unwarranted anxiety and abandonment of the method.

Addressing Myths and Cultural Barriers

In many Nigerian communities, family planning uptake faces cultural and religious hurdles. Common myths claim that contraceptives cause permanent infertility, cause birth defects, or encourage promiscuity among young women.

Nurses play a vital role as health educators by engaging community leaders, husbands, and religious figures. Dispelling these myths requires culturally sensitive dialogue, patience, and clear, simple explanations. Involving male partners in counseling sessions often dispels fears and improves household support for reproductive health choices.
