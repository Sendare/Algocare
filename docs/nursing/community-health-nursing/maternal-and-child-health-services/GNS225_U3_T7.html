Home Delivery

Introduction to Home Delivery in Community Health

Home delivery refers to childbirth occurring outside of a formal medical facility, usually within a private residence attended by traditional birth attendants, relatives, or skilled birth attendants. In many parts of Nigeria, home deliveries are common due to cultural preferences, financial constraints, long distances to health facilities, and perceived mistreatment in hospitals. Midwives and community health extension workers play a vital role in educating communities about the risks and benefits associated with home births, ensuring that when deliveries happen at home, they meet basic safety standards.

Indications and Contraindications for Home Births

A planned home birth is only suitable for low-risk pregnancies with a single fetus in a vertex presentation. The mother must have normal vital signs and a history of uncomplicated pregnancies.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Previous Caesarean section, malpresentation like breech or transverse lie, multiple gestation, severe pre-eclampsia, antepartum hemorrhage, and medical disorders such as severe cardiac disease.
  • Indication for Transfer: Failure to progress in labor, meconium-stained liquor, maternal fever, or prolonged rupture of membranes exceeding 18 hours.

Pre-Natal Preparation and Risk Assessment

Preparation for a safe home delivery begins months before labor starts. Community health workers must conduct thorough risk assessments during antenatal care visits to screen out high-risk cases. Birth preparedness includes identifying a clean room, securing financial resources for emergencies, arranging reliable transportation, and sourcing a clean delivery kit. Pregnant women are also taught to recognize danger signs such as severe headaches, blurring of vision, vaginal bleeding, and reduced fetal movements.

Essential Equipment and Clean Delivery Kit

The WHO clean delivery kit strategy aims to reduce neonatal and maternal infections by promoting the 'six cleans'.

  • Clean hands: Washed thoroughly with soap and clean water.
  • Clean surface: A plastic sheet or clean mat where the mother can lie.
  • Clean cord cutting instrument: A new, boiled, or sterile blade.
  • Clean cord tie: Clean thread to ligate the umbilical cord.
  • Clean cord care: Keeping the cord stump dry without applying harmful substances like saliva, cow dung, or toothpaste.
  • Clean cloth: Warm, dry wrappers for the newborn.

Intrapartum Monitoring and Management

Managing labor at home requires continuous vigilance. Skilled birth attendants monitor maternal pulse, blood pressure, and uterine contractions. The progress of labor is tracked to identify dystocia early. Fetal heart rate is auscultated regularly using a fetal stethoscope or Doppler, especially immediately after a contraction. Attendants must encourage maternal hydration, bladder emptying, and freedom of movement while avoiding harmful practices like aggressive fundal pressure or routine oxytocin administration without medical supervision.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation

Immediately after birth, the baby is placed on the mother's abdomen for skin-to-skin contact. The airway is cleared gently if needed, and the infant is dried thoroughly to prevent hypothermia. The APGAR score is assessed at one and five minutes. If a newborn fails to breathe spontaneously, basic resuscitation must start within the golden minute: clear the airway, stimulate the baby by rubbing the back, and provide positive pressure ventilation with a self-inflating bag and mask if trained and equipped.

Postpartum Care for Mother and Baby

The immediate postpartum period carries high risks, particularly from postpartum hemorrhage. Birth attendants must monitor uterine contraction and vaginal bleeding closely for at least two hours after delivery. Active management of the third stage of labor, including oxytocin administration where available, helps prevent excessive bleeding. Breastfeeding should be initiated within the first hour of life. Both mother and baby need scheduled postnatal check-ups to monitor involution of the uterus, check lochia, assess infant feeding, and provide essential immunizations.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Referral Systems

Despite careful planning, emergencies can arise unexpectedly during a home delivery. Common crises include postpartum hemorrhage, retained placenta, eclamptic fits, obstructed labor, and severe neonatal asphyxia. Community birth attendants must recognize these complications instantly without delay. A functional emergency referral system involves pre-arranged transport, a designated receiving hospital with blood banking and surgical capabilities, and accompanying the family with clear documentation of events that occurred during labor.
