Midwife as a Member of the Health Team

Introduction to the Midwife's Role in Healthcare

The midwife functions as an autonomous and accountable professional who works in partnership with women to give the necessary support, care, and advice during pregnancy, labour, and the postpartum period. Within the Nigerian healthcare system, midwives form the backbone of maternal and child health services. They promote normal birth, detect complications in mother and child, access medical care or other appropriate assistance, and carry out emergency measures. Understanding this role requires viewing the midwife not in isolation, but as a primary healthcare provider whose scope directly impacts maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality indices across rural and urban settings.

The Healthcare Team Structure in Nigeria

Healthcare delivery in Nigeria operates across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, each featuring distinct multidisciplinary teams. At the primary health care (PHC) level, the team typically includes community health extension workers (CHEWs), primary care nurses, and midwives working under the supervision of a local government health authority. In secondary and tertiary facilities, general practitioners, obstetricians, pediatricians, anesthetists, pharmacists, and laboratory scientists join the fold. The midwife acts as a vital bridge in this hierarchy, often initiating the first point of contact and coordinating patient transfers upward when specialized intervention becomes necessary.

Collaborative Practice and Interprofessional Communication

Effective teamwork relies heavily on mutual respect, clear role boundaries, and open communication channels among all health professionals. Midwives frequently interact with doctors, nurses, and laboratory staff to ensure seamless clinical continuity. Poor communication remains a leading root cause of adverse maternal events, often resulting in delayed resuscitations or duplicated efforts. Utilizing structured handover tools, such as the Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR) format, helps eliminate ambiguity during patient transfers or shift changes.

Community Engagement and Primary Health Care Integration

Clinical competence inside hospital walls must be matched by active engagement with the surrounding community. Midwives collaborate with local leaders, traditional birth attendants, and village health committees to encourage antenatal booking and hospital delivery. Integrating services at the grassroots level helps dismantle cultural barriers and transportation delays that often trap women in remote areas. Outreach clinics, immunization drives, and health talks form the daily routine of a community-integrated midwife.

Referral Systems and Managing Obstetric Emergencies

Obstetric emergencies such as eclampsia, ruptured uterus, and severe hemorrhage require rapid recognition and coordinated transport. The midwife serves as the primary decision-maker who triggers the emergency referral chain. A functioning referral protocol requires pre-notification of the receiving facility, availability of emergency transport, and accompanying documentation that outlines the interventions already administered. Delays at any point in this chain significantly worsen maternal survival statistics.

Ethical, Legal, and Professional Standards

The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) regulates professional practice, setting boundaries that protect the public and define legal liability. Midwives must practice within their approved scope, maintain patient confidentiality, and secure informed consent before initiating any procedure. Ethical dilemmas frequently arise when cultural preferences clash with medical necessity, requiring the midwife to advocate fiercely for the mother and unborn child while respecting personal autonomy where legally permissible.

Overcoming Challenges in Team-Based Maternal Care

Practicing midwifery within Nigerian health teams often involves navigating systemic barriers such as staff shortages, erratic power supplies, stock-outs of essential drugs like oxytocin, and inter-professional friction. Professional hierarchies can sometimes silence junior midwives when speaking up for patient safety. Overcoming these hurdles demands resilience, resourcefulness, continuous professional development, and strong advocacy skills to demand better health infrastructure and collaborative working environments.
