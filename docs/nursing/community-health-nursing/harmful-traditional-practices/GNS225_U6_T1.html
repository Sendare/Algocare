Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Internationally recognized by the World Health Organization, the practice is categorized into four distinct types. It is traditionally performed by traditional circumcisers using unsterilized instruments such as blades, knives, or broken glass, often without anesthesia. While international human rights frameworks classify the procedure as a violation of the human rights of girls and women, local proponents often view it as a rite of passage into womanhood or a measure to ensure chastity and marriageability.

Types and Classifications of FGM

Understanding the World Health Organization classification helps nurses accurately document findings during physical examinations.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Unclassified): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Cultural Drivers and Societal Perceptions in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, the persistence of FGM is fueled by deeply entrenched cultural norms, patriarchal structures, and social conformity. Families often participate in the practice out of fear of social ostracization, stigmatization, or the belief that an uncircumcised girl is promiscuous or unclean. Traditional birth attendants and older female relatives frequently perpetuate these beliefs, passing down the practice through generations. Addressing these drivers requires culturally sensitive community engagement rather than outright condemnation, which often drives the practice underground.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Complications

The physical toll of FGM spans from acute surgical trauma to chronic, lifelong morbidities. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, acute urine retention, and localized infections from non-sterile cutting tools. Over time, survivors frequently suffer from chronic pelvic infections, dysmenorrhea, scarring, keloid formation, and painful sexual intercourse. Obstetric complications are also profound, notably prolonged labor, obstetric fistula, perineal tearing, and increased rates of perinatal mortality due to obstructed delivery.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impact

Beyond physical scars, survivors often bear hidden psychological and psychosexual burdens. Anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and chronic body dysmorphia are prevalent among affected women. The removal of or damage to sensitive nerve endings frequently leads to diminished sexual responsiveness, dyspareunia, and an inability to achieve orgasm. These intimate struggles can strain marital relationships and contribute to profound social isolation and emotional distress.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion and provide a safe, confidential environment to uncover a history of FGM. During routine admission or obstetric histories, nurses should use gentle, non-judgmental language when inquiring about reproductive health. Physical assessments require clear explanation and patient consent, noting any anatomical alterations, scarring, or asymmetry of the external genitalia. Establishing trust is essential, as many women feel deep shame or reluctance to discuss the practice due to cultural taboos.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Advocacy

Community health nurses play a pivotal role in eradicating FGM through education, advocacy, and collaboration with local leaders. Nurses work alongside traditional birth attendants, village chiefs, and youth groups to promote dialogue about the health hazards of the practice. Rather than imposing outside values, effective interventions empower local champions to lead advocacy campaigns. Additionally, nurses provide crucial support to survivors through counseling, clinical management of complications, and referral networks.

Legal Frameworks and Policies Against FGM in Nigeria

At the national level, Nigeria enacted the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act in 2015, which explicitly prohibits female genital mutilation and prescribes penalties for offenders. Several states have also domesticated this law or enacted state-specific edicts banning the practice. Despite these legal protections, enforcement remains weak due to underreporting, fear of community backlash, and a preference for traditional resolution mechanisms. Nurses must be familiar with these legal frameworks to support protective services and collaborate with child welfare agencies when minors are at risk.
