Medicalisation of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation and Medicalisation

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Historically, these procedures were performed by traditional practitioners using crude, unsterilized instruments. Medicalisation of FGM occurs when any form of FGM is performed by health care personnel, whether in a public or private clinic, at home, or anywhere else. This practice represents a dangerous shift where clinical skills and sterile equipment are co-opted to legitimize a human rights violation. Healthcare professionals who perform FGM violate core ethical oaths to do no harm, mistakenly believing that involving a trained nurse or doctor makes the procedure safe.

Drivers and Misconceptions of Medicalised FGM

The persistence of medicalised FGM is fueled by deep-seated cultural norms, social pressure, and persistent myths. Communities often pressure healthcare providers to perform the procedure to reduce the severe physical risks associated with traditional practitioners.

  • The Safety Myth: A widespread misconception is that clinical settings eliminate all health complications.
  • Hygiene Beliefs: Practitioners believe sterile blades and antibiotics prevent infections completely.
  • Harm Reduction Fallacy: Some health workers erroneously argue that performing a symbolic 'nick' or minor cut prevents families from seeking severe traditional mutilation.

Despite these justifications, medicalised FGM remains a violation of bodily integrity and medical ethics, regardless of how sterile the environment is.

Types of Procedures in Clinical Settings

Healthcare workers perform various classifications of FGM, ranging from symbolic cuts to severe anatomical alterations.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora.
  • Type IV (Pricking/Incision): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

In clinical settings, health workers may perform any of these types, often using local anesthesia and surgical scissors.

Public Health Implications and Complications

Even when performed in clean clinics with antibiotics, FGM causes profound immediate and long-term physical, psychological, and sexual health complications.

  • Immediate risks: Severe pain, excessive hemorrhage, shock, and acute urinary retention.
  • Long-term consequences: Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, and painful sexual intercourse (dyspareunia).
  • Obstetric hazards: Difficult labor, increased rates of caesarean section, postpartum hemorrhage, and higher risks of neonatal death.
  • Psychological trauma: Anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and chronic distrust of healthcare providers.

Clean surroundings do not eliminate the anatomical and psychological devastation inflicted by these procedures.

Ethical, Legal, and Professional Frameworks for Nurses

Nursing codes of ethics and international human rights law strictly prohibit healthcare professionals from performing or supporting FGM. The World Health Organization (WHO) and nursing regulatory bodies across Africa classify medicalised FGM as professional misconduct. When a nurse engages in this practice, they violate the fundamental duty to protect patient welfare and uphold bodily integrity. Legal frameworks in many nations carry severe penalties, including revocation of nursing licenses and imprisonment. Professional accountability requires nurses to reject community demands for cutting and report any planned or performed procedures to appropriate authorities.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Prevention

Community health nurses occupy a front-line position in eradicating all forms of FGM through education, advocacy, and community engagement. Effective prevention strategies rely on participatory dialogues with traditional leaders, mothers, and youth to demystify the myths surrounding female genitalia. Nurses must collaborate with local opinion makers to design culturally sensitive health promotion campaigns. Outreach programs should emphasize that medicalisation does not equal safety. By empowering women and girls with knowledge about their anatomical rights, nurses help dismantle the social structures that perpetuate the practice.
