Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation and Classifications

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) involves procedures that intentionally alter or cause injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Deeply rooted in cultural, religious, and social traditions across parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, the practice is internationally recognized as a violation of human rights. According to the World Health Organization, FGM is categorized into four main types:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Unclassified): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterizing.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The immediate aftermath of FGM frequently presents as a surgical emergency in clinical settings. Because procedures are traditionally performed by birth attendants or local practitioners using unsterilized blades, scissors, or broken glass without anesthesia, severe trauma is inevitable.

Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage from the rich vascular supply of the clitoral and labial arteries, which can lead to hypovolemic shock. Excruciating acute pain triggers vasovagal syncope. Accidental injury to adjacent structures such as the urethra, vaginal wall, or rectum is common. Furthermore, the use of crude instruments often leads to severe localized tissue swelling, acute urinary retention due to pain or mechanical obstruction, and immediate bacterial contamination.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Women living with the long-term consequences of FGM experience chronic physical morbidity that affects daily life and reproductive health. Scar tissue formation, keloid development, and labial adhesions can obstruct normal anatomical pathways.

  • Chronic Urological Issues: Recurrent urinary tract infections occur due to distorted anatomy, obstructed urinary flow, and trapped secretions. Chronic dysuria and calculus formation are also prevalent.
  • Gynecological Complications: Chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea from trapped menstrual blood (hematocolpos), painful intercourse (dyspareunia), and the development of inclusion dermoid cysts along suture or scar lines are frequent clinical presentations.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Labor and delivery present immense risks for women who have undergone FGM, particularly Type III infibulation. The rigid scar tissue lacks normal elasticity, failing to dilate adequately during the second stage of labor.

This obstruction frequently results in prolonged and obstructed labor, leading to uterine rupture, fetal distress, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, and stillbirth. Women with infibulation often require surgical deroofing or an anterior episiotomy (defibulation) during labor to allow delivery. Furthermore, the risk of severe postpartum hemorrhage and perineal lacerations extending into the anal sphincter is significantly elevated.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impacts

The psychological trauma associated with FGM begins at the moment of the procedure and persists across the lifespan. The experience of betrayal, physical restraint, and intense pain during childhood leaves deep emotional scars.

Many survivors experience chronic anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and persistent feelings of incompleteness or low self-esteem. Psychosexually, the removal of or damage to sensitive erectile tissue, combined with physical scarring and pain, leads to diminished sexual desire, difficulty achieving orgasm, and profound marital distress.

Infectious Risks Including HIV and Tetanus

Infectious complications are a major source of morbidity and mortality following FGM. Because cutting instruments are frequently reused across multiple individuals without sterilization, blood-borne pathogens are easily transmitted.

  • Tetanus: Clostridium tetani spores readily contaminate crude instruments and dressings applied to wounds, leading to high mortality rates from neonatal or adult lockjaw.
  • Blood-Borne Infections: The sharing of cutting tools increases the transmission risk of Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).
  • Local Infections: Abscess formation, cellulitis, and sepsis commonly develop due to poor wound care and the application of traditional substances like herbs, ash, or dung to stop bleeding.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses play a critical role in identifying women and girls who have undergone or are at risk for FGM. Clinical assessment requires a sensitive, non-judgmental approach to build trust, as patients may feel deep shame or fear legal repercussions.

During routine physical examinations, pelvic assessments, or antenatal bookings, nurses should observe for anatomical absence of the clitoris, labial fusion, scarring, or restrictive scar tissue. Nurses must document findings objectively without using stigmatizing language. Assessment must also encompass screening for chronic pain, urinary symptoms, psychological distress, and evaluating readiness for supportive counseling or specialized surgical referral.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Prevention Strategies

Prevention of FGM requires comprehensive community-level interventions led by public health and community health nurses. Nurses work closely with traditional leaders, religious figures, mothers, and youth groups to dismantle the myths supporting the practice.

Interventions focus on health education regarding anatomical facts, infection risks, and the violation of human rights. Nurses advocate for alternative initiation rites (rites of passage without physical cutting) that preserve positive cultural heritage while abandoning harmful procedures. Furthermore, nurses provide direct support, counseling, and rehabilitation services for survivors while strengthening referral networks for legal and psychological protection.
