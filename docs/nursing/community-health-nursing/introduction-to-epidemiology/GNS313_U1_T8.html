Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and the Immune System

Immunity is the body's physiological capability to defend against harmful pathogens, foreign substances, and abnormal cells. The human immune system relies on a complex network of cells, tissues, and organs that coordinate to maintain internal health. Primary lymphoid organs, such as the bone marrow and thymus, are responsible for the production and maturation of lymphocytes. Secondary lymphoid organs, including the spleen, lymph nodes, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), serve as sites where immune cells encounter antigens and launch targeted responses. White blood cells, particularly leukocytes, circulate through blood and lymphatic vessels to patrol the body for signs of infection. When a breach occurs, chemical signals summon these defensive cells to the site of injury or pathogen entry. A robust immune system distinguishes between self and non-self molecules, a crucial feature that prevents the body from attacking its own healthy tissues.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

The human body defends itself using two complementary defense systems: innate and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity is present from birth, offering immediate, non-specific protection against a wide range of pathogens. Physical barriers like intact skin and mucous membranes, alongside chemical secretions such as stomach acid and lysozyme in tears, form the first line of innate defense. If these barriers fail, cellular components like phagocytes, natural killer cells, and inflammatory pathways act rapidly to contain the threat.

Adaptive immunity develops over time through exposure to specific antigens, providing tailored protection and immunological memory. This branch involves specialized lymphocytes that mount a delayed but highly potent response.

Active Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Active immunity occurs when the body's own immune system is stimulated to produce antibodies and activate lymphocytes. This type of immunity provides long-lasting, sometimes lifelong, protection due to the generation of memory cells.

  • Natural Active Immunity: Acquired when an individual contracts a living pathogen, experiences the clinical or subclinical infection, and recovers. The immune system mounts a primary response and retains memory of the specific antigen.
  • Artificial Active Immunity: Acquired through the administration of vaccines containing weakened, killed, or purified parts of a pathogen. This exposure triggers an immune response without causing the actual disease, preparing the body for future encounters.

Passive Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Passive immunity involves the direct transfer of pre-formed antibodies into an individual's body. Because the recipient's immune system does not actively produce these antibodies, protection is immediate but temporary, typically lasting only a few weeks or months as the antibodies degrade.

  • Natural Passive Immunity: Observed when maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta to the foetus during pregnancy, and when IgA antibodies are passed to an infant through breast milk, especially colostrum.
  • Artificial Passive Immunity: Delivered through the injection of immune globulins or antisera harvested from human or animal donors, commonly used for rapid post-exposure prophylaxis against fast-acting toxins or infections like tetanus or rabies.

Herd Immunity and Its Epidemiological Significance

Herd immunity, or community immunity, describes a form of indirect protection from infectious disease that occurs when a large percentage of a population has become immune—either through previous infections or vaccinations—thereby providing a measure of protection for individuals who are not immune. When herd immunity thresholds are reached, the chain of transmission is broken, making it difficult for the pathogen to spread within the community. This concept is vital for protecting vulnerable populations who cannot receive vaccines, such as newborns, severely immunocompromised individuals, or people with severe allergies to vaccine components. The threshold required to achieve herd immunity varies depending on how contagious a disease is, measured by its basic reproduction number. Maintaining high vaccination coverage is essential to sustain herd immunity and prevent resurgences of vaccine-preventable outbreaks.

Factors Influencing Immune Responses in Populations

A population's collective ability to mount an effective immune response is shaped by multiple interconnected biological, environmental, and social determinants. Nutritional status plays a fundamental role; protein-energy malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, such as vitamin A, zinc, and iron, impair lymphocyte function and antibody production. Age is another critical variable, as neonates possess immature immune systems, while older adults experience immunosenescence, a gradual deterioration of immune competence. Chronic illnesses, persistent parasitic infections, and immunosuppressive diseases like HIV weaken the immune machinery. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, including poverty, overcrowding, and inadequate sanitation, increase pathogen exposure while limiting access to healthcare services.

Immunization Programs and Cold Chain Management in Nigeria

Routine immunization is a cornerstone of primary healthcare delivery in Nigeria, managed through frameworks like the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI). A major operational challenge in tropical regions is maintaining the cold chain—a temperature-controlled supply chain necessary to keep vaccines potent from the point of manufacture to the point of administration. Most vaccines, including the oral polio, measles, and pentavalent vaccines, require strict storage temperatures between +2°C and +8°C, while oral polio vaccine can often tolerate freezing temperatures in specific storage tiers. Failures in cold chain logistics, such as erratic electricity supply, fuel shortages for refrigerators, or broken transport coolers, lead to heat exposure that irreversibly damages vaccine potency. Community health nurses must regularly monitor temperature logs, check vaccine vial monitors, and adhere strictly to cold chain protocols during outreach sessions.

Community Health Nursing Roles in Vaccine Advocacy and Delivery

Community health nurses serve as the vital link between health systems and local populations in promoting immunization uptake. Nurses combat vaccine hesitancy by engaging community leaders, addressing cultural myths, and providing transparent, evidence-based education during antenatal visits and home health visits. Beyond advocacy, nurses plan and execute immunization sessions, manage vaccine inventories, and ensure safe injection practices and proper biomedical waste disposal. Active surveillance for adverse events following immunization (AEFI) is another key nursing responsibility, requiring prompt identification, management, and reporting of any unusual post-vaccination reactions to maintain public trust in immunization programs.
