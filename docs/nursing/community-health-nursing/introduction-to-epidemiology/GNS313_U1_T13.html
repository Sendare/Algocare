Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

A communicable disease is an illness caused by a specific infectious agent or its toxic products that arises through transmission of that agent from an infected person, animal, or inanimate reservoir to a susceptible host. In Nigeria, the burden of communicable diseases remains a major public health challenge, driven by environmental factors, poverty, and gaps in public health infrastructure. Endemic diseases such as malaria, tuberculosis, Lassa fever, and cholera place a continuous strain on healthcare facilities across both rural and urban settlements.

Tropical climates and seasonal shifts heavily influence disease patterns in the region. For instance, the dry harmattan season often triggers outbreaks of cerebrospinal meningitis in the northern states, while heavy rainy seasons frequently facilitate waterborne disease transmission in areas with compromised sanitation. Community health nurses must understand these local epidemiological trends to anticipate outbreaks and educate vulnerable populations effectively.

The Epidemiological Triangle and Chain of Infection

Understanding how infections spread requires examining the epidemiological triangle, which consists of three core components: the agent, the host, and the environment. The agent is the microorganism responsible for the disease, such as a bacterium, virus, fungus, or parasite. The host is the human or animal that harbors the infectious agent. The environment includes all external factors—such as sanitation, climate, and housing density—that allow the agent to move toward the host.

The chain of infection details the specific steps an infectious agent takes to move from a reservoir to a new host:

  1. Infectious Agent: The pathogen capable of causing disease.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the agent lives and multiplies, such as humans, animals, soil, or water.
  3. Portal of Exit: The path by which the agent leaves the reservoir, like the respiratory tract, blood, or gastrointestinal tract.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen travels, whether through direct contact, air droplets, vectors like mosquitoes, or vehicles like contaminated food.
  5. Portal of Entry: The site through which the agent enters the susceptible host, such as broken skin or mucous membranes.
  6. Susceptible Host: An individual lacking immunity or resistance to ward off the pathogen.

Classification and Modes of Disease Transmission

Pathogens spread through distinct pathways that dictate how public health interventions are designed. Transmission routes are broadly categorized into direct and indirect mechanisms.

  • Direct Transmission: Involves immediate physical transfer of the pathogen from an infected individual to a susceptible host through kissing, sexual intercourse, skin-to-skin contact, or droplet spread over short distances.
  • Indirect Transmission: Occurs through an intermediary object or living creature, which includes:
  • Vehicles: Inanimate objects like contaminated water, food, surgical instruments, or bedding.
  • Vectors: Living organisms, predominantly insects like mosquitoes and tics, that carry pathogens from one host to another.
  • Airborne: Fine droplet nuclei suspended in the air over longer distances, commonly seen in tuberculosis transmission.

Stages of the Natural History of Disease

Every communicable disease follows a predictable timeline within the human body, known as the natural history of disease. Recognizing these phases helps nurses determine appropriate clinical interventions.

StageDescriptionClinical Focus
Pre-pathogenesisBefore agent interacts with hostPrimary prevention and health promotion
PathogenesisAgent enters host and multipliesSecondary prevention and early diagnosis
Clinical DiseaseManifestation of signs and symptomsTreatment, nursing care, and tertiary prevention
ResolutionRecovery, disability, or deathRehabilitation and supportive care

The incubation period sits within the pathogenesis stage, representing the time between exposure to a pathogen and the appearance of the first symptoms. This window is critical for contact tracing because infected individuals may feel healthy while shedding the virus or bacteria.

Epidemiological Surveillance and Outbreak Investigation

Epidemiological surveillance is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential to the planning, implementation, and evaluation of public health practice. In Nigeria, the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework guides health workers in tracking priority diseases. Surveillance data allows health authorities to spot unusual disease spikes and declare outbreaks early.

When an outbreak occurs, community health nurses often participate in formal investigations following standardized steps:

  1. Confirm the existence of an outbreak and verify the diagnosis.
  2. Establish a case definition to identify who is affected.
  3. Conduct descriptive epidemiology to find out who, where, and when cases occur.
  4. Formulate and test hypotheses regarding the source of infection.
  5. Implement control and prevention measures immediately.
  6. Communicate findings through official public health reports.

Prevention and Control Strategies in Community Health

Controlling communicable diseases relies on a layered defense model divided into three main levels of prevention.

Primary prevention stops a disease before it ever occurs. Key interventions include childhood immunization schedules, health education on handwashing, vector control through insecticide-treated bed nets, and environmental sanitation improvements like proper waste disposal.

Secondary prevention focuses on early diagnosis and prompt treatment to halt disease progression and prevent complications. Mass screening campaigns for tuberculosis, rapid diagnostic tests for malaria, and contact tracing for infectious contacts fall under this tier.

Tertiary prevention reduces the impact of an established disease by minimizing complications and restoring function. For example, physical rehabilitation for polio survivors or strict adherence counseling for patients living with HIV help manage chronic manifestations and improve quality of life.

Role of the Community Health Nurse in Disease Management

The community health nurse acts as a vital link between healthcare systems and local populations. In the management of communicable diseases, nurses execute multiple roles, including clinician, educator, advocate, and epidemiologist.

Nurses conduct community diagnoses, administer vaccines, treat uncomplicated cases using local standing orders, and monitor patient adherence to treatment regimens. Beyond direct care, they address cultural myths and misconceptions surrounding disease transmission. For instance, when managing outbreaks like cholera or Lassa fever, nurses must counter dangerous traditional beliefs with clear, culturally sensitive health education regarding hygiene, safe water use, and rodent control. Empowering communities through participatory health talks ensures sustained behavioral change.
