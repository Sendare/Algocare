Source of Infection

The Chain of Infection and Sources

Infection spreads through a continuous sequence known as the chain of infection. Every link must be intact for disease to transmit from one person to another. The starting point of this chain is the source of infection, which includes both the reservoir and the specific site where the pathogen lives, grows, and multiplies. Understanding this chain helps nurses interrupt transmission in hospital wards and rural community health centres alike.

Reservoirs Versus Sources of Infection

While often used interchangeably in casual conversation, reservoirs and sources have distinct epidemiological definitions.

  • Reservoir: The natural habitat where an infectious agent normally lives, multiplies, and grows. This can be humans, animals, soil, or water.
  • Source: The immediate site or object from which the infectious agent is directly transmitted to a host.

For example, soil is the reservoir for *Clostridium tetani*, but a contaminated rusty nail is the source of infection for a patient with tetanus.

Human Sources: Cases and Carriers

Humans are the most frequent source of infection for other human beings. Human sources are broadly categorized into clinical cases and carriers.

  • Clinical Cases: Individuals showing active signs and symptoms of a disease. They shed high numbers of pathogens and are easily identified.
  • Convalescent Carriers: People recovering from an illness who continue to harbor and shed the pathogen.
  • Healthy Carriers: Individuals who harbor the infectious agent without ever developing symptoms, making them dangerous silent spreaders in the community.

Animal and Environmental Reservoirs

Many infections originate from non-human reservoirs. Zoonotic diseases pass naturally from vertebrate animals to humans. Examples include rabies from dogs and Lassa fever from multimammate rats common in parts of West Africa. Environmental reservoirs include soil, water, and decaying organic matter. Water sources contaminated with faeces frequently serve as environmental sources for cholera outbreaks during the rainy season.

Tracing and Identifying Sources in Clinical Practice

When a disease outbreak occurs in a ward or community, public health nurses and epidemiologists must trace the source to stop transmission. This process involves case finding, interviewing patients about their movements and contacts, and collecting laboratory specimens. Identifying the index case—the first identified case in a group of related cases—often points directly to the primary source of infection.

Controlling the Source

The most effective way to control an infectious disease is to target its source. Nursing interventions focus on eliminating or neutralizing the source whenever possible. Treatment of infected individuals with antibiotics neutralizes human cases. Isolation prevents contact with susceptible hosts. Environmental sanitation, safe waste disposal, and vector control destroy non-human sources and break the chain of infection at its root.
