Occupational Health Services

Introduction to Occupational Health Services

Occupational Health Services (OHS) focus on the promotion and maintenance of the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations. These services protect workers from employment risks, adapt the working environment to human capabilities, and place workers in positions suited to their physiological and psychological abilities. In Nigeria, the Factories Act and the International Labour Organization (ILO) conventions provide the foundational mandate for establishing these services across industrial, agricultural, and service sectors.

Identification and Assessment of Workplace Hazards

Workplace hazards are conditions or situations that have the potential to cause harm to a worker's health or safety. Systematic hazard identification involves regular walk-through inspections, job safety analyses, and reviewing injury logs. Hazards are generally grouped into four major categories:

  • Physical hazards: Noise, vibration, extreme temperatures, and ionizing or non-ionizing radiation.
  • Chemical hazards: Dusts, fumes, gases, vapors, and toxic solvents handled during production or cleaning.
  • Biological hazards: Bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites encountered by healthcare workers or agricultural laborers.
  • Ergonomic and Psychosocial hazards: Repetitive movements, awkward postures, heavy lifting, high work demands, and workplace stress.

Occupational Health Surveillance and Medical Examinations

Health surveillance involves the ongoing collection, analysis, and dissemination of health data related to the workplace. It ensures early detection of occupational diseases before irreversible damage occurs. Medical examinations are categorized into:

  1. Pre-placement examinations: Establish baseline health status and ensure a worker is medically fit for specific job demands.
  2. Periodic examinations: Monitor workers exposed to specific hazards over time, such as audiometry for factory workers exposed to high noise levels.
  3. Return-to-work examinations: Assess an employee's readiness to resume duties after a prolonged illness or injury.

Prevention and Control Measures in the Workplace

Controlling workplace hazards relies on the hierarchy of controls, a system used in industry to minimize or eliminate exposure to hazards. Employers must prioritize these measures from the most effective to the least effective:

  • Elimination: Physically removing the hazard entirely from the workplace.
  • Substitution: Replacing hazardous materials or processes with safer alternatives.
  • Engineering controls: Isolating workers from hazards using physical barriers, machine guards, or ventilation systems.
  • Administrative controls: Changing work practices, rotating shifts, or implementing training to reduce exposure duration.
  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Providing respirators, earplugs, gloves, and safety boots as a last line of defense.

Management of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses

Despite rigorous preventive measures, workplace injuries and occupational illnesses still occur and require prompt, structured management. Immediate first aid must be administered on-site by trained personnel to stabilize the injured worker and prevent complications. Severe cases necessitate rapid referral to secondary or tertiary health facilities. Furthermore, thorough incident investigation must follow every accident to uncover root causes—such as equipment failure or lack of supervision—rather than merely assigning blame. Accurate record-keeping of all incidents is mandatory for regulatory compliance and epidemiological tracking.

Worker Education, Training, and Ergonomics

Empowering workers with knowledge is a cornerstone of occupational health. Regular safety toolbox talks, hazard communication training, and proper demonstration of equipment usage significantly reduce careless errors. Ergonomics complements training by designing workstations, tools, and tasks to match human anatomical and physiological limits. Poor ergonomic design leads to musculoskeletal disorders, chronic back pain, and fatigue. Nurses teach correct lifting techniques, encourage micro-breaks, and advocate for adjustable seating and tool handles.

The Role of the Community Health Nurse in Occupational Settings

The occupational health nurse functions as a clinician, educator, manager, and researcher within the workplace. Responsibilities include delivering primary care for minor ailments, managing emergencies, conducting health education sessions, and liaising with management and safety committees. Occupational health nurses also advocate for safe working conditions and coordinate rehabilitation programs for injured employees returning to duty. Their unique position bridges clinical practice and public health within industrial communities.
