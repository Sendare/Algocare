Refugees

Definition and Legal Framework of Refugees

A refugee is an individual who flees their country of origin due to a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and who is unable or unwilling to return. This classification is distinct from internally displaced persons, who remain within the borders of their own nation despite similar forced migration triggers. The primary international instrument guiding refugee protection is the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol, which enshrine the cardinal principle of non-refoulement. Non-refoulement prohibits countries from returning an asylum seeker or refugee to territories where their life or freedom would be threatened. In Nigeria, the National Commission for Refugees, Migrants and Internally Displaced Persons operates under domestic and international laws to safeguard these populations, granting them legal access to basic social services.

Displacement Dynamics and Context in Nigeria

Nigeria experiences complex population movements driven by multi-faceted crises, including insurgencies in the North-East, farmer-herder clashes in the North-Central zone, banditry in the North-West, and environmental degradation in the coastal regions. These localized conflicts create massive waves of internal displacement and spill over into neighboring countries, while Nigeria simultaneously hosts refugees from regional conflicts. Displaced settlements range from formal, government-managed camps to informal urban slums where resources are severely stretched. The sheer volume of displaced persons overwhelms local health infrastructure, creating logistical hurdles for community health nurses attempting to deliver routine immunizations and primary care services.

Common Health Challenges and Vulnerabilities

  • Communicable diseases: Overcrowded living conditions and poor sanitation facilitate rapid transmission of respiratory infections, acute watery diarrhea, and measles.
  • Malnutrition: Food insecurity and disrupted supply chains frequently lead to severe acute malnutrition, particularly among young children.
  • Chronic conditions: Hypertension, diabetes, and asthma often go unmanaged due to interrupted medication supplies and lost clinical records.
  • Physical injuries: Trauma sustained during flight, structural collapses, or interpersonal violence requires prompt surgical and rehabilitative care.

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support Needs

Displaced populations experience extreme stressors, including witnessing violence, losing family members, and enduring the profound uncertainty of camp life. These experiences frequently manifest as post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and depression. Community health nurses play a vital role in identifying psychological distress through active listening and culturally appropriate screening. Psychosocial support interventions focus on restoring community networks, creating safe spaces for children, and teaching basic coping strategies. Integrating mental health care into primary healthcare settings helps reduce stigma and ensures psychological first aid is accessible to those who need it most.

Community Health Nursing Assessment

Conducting a comprehensive health assessment in a refugee setting requires cultural sensitivity, observational acumen, and systematic data collection. Nurses evaluate environmental determinants such as water sources, waste disposal systems, and shelter integrity, which directly influence community health profiles. Individual clinical assessments must account for language barriers, requiring the use of trained interpreters whenever possible. Nurses also map existing community assets, identifying traditional birth attendants, religious leaders, and informal youth groups who can assist in health mobilization and outreach.

Infectious Disease Surveillance and Outbreak Control

Early detection of infectious disease outbreaks is a cornerstone of refugee health management. Disease Early Warning, Alert and Response Systems help track syndromic patterns such as acute watery diarrhea, bloody stools, and fever with rash. When an alert triggers, rapid response teams implement containment measures, including isolation protocols, contact tracing, and mass vaccination campaigns. Environmental sanitation, safe drinking water distribution, and vector control are executed concurrently to block transmission pathways before an epidemic overwhelms the settlement.

Maternal and Child Health Interventions

Pregnant women and young children face disproportionate health risks during displacement due to collapsed health systems and nutritional deficits. Essential maternal care includes antenatal monitoring, safe delivery services, and emergency obstetric referrals to prevent hemorrhage and sepsis. Newborn care focuses on thermal regulation, early initiation of breastfeeding, and clean cord care. Expanded Program on Immunization schedules must be rapidly re-established within camps to protect infants against vaccine-preventable diseases like polio, measles, and tetanus, despite logistical constraints.

Multi-Agency Healthcare Delivery

No single organization can independently meet the complex health and social needs of a displaced population. Effective healthcare delivery relies on coordinated partnerships between government ministries, the World Health Organization, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, and local non-governmental organizations. Clear role division prevents duplication of effort and ensures that all sectors—ranging from nutrition and sanitation to clinical care and mental health—receive adequate attention. Community health nurses often act as bridges between displaced communities and external humanitarian agencies, facilitating communication, participatory planning, and culturally appropriate service delivery.
