Street Children

Definition and Categorization of Street Children

Street children are minors for whom the street has become home or their main source of livelihood, lacking adequate protection, supervision, or direction from responsible adults. UNICEF categorizes this population into distinct groups to help nurses and social workers plan appropriate interventions:

  • Children of the street: These minors live, sleep, and work entirely on the streets, having broken almost all family ties due to severe neglect, abuse, or abandonment.
  • Children on the street: These youths spend most of their daytime hours working or begging on urban corridors, but they return home to their families at night, often contributing meager earnings to household survival.
  • Unaccompanied minors: These are displaced children completely detached from any family support system, frequently resulting from communal conflicts, natural disasters, or orphanhood.

Misclassifying these groups can lead to inappropriate care plans. A child who still maintains a home requires family-focused interventions, whereas a child living entirely on the street needs immediate emergency housing and protection.

Socioeconomic Drivers and Epidemiology in Nigeria

Streetism in Nigeria is heavily driven by widespread poverty, rapid urbanization, and population displacement. Major cities like Lagos, Kano, and Ibadan host massive numbers of vulnerable youths due to rural-urban migration fueled by the search for better economic prospects.

Economic instability forces many households below the poverty line, making child labor an informal survival strategy. Cultural and religious practices also influence local epidemiology. For instance, the Almajiri system in Northern Nigeria involves young boys migrating to Islamic scholars for Quranic education, but economic hardships often leave these children without basic care, forcing them onto the streets to beg.

Nursing assessments in Nigerian urban clinics must recognize that streetism is rarely an individual choice. It is a systemic symptom of economic breakdown, weak social welfare safety nets, and large family sizes exceeding parental financial capacity.

Push and Pull Factors Leading to Streetism

The transition of a child from a home environment to the pavement is driven by two distinct forces: push factors that drive them away from home, and pull factors that attract them to the streets.

Push factors originate within the domestic or community environment. They include domestic violence, physical or sexual abuse, step-parent maltreatment, extreme parental poverty, and the death of breadwinning parents without extended family support.

Pull factors are perceived benefits associated with street life. They include the illusion of financial independence, peer influence from friends already living on the streets, freedom from strict parental control, and the excitement of urban centers. Understanding these dual dynamics helps nurses counsel families and design targeted prevention programs in primary healthcare settings.

Health Risks and Vulnerabilities

Living on the streets exposes children to severe physical hazards and high morbidity rates. Lacking clean water, adequate shelter, and nutritional security, these minors face constant threats to their physical well-being.

  • Infectious Diseases: Overcrowded sleeping quarters and poor hygiene promote the spread of tuberculosis, scabies, and diarrheal illnesses.
  • Environmental Hazards: Prolonged sun exposure, heavy rainfall, and cold harm create high risks for respiratory infections and malaria.
  • Trauma and Injury: Road traffic accidents are common because children work and sleep near busy motor parks and highways. Physical and sexual assault by older peers or adults also cause frequent traumatic injuries.
  • Substance Use: Many street children inhale volatile substances like solutions and glue to suppress hunger, cold, and fear, leading to permanent neurological damage.

Psychosocial and Developmental Impacts

The absence of stable parental guidance severely hampers the emotional and cognitive development of street children. Constant exposure to violence, rejection, and instability triggers chronic stress responses, elevating cortisol levels and leading to trauma-related disorders.

Deprived of consistent affection, these youths often struggle to form healthy interpersonal relationships, frequently displaying defensive aggression or deep mistrust toward authority figures, including healthcare providers. Educational deprivation is another profound consequence. Out of school, they miss critical literacy milestones, locking them out of formal employment opportunities and perpetuating a cycle of generational poverty. Psychiatric conditions such as depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder are widespread yet rarely diagnosed or treated.

Community Health Nursing Assessment

Assessing street children requires specialized communication techniques because traditional clinical approaches often fail to build rapport. Nurses must approach target locations—such as motor parks, markets, and under bridges—with patience, empathy, and a non-judgmental attitude.

The assessment process begins with observing general appearance, signs of physical abuse, nutritional status, and evidence of substance use. Nurses should perform rapid screening for common ailments like malaria, skin infections, and malnutrition. Gathering history requires building trust slowly, as children may lie or hide details to protect themselves from perceived threats or law enforcement. Documenting social vulnerabilities is just as crucial as tracking physical vital signs to ensure holistic care planning.

Intervention, Rehabilitation, and Reintegration

Helping street children transition off the pavement requires comprehensive, multi-step interventions managed by interdisciplinary teams. The primary goal is restoring safety, health, and social stability.

  • Outreach and Drop-In Centers: Safe spaces where children can access first aid, clean clothing, food, and counseling without immediate pressure to leave the streets.
  • Shelter and Care: Providing temporary residential facilities that offer stabilization, nutrition, and protection from abuse.
  • Family Tracing and Reintegration: Locating biological relatives, assessing home safety, and mediating family conflicts to facilitate safe reunification when possible.
  • Vocational Training and Education: Equipping older minors with practical trade skills like tailoring, carpentry, or digital literacy to ensure sustainable livelihoods.

Forcing children off the streets through police raids without rehabilitation or family support rarely succeeds, as youths typically return to the streets to escape harsh detention environments.

Policy Frameworks and Stakeholder Roles

Addressing streetism effectively demands coordinated action across multiple sectors, moving beyond clinical medicine into social policy and community development. Nurses play a crucial advocacy role in shaping policies that protect vulnerable minors.

Key legislative instruments, such as the Child Rights Act in Nigeria, provide legal protection against child abuse, exploitation, and neglect, though enforcement remains inconsistent across states. Multi-sectoral collaboration involves combining resources from healthcare services, social welfare departments, law enforcement, religious organizations, and non-governmental agencies.

Nurses collaborate with these stakeholders by conducting health education, advocating for free healthcare access for indigent minors, and training community leaders to recognize early signs of family breakdown before children run away to the streets.
