Physical Needs of Persons with Physical Disabilities

Introduction to Physical Disabilities and Nursing Care

Physical disabilities limit an individual's motor function, mobility, stamina, or dexterity. In clinical practice across Nigeria, nurses encounter patients with impairments stemming from road traffic accidents, stroke, spinal cord injuries, or congenital conditions. Holistic nursing care for these individuals requires shifting focus from the deficit to remaining functional capacities. The goal is promoting independence and preventing secondary complications like contractures and muscle atrophy.

Mobility and Transfer Support Techniques

Safe movement requires proper body mechanics to protect both the nurse and the patient. When transferring a patient from a bed to a wheelchair, position the chair at a forty-five-degree angle to the bed and lock all wheels. Use gait belts for stability and encourage the patient to use their stronger side to lead the movement. Never pull on a patient's weak arm or shoulder, as this risks joint dislocation or soft tissue injury.

Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) and Personal Hygiene

Assisting with activities of daily living involves bathing, dressing, grooming, and feeding while preserving patient dignity. Nurses should encourage self-care by allowing patients to perform whatever tasks they can manage independently. Adaptive equipment such as long-handled sponges, velcro fasteners, and plate guards bridge the gap between limitation and autonomy. Establishing a predictable daily routine reduces frustration and encourages participation.

Nutritional Needs and Dietary Management

Immobilized patients have lower basal energy requirements, which increases their risk of excessive weight gain and subsequent mobility strain. Conversely, some patients struggle with inadequate intake due to fatigue or depression. Diets must be rich in high-quality protein to support tissue repair and maintain muscle mass, alongside adequate fiber and fluids to prevent constipation.

Elimination, Bowel, and Bladder Care

Bladder and bowel dysfunction frequently accompany physical disabilities, particularly neurological impairments. Nurses must implement structured voiding schedules and bowel training programs to maintain continence and prevent urinary tract infections. Proper perineal hygiene and adequate hydration are fundamental. Indwelling catheters should be avoided whenever possible due to infection risks, favoring intermittent catheterization or external collection devices.

Prevention and Management of Pressure Ulcers

Constant pressure on bony prominences cuts off capillary blood flow, leading to tissue ischemia and necrosis. Nurses must reposition bedbound patients at least every two hours and wheelchair-bound patients every hour. Use pressure-relieving mattresses, keep skin clean and dry, and avoid dragging patients across bed sheets during repositioning to prevent shear friction injuries.

Pain Management and Physical Comfort Measures

Chronic pain is a common companion to physical disabilities, stemming from muscle spasms, nerve damage, or joint misalignment. Pharmacological interventions must be balanced with non-pharmacological methods like heat therapy, cold packs, gentle massage, and proper body alignment. Continuous discomfort interferes with sleep, healing, and participation in rehabilitation programs.

Assistive Devices and Environmental Modifications

Environmental barriers often disable a person more than their physical impairment. Nurses educate patients on the correct use of assistive devices like crutches, walkers, and wheelchairs. Home assessments are vital to recommend modifications such as ramp installations, widened doorways, grab bars in bathrooms, and the removal of loose rugs to prevent falls.
