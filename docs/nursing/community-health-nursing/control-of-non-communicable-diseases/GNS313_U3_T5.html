Smoking

Introduction to Smoking and Nicotine Dependence

Tobacco smoking remains a major global public health challenge, driven primarily by the highly addictive nature of nicotine. When an individual inhales cigarette smoke, nicotine is rapidly absorbed into the pulmonary circulation and reaches the brain within seconds. This creates a quick spike in dopamine release, reinforcing the habit. Over time, neuroadaptation occurs, requiring higher amounts of nicotine to achieve the same pleasurable effect or simply to avoid withdrawal symptoms. Nicotine dependence involves both physical reliance and deep-rooted behavioral habits, making cessation difficult without a structured approach.

Epidemiology and Public Health Impact in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the landscape of tobacco use is shifting. While traditional cigarette smoking has long been prevalent among adult males, emerging trends show rising experimentation with shisha (waterpipe) and electronic cigarettes among urban youth and young adults. Aggressive marketing by tobacco companies and weak enforcement of public smoking bans compound the issue. The public health burden extends far beyond direct medical costs, contributing heavily to non-communicable diseases such as cancers, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cardiovascular complications in local healthcare facilities.

Pathophysiology and Health Consequences of Tobacco Use

Cigarette smoke contains thousands of chemicals, including tar, carbon monoxide, and various carcinogens that systematically damage human organ systems.

  • Respiratory System: Paralyzes cilia, leading to mucus accumulation, chronic bronchitis, and eventual destruction of alveolar walls seen in emphysema.
  • Cardiovascular System: Promotes atherosclerosis, increases platelet stickiness, and raises blood pressure, significantly elevating the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke.
  • Oncogenesis: Direct cellular DNA damage from tar carcinogens initiates malignant cell transformation across multiple organs, most notably the lungs, larynx, and bladder.

Behavioral and Psychological Aspects of Addiction

Tobacco addiction is deeply intertwined with daily routines and emotional coping mechanisms. Smokers often associate lighting a cigarette with specific triggers, such as drinking morning tea, experiencing workplace stress, or socializing with peers. Over time, these triggers create powerful conditioned responses. Psychological dependence means the patient relies on smoking to regulate mood, manage anxiety, or alleviate boredom. Comprehensive cessation care must address these behavioral patterns alongside physical nicotine withdrawal.

Community Health Assessment and Screening for Tobacco Use

Routine screening is vital for identifying tobacco users and initiating timely interventions in primary care settings. Nurses should systematically ask every patient about their tobacco use history during routine health assessments, documenting the type of product used, frequency, and duration. Using brief screening tools helps quantify dependence and readiness to quit.

Smoking Cessation Interventions and Counseling

Behavioral counseling combined with supportive encouragement greatly improves quit rates. The standard clinical framework for brief interventions is the 5 As approach: Ask about tobacco use, Advise all users to quit, Assess willingness to make a quit attempt, Assist with a quit plan, and Arrange follow-up contact. For patients not yet ready to quit, the 5 Rs approach (Relevance, Risks, Rewards, Roadblocks, Repetition) helps build intrinsic motivation without confrontation.

Pharmacotherapy in Tobacco Dependence Management

Pharmacotherapy doubles or triples the success rate of smoking cessation by easing withdrawal symptoms and blunting the rewarding effects of nicotine. Treatment options include Nicotine Replacement Therapy (such as patches, gums, and lozenges) which delivers controlled doses of nicotine without the toxic tars of cigarette smoke. Prescription non-nicotine medications like varenicline or bupropion can also reduce cravings and block brain receptor sites. Nurses play a crucial role in educating patients on proper administration and managing potential side effects.

Community-Based Prevention and Public Health Policies

Individual counseling alone is insufficient to curb the tobacco epidemic; robust population-level policies are essential. Effective public health measures include high tobacco taxation, comprehensive bans on advertising and sponsorships, enforcement of smoke-free public spaces, and prominent graphic health warnings on product packaging. Community health nurses contribute by advocating for policy implementation, educating school groups, and participating in public awareness campaigns that promote tobacco-free living.
