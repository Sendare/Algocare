Hypertension

Introduction and Definition of Hypertension

Hypertension, commonly referred to as high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition in which the force of blood against the artery walls is persistently elevated. Nurses encounter this condition daily across all clinical settings in Nigeria, ranging from rural primary health centers to tertiary teaching hospitals. Blood pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and is recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure, representing the pressure when the heart beats, over diastolic pressure, representing the pressure when the heart rests between beats. A single elevated reading does not confirm a diagnosis, as transient factors like anxiety, pain, or physical exertion can temporarily spike pressure. Diagnosis requires multiple elevated readings taken on separate occasions under standardized conditions. Chronic elevation forces the heart to work harder to circulate blood, gradually damaging delicate vascular endothelium throughout the body and setting the stage for severe cardiovascular and renal morbidity.

Epidemiology and Public Health Significance in Nigeria

Non-communicable diseases constitute a rising burden in sub-Saharan Africa, with hypertension leading the surge. In Nigeria, prevalence rates among adults often exceed thirty percent, cutting across urban and rural demographics. Urbanization, adoption of sedentary lifestyles, and dietary shifts toward high-sodium processed foods heavily contribute to this trend. Public health systems face severe strain because hypertension frequently remains asymptomatic for years, earning it the moniker 'the silent killer.' Many Nigerians discover their status only when presenting with a catastrophic end-organ event like a stroke or acute kidney injury at a secondary or tertiary facility. Community health nursing strategies must pivot toward aggressive screening and decentralized care models to mitigate this silent epidemic.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Risk Factors

Blood pressure is determined by the interaction between cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. When systemic vascular resistance increases due to arteriolar vasoconstriction, or when cardiac output rises, hypertension ensues. Primary or essential hypertension accounts for the vast majority of cases, possessing no single identifiable organic cause but strongly linked to genetics, aging, and environmental factors. Secondary hypertension stems from an underlying, identifiable pathology, most commonly chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like Conn's syndrome.

  • Modifiable Risk Factors: Obesity, high dietary sodium intake, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and chronic psychological stress.
  • Non-Modifiable Risk Factors: Advanced age, positive family history of cardiovascular disease, and genetic predisposition.

Classification and Blood Pressure Categories

Clinical classification guides therapeutic decisions and stratifies cardiovascular risk. Standard guidelines divide blood pressure into distinct categories based on systolic and diastolic thresholds.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Techniques

Most individuals with chronic hypertension experience no symptoms until vascular damage becomes advanced. When symptoms do surface, they are often vague and non-specific, including early morning occipital headaches, dizziness, fatigue, palpitations, or blurred vision. Nurses must master proper auscultatory blood pressure measurement using a calibrated sphygmomanometer and stethoscope. The patient must sit quietly with feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level for at least five minutes prior to cuff inflation. Using an incorrect cuff size is a frequent clinical error; a cuff that is too small yields falsely elevated readings, while an oversized cuff under-reports blood pressure.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management

Managing hypertension requires a combination of lifestyle modifications and targeted drug therapy. Initial non-pharmacological interventions form the foundation of care for all patients, focusing on dietary sodium restriction, weight reduction, regular aerobic exercise, moderation of alcohol intake, and smoking cessation. When lifestyle adjustments prove insufficient or when initial blood pressure is markedly elevated, clinicians prescribe antihypertensive medications. Common pharmacological classes include thiazide diuretics, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers. Nurses play a crucial role in monitoring for adverse drug reactions, such as dry cough with ACE inhibitors or electrolyte imbalances with diuretics, and reinforcing adherence to long-term regimens.

Complications and Crisis Intervention

Uncontrolled hypertension exerts relentless mechanical stress on the cardiovascular system, leading to multi-organ damage over time. Major complications include cerebrovascular accidents, myocardial infarction, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and hypertensive retinopathy. A hypertensive crisis represents a life-threatening medical emergency divided into urgency and emergency. Hypertensive urgency involves severe blood pressure elevation without acute target organ damage, managed with oral medications over hours or days. Hypertensive emergency involves severe elevation coupled with evidence of acute end-organ damage, such as encephalopathy, intracranial hemorrhage, or pulmonary edema, requiring immediate intravenous drug therapy in a high-dependency unit.

Role of the Community Health Nurse in Prevention and Patient Education

Community health nurses serve as the frontline defenders against the burden of hypertension through active outreach, health education, and home visit monitoring. Nurses organize mobile screening camps in markets, churches, and community centers to reach individuals who rarely visit formal health facilities. Educational programs focus on demystifying the disease, emphasizing that hypertension is a lifelong condition requiring continuous management rather than a temporary illness cured by a short course of herbal remedies or pharmaceuticals. Nurses must address local misconceptions, such as the belief that hypertension can be felt whenever it rises, and promote continuous blood pressure tracking and strict medication adherence.
