Breast Cancer

Introduction and Epidemiology in Nigeria

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women globally and in Nigeria. Local epidemiological data reveal a troubling trend: Nigerian women present with the disease at a much younger age compared to their Caucasian counterparts in Western countries. Furthermore, patients in Nigerian hospitals often arrive at advanced stages, heavily limiting treatment success rates. Cultural beliefs, financial constraints, and poor access to diagnostic facilities contribute significantly to this delayed presentation. Understanding these local realities helps nursing students appreciate why early community sensitization is just as vital as hospital-based chemotherapy.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Breast

The human breast is a modified sweat gland located in the superficial fascia of the anterior chest wall. Structurally, it consists of 15 to 20 glandular lobes radiating around the nipple, separated by adipose and fibrous connective tissue known as Cooper's ligaments. These ligaments provide structural support and shape to the breast. Each lobe drains into a lactiferous duct that converges toward the nipple-areolar complex. The vast majority of breast cancers originate within the epithelial lining of the terminal duct lobular units (TDLUs), making this microscopic region clinically critical for nurses to understand.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Developing breast cancer involves a complex interplay of genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors. Non-modifiable risks include biological female gender, advancing age, and a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer involving BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations. Modifiable lifestyle factors include prolonged exposure to endogenous estrogen, such as early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, and lack of breastfeeding. Additional contributors involve physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, and postmenopausal obesity. Recognizing these triggers guides nurses in identifying high-risk women during community outreaches.

Pathophysiology and Staging

Breast cancer begins as a local genetic mutation that allows uncontrolled cell multiplication, escaping normal apoptotic signals. It progresses from in situ lesions, where abnormal cells remain confined inside the duct or lobule, to invasive carcinoma that breaches the basement membrane and infiltrates surrounding stroma. Metastasis occurs primarily via the lymphatic system to the axillary lymph nodes, and via the bloodstream to distant organs such as bones, lungs, liver, and brain. Healthcare teams use the TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) staging system to classify the extent of the disease and plan targeted interventions.

Clinical Manifestations and Warning Signs

Patients with early-stage breast cancer are frequently asymptomatic, which makes routine screening indispensable. When symptoms eventually arise, they commonly present as a painless, hard, single, and immobile lump within the breast tissue. Advanced or inflammatory presentations reveal characteristic skin changes, including dimpling, retraction of the nipple, peau d'orange appearance resembling an orange peel, and persistent localized pain. Ulceration of the skin and bloody or clear nipple discharge from a single duct also demand immediate clinical investigation.

Screening and Community Assessment Methods

  • Breast Self-Examination (BSE): A monthly self-check procedure performed a few days after menstruation ends to help women notice unusual changes.
  • Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): Physical examination of the breasts and axilla performed by trained healthcare providers during clinic visits.
  • Mammography: Low-dose X-ray imaging used as the gold standard for detecting early microcalcifications and non-palpable masses.
  • Ultrasonography: Imaging modality helpful for differentiating solid masses from fluid-filled cysts, especially in younger women with dense breast tissue.

Medical, Surgical, and Nursing Management

Treatment for breast cancer is multimodal, combining surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted biological agents. Surgical choices range from breast-conserving procedures like lumpectomy to total mastectomy with lymph node dissection. Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring surgical drains, preventing infection, and managing lymphedema in the affected upper extremity. Nurses teach patients to avoid blood draws, injections, or blood pressure measurements on the arm of the affected side to protect compromised lymphatic drainage.

Palliative Care and Rehabilitation

For patients with advanced or terminal breast cancer, palliative care becomes the cornerstone of management, prioritizing comfort and dignity over curative intent. Nurses coordinate pain management using the WHO analgesic ladder, address distressing symptoms like fatigue and dyspnea, and offer psychological support to both patient and family. Rehabilitation programs incorporate gentle physical therapy to restore shoulder mobility, psychological counseling for body image concerns, and support groups to combat social isolation. Comprehensive end-of-life care ensures that the patient passes with dignity and minimal suffering.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Primary prevention strategies target modifiable risk factors by promoting physical activity, healthy diets, reduced alcohol intake, and breastfeeding. Secondary prevention centers on early detection through organized community awareness campaigns and screening programs tailored to local languages and cultural nuances. Nurses play a frontline role in dismantling myths, such as the erroneous belief that breast cancer is caused by witchcraft or spiritual attacks. By empowering women with accurate knowledge and teaching correct self-examination techniques, nurses bridge the gap between late presentation and early, successful treatment.
