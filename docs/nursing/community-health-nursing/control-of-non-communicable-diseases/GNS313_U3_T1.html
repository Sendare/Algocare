Control of Non-Communicable Diseases

Introduction and Epidemiology of Non-Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a silent epidemic quietly overtaking infectious diseases across sub-Saharan Africa. In Nigeria, conditions such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and various cancers now contribute significantly to adult morbidity and mortality. Unlike acute infections, NCDs develop slowly, persist for prolonged periods, and rarely achieve spontaneous resolution. Health facilities in both urban and rural communities face a rising double burden of disease, straining resources previously dedicated solely to malaria and maternal-child health.

Risk Factors and Determinants of Non-Communicable Diseases

The emergence of NCDs is fueled by modifiable lifestyle behaviors and unmodifiable biological traits. Urbanization, economic shifts, and aggressive marketing of ultra-processed foods have radically altered traditional Nigerian diets and physical activity patterns.

  • Unhealthy Diet: High consumption of sodium, refined carbohydrates, and saturated fats.
  • Physical Inactivity: Sedentary occupations and reduced walking due to motorized transport.
  • Tobacco Use: Cigarette smoking and increasing uptake of shisha among youths.
  • Harmful Alcohol Use: Binge drinking and consumption of unregulated local brews.
  • Unmodifiable Factors: Advancing age, genetic predisposition, and family history.

Primary Prevention and Health Promotion Strategies

Primary prevention aims to intercept disease development before biological changes occur. In primary healthcare settings, nurses lead public education campaigns addressing dietary modification, regular aerobic exercise, tobacco cessation, and moderation of alcohol intake. Effective strategies must extend beyond individual counseling to engage entire communities through market squares, churches, mosques, and schools, fostering environments that make healthy choices accessible and culturally acceptable.

Secondary Prevention: Screening and Early Detection in Primary Healthcare

Because NCDs remain asymptomatic for years, secondary prevention focuses on routine screening to catch abnormalities at an early stage. Primary health clinics serve as the first line of defense, offering blood pressure measurement, random or fasting blood glucose testing, and visual inspection with acetic acid for cervical cancer screening. Early identification prevents acute crises like stroke or diabetic ketoacidosis and reduces long-term healthcare costs.

Clinical Management and Rehabilitation of Chronic Conditions

Managing diagnosed chronic conditions requires lifelong adherence to pharmacotherapy, dietary control, and regular monitoring. Nurses educate patients on self-monitoring techniques, such as using glucometers and home blood pressure cuffs. Rehabilitation programs help patients with established disease—such as survivors of strokes or myocardial infarctions—regain functional independence and prevent secondary disabilities through tailored physical activity and psychological support.

The Role of the Community Health Nurse in NCD Control

Community health nurses act as the bridge between clinical facilities and the populations they serve. Their responsibilities span home visits for vulnerable elderly clients, organizing mobile screening outreaches, tracking defaulters who miss clinic appointments, and advocating for healthier local policies. By understanding local cultural beliefs, nurses tailor health education to resonate with community members, improving compliance and health outcomes.

Multisectoral Collaboration and Policy Frameworks

Controlling NCDs extends far beyond the health sector. Successful prevention requires robust policies involving agriculture, education, urban planning, and finance. Governments must implement fiscal measures—such as taxing sugary beverages and tobacco products—while creating safe public spaces for physical exercise. Nurses contribute to these frameworks by providing grassroots epidemiological data and advocating for policies that prioritize population health.
