Violence

Introduction and Definition of Violence

Violence is defined by the World Health Organization as the intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community, that either results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment, or deprivation. This definition broadens the traditional view of violence beyond physical blows to include power dynamics, verbal threats, and systemic neglect. Nurses encounter various forms of violence daily in clinical practice, ranging from intimate partner abuse in community health centres to community-based trauma in emergency departments. Recognizing that violence involves psychological and coercive power as much as physical harm is essential for accurate identification and documentation.

Types and Classifications of Violence

Public health frameworks categorize violence into three broad domains based on the characteristics of the perpetrator and the victim:

  • Self-directed violence: Suicidal behavior and self-abuse, including cutting or intentional poisoning.
  • Interpersonal violence: Family and intimate partner violence, as well as community violence perpetrated by individuals unrelated to the victim, such as gang clashes or armed robbery.
  • Collective violence: Instrumental use of violence by states or organized groups to achieve social, political, or economic objectives, including war, terrorism, and state-sponsored oppression.

Understanding these distinctions helps nurses tailor their triage, safety planning, and reporting obligations according to the specific context of the trauma.

Risk Factors and Root Causes of Violence

Violence is rarely random; it emerges from an interplay of individual, relational, community, and societal risk factors. Poverty, unemployment, lack of educational opportunities, gender inequality, and weak community laws create fertile ground for violent behavior. Substance abuse, particularly alcohol and illicit drug dependency, frequently acts as a catalyst that lowers inhibition and escalates conflict. Historical trauma and exposure to violence during childhood heavily increase the likelihood that an individual will either perpetrate or experience violence later in life. Nurses must examine these underlying determinants rather than viewing violent acts in isolation.

Community and Individual Health Consequences

The aftermath of violence extends far beyond immediate physical injuries, bone fractures, or lacerations. Survivors frequently suffer from chronic health conditions such as gastrointestinal disorders, chronic pain syndromes, and persistent neurological symptoms. Mental health consequences are profound, encompassing post-traumatic stress disorder, major depressive disorder, severe anxiety, and substance abuse disorders used as coping mechanisms. At the community level, pervasive violence erodes social trust, closes local businesses, disrupts healthcare delivery systems, and leads to long-term economic stagnation.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of At-Risk Populations

Clinical identification of violence requires a high index of suspicion, as victims often conceal the true source of their injuries due to fear, shame, or threats from perpetrators. Nurses should routinely screen patients in safe, private environments using direct, non-judgmental questioning. Vulnerable groups—including women, children, older adults, individuals with disabilities, and migrants—demand heightened vigilance. Documenting physical findings objectively, preserving forensic evidence carefully without altering wounds, and noting exact patient statements in quotation marks are fundamental nursing responsibilities.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Management

Managing victims of violence requires a compassionate, trauma-informed approach that prioritizes immediate physical stabilization and emotional safety. Community health nurses coordinate emergency medical care, mental health referrals, and safe housing options. Creating a confidential safety plan is critical for individuals returning to potentially dangerous environments. Nurses must also fulfill legal mandates by reporting suspected child abuse or severe assault to appropriate authorities while maintaining patient trust and confidentiality where legally permissible.

Prevention Strategies and Multi-Sectoral Collaboration

Stopping violence before it occurs requires public health prevention strategies spanning primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary prevention involves community education campaigns, youth empowerment programs, and promoting gender equity. Secondary prevention focuses on early identification and rapid response for high-risk families. Tertiary prevention rehabilitates survivors and prevents re-victimization. Because violence touches legal, social, educational, and medical spheres, nurses cannot tackle it alone; active collaboration with social workers, law enforcement, legal aid organizations, and community leaders is indispensable for sustainable safety.
