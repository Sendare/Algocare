Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning in Community Health

Poisoning remains a critical public health challenge across Nigerian communities, cutting across urban and rural settlements alike. A poison is any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—that can disrupt normal body function, cause cellular damage, or lead to death when introduced into the body. In local health practice, cases often stem from accidental ingestion by toddlers at home, occupational hazards among agricultural workers handling agrochemicals, or intentional self-harm. Community health nurses occupy the frontline in recognizing these emergencies, stabilizing victims, and guiding families on preventive safety measures. Understanding the broad spectrum of toxicology allows healthcare providers in primary health centres to act swiftly before irreversible organ damage occurs.

Common Types and Sources of Poisons

Toxic exposures vary widely depending on the immediate environment, socioeconomic factors, and seasonal agricultural practices. Recognizing these sources helps nurses quickly narrow down differential diagnoses during emergency admissions.

  • Agrochemicals: Organophosphates and carbamates used widely in farming communities for pest control.
  • Household Chemicals: Bleaches, kerosene, caustic soda, and detergents commonly stored in unlocked bottles.
  • Pharmaceuticals: Accidental or intentional overdoses of paracetamol, analgesics, and leftover antibiotics.
  • Traditional Remedies: Unregulated herbal concotions or preparations administered without standardized dosing.
  • Venomous Bites: Snakebites and scorpion stings prevalent during the rainy and farming seasons.

Routes of Exposure and Mechanisms of Toxicity

Poisons enter the human body through four primary routes: ingestion, inhalation, absorption, and injection. Ingestion remains the most common route in domestic settings, where toxic agents cross the gastrointestinal mucosa into the systemic circulation. Inhalation involves breathing in noxious gases, carbon monoxide, or chemical fumes, which rapidly cross the alveolar-capillary membrane. Absorption occurs when liquid chemicals or organic solvents pass directly through the skin or mucous membranes. Injection introduces toxins directly into tissues or bloodstreams via animal fangs, insect stingers, or contaminated needles. Once inside, toxins exert their mechanisms by binding to cellular receptors, altering enzyme activity, or disrupting cellular oxygen transport, ultimately leading to metabolic failure.

Assessment and Clinical Manifestations of Acute Poisoning

A systematic nursing assessment is vital when evaluating a poisoned patient whose condition can deteriorate rapidly. Begin with the primary survey checking airway, breathing, and circulation, followed by a targeted toxicological evaluation. Nurses must observe vital signs for life-threatening deviations such as severe bradypnea or tachycardia. Look for specific physical signs known as toxidromes, which point toward particular classes of substances.

Emergency First Aid and Initial Management

Immediate management of acute poisoning focuses on stabilization, preventing further absorption, and enhancing elimination. Nurses must first ensure personal safety by wearing appropriate personal protective equipment before touching contaminated patients. For dermal exposures, strip off contaminated clothing immediately and flush the skin with copious amounts of clean water for at least fifteen minutes. For ingested poisons, traditional practices such as inducing vomiting with salt water or palm oil must be strongly discouraged, as they increase the risk of aspiration and chemical burns to the esophagus. Activated charcoal may be administered if the patient is fully conscious and presents within a safe time window, as it adsorbs many toxins in the gastrointestinal tract. Continuous monitoring of neurological status and airway patency remains the bedrock of emergency nursing care.

Community Prevention and Public Health Education

Prevention remains superior to cure, and community health nurses hold the mandate to drive local safety awareness campaigns. Education sessions should target parents, school teachers, and market vendors on the hazards of improper chemical storage. Nurses should demonstrate safe handling of domestic insecticides and warn against transferring dangerous fluids into drinking bottles or food containers. Collaboration with local leaders and media houses can help broadcast safety messages in native languages. Furthermore, establishing clear emergency contact numbers for poison information centres or local hospitals in public spaces ensures rapid response when accidents occur.

Safe Storage and Disposal of Hazardous Household Substances

Proper management of hazardous substances inside homes and workplaces prevents accidental exposure and environmental contamination. Household chemicals and medications should be stored in locked cupboards high above the reach of children, preferably in their original containers with legible labels. Expired drugs or leftover prescription medications must never be thrown into household dustbins where children or scavengers can access them; instead, they should be returned to health facility take-back programs. Similarly, empty agrochemical containers must not be reused for storing drinking water or cooking oil. Safe disposal practices protect both human life and surrounding water sources from toxic pollution.
