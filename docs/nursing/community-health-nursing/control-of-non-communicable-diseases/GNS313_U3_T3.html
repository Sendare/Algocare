Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Definition of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder marked by persistent hyperglycemia. This high blood sugar results from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Insulin is the vital hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets that allows body cells to absorb glucose from the bloodstream for energy. When this system fails, glucose accumulates in the blood, spilling over into the urine and causing widespread metabolic disturbances.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Concepts

The body breaks down digested carbohydrates into glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Normally, rising blood glucose triggers the pancreas to release insulin. Insulin binds to cellular receptors, signaling glucose transporters to move sugar inside. In diabetes, either the pancreas fails to produce enough insulin, or the target tissues become resistant to its effects. Without effective insulin action, cells starve for energy while glucose builds up dangerously in the extracellular fluid.

Types and Classifications of Diabetes

  • Type 1 Diabetes: An autoimmune condition where the body destroys pancreatic beta cells, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Patients require lifelong exogenous insulin therapy.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Characterized by peripheral insulin resistance combined with a progressive decline in insulin secretion. It often links closely to lifestyle factors and genetics.
  • Gestational Diabetes: Glucose intolerance recognized for the first time during pregnancy, posing risks to both mother and child.
  • Other Specific Types: Includes diabetes caused by genetic defects, pancreatic diseases, or drug toxicities.

Risk Factors and Causes in the Nigerian Context

While global risk factors include physical inactivity and obesity, the Nigerian context presents unique socioeconomic and demographic dynamics. Rapid urbanization has shifted traditional diets toward processed, high-calorie foods and sugary drinks. Genetic predisposition, increasing age, and chronic stress also contribute significantly. Furthermore, a rising prevalence of gestational diabetes and hypertension compounds the burden of Type 2 diabetes across urban and rural healthcare facilities.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Criteria

Classic clinical symptoms of diabetes include polyuria, polydipsia, and unexplained weight loss, often accompanied by polyphagia and fatigue. Nurses must assess patients for these cardinal signs during routine triage. Diagnosis relies on specific biochemical thresholds rather than symptoms alone.

Test ParameterThreshold for Diagnosis
Fasting Plasma Glucose≥ 7.0 mmol/L
Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c)≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol)
Random Plasma Glucose≥ 11.1 mmol/L with symptoms
Oral Glucose Tolerance Test≥ 11.1 mmol/L at 2 hours

Medical and Nursing Management

Managing diabetes requires a multidisciplinary approach focusing on glycemic control, lifestyle modification, and complication prevention. Nurses play a frontline role in administering prescribed oral hypoglycemic agents or subcutaneous insulin. Patient education must cover self-monitoring of blood glucose, proper injection site rotation, and recognising hypoglycemia symptoms. Regular foot care assessments and nutritional counseling are equally vital to long-term clinical stability.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Community-level interventions are essential to curb the rising incidence of diabetes in Nigeria. Public health campaigns should promote balanced nutrition using locally available foodstuffs, encourage regular physical activity, and advocate for routine screening in primary healthcare centers. Empowering communities with knowledge helps reduce stigma, encourages early detection, and prevents long-term disability.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Uncontrolled diabetes leads to severe acute and chronic complications. Acute emergencies include diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS), both requiring urgent medical resuscitation. Chronic complications affect both small and large blood vessels. Microvascular damage causes retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. Macrovascular damage increases the risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, and peripheral vascular disease, frequently culminating in diabetic foot ulcers and amputations.
