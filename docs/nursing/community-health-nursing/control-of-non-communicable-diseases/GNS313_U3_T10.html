Cervical Cancer

Introduction and Overview of Cervical Cancer

Cervical cancer originates in the cells of the cervix, the lower cylindrical portion of the uterus that opens into the vagina. It develops primarily in the transformation zone where the squamous epithelium of the ectocervix meets the columnar epithelium of the endocervix. Globally and within Nigeria, it remains a major public health concern, disproportionately affecting women in low-resource settings due to limited access to routine screening services.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Persistent infection with high-risk types of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV), particularly types 16 and 18, is the primary etiological agent responsible for nearly all cervical malignancies. Unlike many other adult cancers, viral persistence drives the disease process. Several co-factors elevate risk by facilitating viral persistence or promoting cellular dysplasia:

  • Early onset of sexual activity: Immature cervical tissue is more vulnerable to viral penetration.
  • Multiple sexual partners: Increases exposure likelihood to high-risk HPV strains.
  • Immunosuppression: Conditions like HIV reduce viral clearance capacity.
  • Multiparity: Frequent hormonal shifts and mechanical trauma to the cervix.
  • Tobacco smoking: Direct carcinogens accumulate in cervical mucus.

Pathophysiology and Progression from HPV Infection

Infection begins when HPV enters micro-abrasions in the cervical epithelium, infecting basal cells. High-risk viral proteins, specifically E6 and E7, inactive host tumor suppressor proteins p53 and retinoblastoma (Rb). This inactivation leads to uncontrolled cellular division and genomic instability.

Over time, persistent infection progresses through distinct histological stages known as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN):

  1. CIN 1 (Mild dysplasia): Changes confined to the lower third of the epithelial layer.
  2. CIN 2 (Moderate dysplasia): Abnormal cells occupy the lower two-thirds of the epithelium.
  3. CIN 3 (Severe dysplasia/Carcinoma in situ): Full-thickness involvement without basement membrane invasion.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

Early-stage cervical cancer is frequently asymptomatic, which underscores the necessity of screening programs. As the tumor grows and invades deeper stroma or vascular channels, clinical signs emerge. The hallmark symptom is abnormal vaginal bleeding, including post-coital bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, and foul-smelling watery or blood-tinged vaginal discharge. Advanced disease causes pelvic pain, lower back ache, leg swelling from lymphatic obstruction, and urinary symptoms if the bladder is involved.

Staging relies heavily on the FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) system, transitioning from localized cervical involvement to distant metastasis.

Diagnostic Procedures and Evaluations

When screening results return abnormal, definitive diagnostic workup is required. A colposcopy magnifies the cervical epithelium using a specialized binocular microscope, allowing visualization of vascular patterns and iodine uptake abnormalities. During colposcopy, directed punch biopsies are taken from suspicious areas. If endocervical involvement is suspected or the transformation zone is not fully visible, an endocervical curettage (ECC) is performed. Histological confirmation through biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing cervical cancer.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment modalities depend heavily on the clinical stage of the tumor, patient age, and overall health status.

  • Surgical Interventions: Early-stage tumors are managed with fertility-sparing procedures like conoid biopsy or trachelectomy, or radical hysterectomy for patients who have completed childbearing.
  • Radiation Therapy: Utilizes external beam radiation therapy combined with brachytherapy (internal radioactive sources placed directly into the uterine cavity and vagina).
  • Chemotherapy: Often administered concurrently with radiation therapy to radiosensitize tumor cells and treat metastatic disease.

Nursing Care and Palliative Support

Comprehensive nursing care addresses the complex physical and emotional needs of patients undergoing treatment and those with advanced disease. Nurses manage acute treatment side effects, such as radiation-induced cystitis, proctitis, and bone marrow suppression. For patients with advanced or terminal cervical cancer, palliative care focuses on pain management using the WHO analgesic ladder, controlling malodorous vaginal discharge, and offering psychological support to the patient and their family.

Prevention Strategies and HPV Vaccination Programs

Primary prevention focuses on stopping HPV infection before it occurs. Prophylactic vaccines target major oncogenic types and are most effective when administered to adolescents before sexual debut. Community health campaigns must also dispel cultural misconceptions about vaccines and promote barrier contraceptives. While condoms reduce transmission risk, they do not provide absolute protection because HPV can infect skin areas not covered by barrier methods.
