Universal Precautions

Introduction to Universal Precautions and Standard Precautions

Universal Precautions were originally introduced by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 1985 to protect healthcare workers from bloodborne pathogens like HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C. The core concept treated all human blood and certain body fluids as if they were infected with these viruses. Over time, this concept evolved into Standard Precautions, a broader system that applies to all patients receiving care in any healthcare setting, regardless of their suspected or confirmed infection status.

Standard Precautions apply to:

  • Blood: All blood products and visible blood.
  • Body fluids: Semen, vaginal secretions, cerebrospinal fluid, and synovial fluid.
  • Non-intact skin: Open wounds, abrasions, and dermatitis.
  • Mucous membranes: Conjunctiva, oral cavity, and nasal passages.

Sweat is notably excluded from these precautions unless it contains visible blood. In busy Nigerian hospitals and primary health clinics, applying Standard Precautions to every single patient prevents cross-contamination and shields nurses from unexpected occupational exposures.

Rationale and Core Principles in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Healthcare delivery in Nigeria faces unique challenges, including infrastructural deficits, erratic water supply, and high patient-to-nurse ratios. In environments where resources are constrained, adhering to the core principles of infection prevention is the primary line of defense for both staff and patients.

The foundational principles dictate that:

  1. Every patient is treated as potentially infectious.
  2. Risk assessment must occur before every clinical procedure.
  3. Engineering controls and administrative policies must support safe practices.

A common misconception in under-resourced wards is that protective measures are only necessary when dealing with patients who look visibly ill or known to have a chronic infection. Nurses must understand that asymptomatic carriers shed pathogens daily. Maintaining strict barrier protection regardless of patient diagnosis protects the nursing workforce and preserves community health.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Selection and Proper Usage

Personal Protective Equipment acts as a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and hazardous biological agents. Selecting the correct item depends on the anticipated route of exposure during a specific clinical task.

PPE ItemPrimary Clinical IndicationKey Safety Rule
GlovesAnticipated contact with blood, fluids, or non-intact skinChange between patients; do not wash or reuse
Medical Masks & Eye ProtectionRisk of splashes or sprays into face and mucous membranesFit snugly over nose and mouth; remove without touching front
GownsRisk of clothing contamination during proceduresTie securely; remove carefully to avoid touching outer surface

Donning and doffing sequence matters immensely. Gloves are always put on last before patient contact and removed first after contact. In many Nigerian clinical settings, shortages can tempt staff to reuse disposable gloves or wash them with liquid soap and bleach. This practice compromises glove integrity through micro-punctures and must be avoided.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Clinical Best Practices

Hand hygiene is universally recognized as the single most effective intervention to interrupt the chain of infection in healthcare facilities. Microorganisms on hands are categorized into transient flora, acquired through direct contact with patients or contaminated surfaces, and resident flora, which live deep in the skin layers.

The World Health Organization promotes the Five Moments for Hand Hygiene:

  1. Before touching a patient.
  2. Before clean or aseptic procedures.
  3. After body fluid exposure risk.
  4. After touching a patient.
  5. After touching patient surroundings.

When hands are visibly soiled with blood or other body fluids, or when caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like *Clostridioides difficile*, nurses must wash with soap and running water for at least 40 to 60 seconds. Alcohol-based hand rubs are preferred when hands are not visibly soiled, requiring 20 to 30 seconds to cover all surfaces thoroughly.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Injury Prevention

Sharps injuries pose a severe occupational threat to nurses, midwives, and laboratory scientists through the potential transmission of Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Safe injection practices protect both healthcare workers and patients from preventable bloodborne infections.

Key preventive measures include:

  • Never recap needles: Using two hands to recap a used needle is a primary cause of accidental needle-stick injuries.
  • Use safety devices: Employ retractable needles or needleless systems whenever available in the facility.
  • Immediate disposal: Drop used syringes and needles directly into a puncture-resistant safety box located at the point of use.
  • Strict capacity limits: Never fill safety boxes beyond three-quarters (75%) capacity to prevent protruding needles from scratching passersby.

A prevalent dangerous practice in some clinics is leaving used needles resting on tray surfaces or overfilling safety boxes until lids can no longer close. Institutional enforcement of proper sharps disposal remains critical across all healthcare tiers.

Management of Healthcare Waste and Spills

Proper segregation of healthcare waste at the point of generation prevents environmental contamination and protects waste handlers and scavengers in the community. Color-coding systems categorize waste streams to ensure appropriate final treatment, such as incineration or autoclaving.

Standard waste segregation categories include:

  • Black or Brown Bins: General municipal waste, including food wrappers, paper, and non-hazardous office refuse.
  • Yellow Bins with Biohazard Symbol: Infectious waste, including cotton swabs, soiled dressings, gloves, and disposable linen contaminated with body fluids.
  • Yellow Safety Boxes: Sharps, needles, blades, and broken glass ampoules.

When blood or body fluid spills occur on hard floors, nurses must manage them promptly using chlorine-based disinfectants. First, put on heavy-duty utility gloves and PPE. Cover the spill with absorbent material, apply a 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution over the area, leave it for ten minutes of contact time, and then clear the waste into the infectious waste bin.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) and Incident Management

Despite rigorous preventive practices, accidental needle-stick injuries and mucosal splashes still occur. Immediate and calm management of an occupational exposure incident can mean the difference between infection and safety for a healthcare worker.

Immediate first aid steps following an exposure:

  1. Wash wounds: Clean puncture wounds immediately with soap and running water. Do not scrub or squeeze the wound.
  2. Flush mucous membranes: Rinse splashes to the eyes, nose, or mouth thoroughly with clean water or normal saline.
  3. Report the incident: Notify the infection control officer or ward manager immediately to document the event and assess the source patient's risk profile.
  4. Initiate PEP: If indicated, start antiretroviral medications for HIV exposure within 72 hours, preferably within the first few hours.

Delaying PEP diminishes its effectiveness. Facilities must maintain a clear, non-punitive reporting culture so staff feel safe reporting exposures without fear of reprimand.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Infection Control Compliance

Infection prevention extends far beyond the four walls of tertiary and secondary health facilities into primary healthcare centers and community outreach programs. Community health nurses play a pivotal role in educating families, traditional birth attendants, and local patent medicine vendors on safe hygiene practices.

Community-level strategies involve:

  • Conducting health talks on handwashing and safe water storage.
  • Training local birth attendants on disposable cord-cutting blades and basic asepsis.
  • Advocating for proper community waste disposal sites away from residential water sources.
  • Monitoring vaccine cold chains to maintain immunization coverage against preventable infections like Hepatitis B.

By empowering rural communities with simple, actionable knowledge, nurses bridge the gap between institutional policies and everyday grassroots behavior, significantly reducing the burden of infectious diseases nationwide.
