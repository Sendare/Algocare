Food Poisoning

Introduction and Definition of Food Poisoning

Food poisoning, medically known as foodborne illness, occurs when an individual consumes food or water contaminated with bacteria, viruses, parasites, or toxic chemicals. In clinical settings, it presents acutely with gastrointestinal distress. The condition ranges from mild, self-limiting discomfort to severe, life-threatening systemic illness requiring hospitalization. In Nigeria, outbreaks frequently occur during community celebrations, communal feasts, or from unregulated roadside food vending where hygiene standards lapse.

Etiology and Common Pathogens in Nigeria

  • Salmonella species: Commonly linked with undercooked poultry, eggs, and contaminated water sources.
  • Escherichia coli (E. coli): Frequently spread through poorly washed vegetables and faecally contaminated borehole water.
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Often introduced into cooked foods like rice or stew by food handlers with infected skin lesions.
  • Bacillus cereus: Associated with starchy foods, especially cooked rice left at room temperature for too long.
  • Chemical toxins: Accidental ingestion of pesticides, heavy metals, or poisonous mushrooms.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Modes

Pathogens enter the digestive tract via the feco-oral route, often mediated by contaminated hands, utensils, or water. Once inside the gastrointestinal tract, these microorganisms or their pre-formed enterotoxins irritate the mucosal lining. This irritation triggers local inflammatory responses, hypersecretion of electrolytes, and accelerated peristalsis. Some bacterial toxins cross the intestinal barrier, entering the bloodstream to cause systemic symptoms such as fever, myalgia, and neurological disturbances.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Symptoms usually appear a few hours to several days after ingestion, depending on the causative agent. Pre-formed toxins like those from Staphylococcus act within hours, whereas bacterial infections take days.

  • Sudden onset of nausea and vomiting
  • Crampy abdominal pain and severe colic
  • Watery or bloody diarrhea
  • Low-grade to high-grade fever and chills
  • Generalized body weakness and fatigue

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough nursing assessment focuses on identifying hydration status, vital sign instabilities, and a detailed dietary history of the past 24 to 72 hours. Nurses should check for signs of dehydration, including dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, and reduced urine output. Diagnostic tests ordered by the physician may include stool microscopy, culture and sensitivity, and blood electrolyte panels to evaluate fluid and electrolyte imbalances.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management centers on fluid resuscitation and supportive care. Oral rehydration therapy using Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) is sufficient for mild cases. Severe dehydration requires intravenous fluid replacement with normal saline or Ringer's lactate. Antiemetics and antidiarrheal medications are administered selectively, keeping in mind that stopping diarrhea caused by invasive bacteria can sometimes prolong the illness. Antibiotics are reserved for confirmed bacterial infections or high-risk patients.

Complications of Severe Food Poisoning

Untreated or severe food poisoning can escalate into life-threatening conditions. Hypovolemic shock develops secondary to profound fluid loss. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly severe hypokalemia or hyponatremia, can trigger cardiac arrhythmias and seizures. Specific pathogens like certain strains of E. coli can cause Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS), leading to acute kidney injury, hemolytic anemia, and thrombocytopenia.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Preventing food poisoning requires sustained public health education on food safety practices. Nurses play a vital role in teaching communities the principles of safe food handling.

  • Hand hygiene: Washing hands thoroughly with soap before cooking and eating.
  • Proper cooking: Ensuring meat, poultry, and fish are cooked to safe internal temperatures.
  • Food separation: Keeping raw meats away from ready-to-eat foods to prevent cross-contamination.
  • Safe storage: Refrigerating cooked food promptly and avoiding prolonged storage at room temperature.
  • Water safety: Drinking treated, boiled, or bottled water in areas with compromised public water systems.
