Gastroenteritis

Introduction and Definition of Gastroenteritis

Gastroenteritis is an inflammation of the mucous membrane lining the stomach and the intestines. Clinically, it presents as a sudden onset of diarrhea, frequently accompanied by nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and low-grade fever. In Nigerian clinical practice, acute episodes are a leading cause of pediatric hospital admissions, particularly during the rainy season and periods of water scarcity. Nurses must recognize that while the condition is often self-limiting in healthy adults, it carries a high risk of rapid, life-threatening dehydration in vulnerable populations such as infants, young children, and the elderly.

Etiology and Common Causative Pathogens in Nigeria

  • Rotavirus: The leading viral cause of severe, watery diarrhea among infants and young children under two years old.
  • Escherichia coli (Enterotoxigenic): A major bacterial culprit often linked to contaminated food and drinking water sources.
  • Vibrio cholerae: Responsible for epidemic outbreaks characterized by profuse 'rice-water' stools and rapid collapse.
  • Entamoeba histolytica: A protozoan parasite causing amoebic dysentery with blood and mucus in the stool.
  • Salmonella species: Bacterial pathogens transmitted through ingestion of undercooked foods or unhygienic food handling.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

Transmission of enteric pathogens typically occurs via the fecal-oral route. This happens through ingestion of contaminated water, unwashed fruits, or food handled with unwashed hands. Once ingested, pathogens bypass or overwhelm gastric acid barriers and colonize the intestinal mucosa. Depending on the specific agent, they either produce enterotoxins that trigger massive fluid secretion without cell death, or they invade the mucosal cells directly, causing inflammation, ulceration, and exudation of blood and mucus. This disruption impairs normal water and electrolyte absorption in the bowel, driving the characteristic diarrhea.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Patients present with varying degrees of illness depending on the causative agent and hydration status. Assessment must focus on evaluating signs of dehydration:

  • Skin turgor: Slow recoil indicates significant fluid loss.
  • Mucous membranes: Dry mouth and tongue reflect systemic dehydration.
  • Fontanelles: Sunken fontanelles in infants are a critical red flag.
  • Vital signs: Tachycardia and hypotension signal impending hypovolemic shock.
  • Output: Decreased or absent urine output over several hours indicates renal compromise.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Most cases of acute gastroenteritis are diagnosed clinically without extensive laboratory workups. However, targeted investigations become necessary when symptoms are severe, prolonged, or bloody. Stool microscopy, culture, and sensitivity tests help identify bacterial or parasitic agents. Serum electrolytes, urea, and creatinine tests evaluate kidney function and electrolyte imbalances like hypokalemia or hyponatremia. Packed cell volume may be checked to assess hemoconcentration resulting from severe dehydration.

Medical and Nursing Management

The cornerstone of gastroenteritis management is fluid replacement. Oral rehydration therapy is highly effective for mild to moderate dehydration. Severe dehydration requires prompt intravenous fluid resuscitation with normal saline or Ringer's lactate. Nurses must monitor intravenous sites closely to prevent infiltration and track strict fluid intake and output charts. Zinc supplementation is routinely administered to children in developing countries to reduce the duration and severity of diarrheal episodes. Antibiotics are withheld in viral cases and reserved strictly for confirmed bacterial or parasitic infections.

Complications of Gastroenteritis

Uncontrolled fluid loss leads rapidly to hypovolemic shock, prerenal acute kidney injury, and multiple organ failure. Severe electrolyte shifts, particularly profound hypokalemia or hypernatremia, can trigger dangerous cardiac arrhythmias and neurological seizures. Persistent mucosal damage can also result in temporary secondary lactase deficiency or prolonged malabsorption syndromes, further compromising the nutritional status of recovering children.

Community Prevention and Control Strategies

Preventing gastroenteritis requires robust public health measures, especially in resource-constrained settings. Access to safe, treated drinking water and proper sanitation infrastructure is vital. Hand hygiene promotion—specifically washing hands with soap after using the toilet and before preparing food—breaks the fecal-oral transmission chain. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months builds infant immunity against enteric infections. Furthermore, community immunization programs incorporating the rotavirus vaccine significantly reduce the burden of severe childhood diarrhea.
