Transmission-Based Precautions

Introduction to Transmission-Based Precautions

Transmission-Based Precautions are the second tier of basic infection control used in healthcare settings. They are applied when the routes of transmission are not completely interrupted by standard precautions alone. These measures target specific pathogens based on how they spread, whether through direct contact, respiratory droplets, or airborne nuclei. Recognizing these pathways early helps prevent nosocomial infections among vulnerable patients and healthcare workers alike.

Standard Precautions as the Foundation

Standard precautions form the core of safe patient care and apply to all patients regardless of their diagnosis or infection status. They treat all body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes as potential sources of infectious agents. Key elements include rigorous hand hygiene, safe injection practices, and the appropriate use of personal protective equipment whenever exposure is anticipated. Skipping these fundamental steps increases the risk of cross-contamination across the entire ward.

Contact Precautions: Indications and Implementation

  • Indications: Used for infections spread by direct patient contact or indirect contact with environmental surfaces, such as multi-drug resistant organisms, Clostridioides difficile, and scabies.
  • Barrier Methods: Healthcare workers must wear gloves and a long-sleeved gown upon entering the room and remove them before leaving.
  • Equipment Dedication: Stethoscopes, blood pressure cuffs, and thermometers should remain in the room and be disinfected before use on another patient.

Droplet Precautions: Pathogens and Barrier Methods

Droplet precautions are necessary for diseases transmitted through large respiratory particles generated during coughing, sneezing, or talking. These droplets travel short distances, usually one meter or less, and do not remain suspended in the air.

  1. Pathogens: Influenza, pertussis, and Neisseria meningitidis.
  2. Mask Requirement: Put on a standard surgical mask when entering the patient's room.
  3. Patient Placement: Place the patient in a private room or cohort them with someone having the same infection.

Airborne Precautions: Engineering Controls and Respiratory Protection

Airborne precautions manage tiny droplet nuclei that remain infectious over long distances and time in the air. These infections demand specialized engineering controls to protect staff and other patients in the facility.

A common misconception is that a regular surgical mask protects against airborne pathogens; however, surgical masks lack the filtration capacity and tight facial seal required to block microscopic nuclei.

Proper Donning and Doffing of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Putting on and taking off personal protective equipment safely prevents self-contamination during patient care. Donning follows an order that ensures maximum protection before contact, typically starting with the gown, followed by the mask, eye protection, and finally gloves. Doffing reverses this process with careful attention to hand hygiene between steps. Gloves and gowns are removed first because they carry the highest pathogen load, followed by eye protection and the respirator outside the patient room.

Patient Placement, Transport, and Environmental Disinfection

Managing the physical environment is vital to containing infectious agents within a healthcare facility. When private isolation rooms are unavailable, patients with identical active infections may be cohorted together. Transporting an infected patient outside their room should be restricted to medically necessary procedures only, requiring the patient to wear a barrier mask or cover lesions. Environmental surfaces must undergo rigorous cleaning and terminal disinfection using approved hospital-grade disinfectants to eliminate residual pathogens.

Infection Control in Nigerian Community Health Settings

Implementing transmission-based precautions in resource-limited Nigerian primary healthcare centers presents unique operational hurdles. Frequent water shortages, erratic electricity supply, and irregular PPE supplies require nurses to adapt without compromising core safety principles. Improvised handwashing stations using tippy-taps, locally prepared chlorine solutions for surface decontamination, and prioritizing barrier use for high-risk procedures help bridge safety gaps in rural clinics.
