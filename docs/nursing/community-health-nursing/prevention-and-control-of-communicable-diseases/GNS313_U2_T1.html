Concept of Communicable Diseases

Introduction to Communicable Diseases

A communicable disease is an illness caused by a specific infectious agent or its toxic products that can be transmitted from an infected person, animal, or inanimate reservoir to a susceptible host, either directly or indirectly. These diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in developing regions like Nigeria where environmental and socio-economic factors facilitate pathogen spread. Understanding the basic biology of pathogens—including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites—is fundamental for clinical assessment and public health planning. Nurses must recognize that these conditions disrupt normal physiological function and frequently trigger community-wide outbreaks if containment measures fail. The presence of a communicable disease depends on the dynamic interplay between the infectious agent, the human host, and the surrounding environment.

The Epidemiological Triangle and Chain of Infection

Infectious disease transmission relies on a predictable sequence of events and factors. The epidemiological triangle consists of three interactive elements:

  • Agent: The micro-organism responsible for the disease, such as bacteria or viruses.
  • Host: The human or animal that harbors the infectious agent.
  • Environment: External factors that allow the agent to thrive and spread.

Meanwhile, the chain of infection breaks down the transmission process into specific links: the infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host. Breaking any single link in this chain stops an outbreak.

Modes of Transmission in the Community

Pathogens travel from their source to a new host through various routes. Direct transmission involves immediate physical transfer between an infected individual and a susceptible host, such as through touching, kissing, or sexual intercourse. Indirect transmission requires a vehicle or intermediary, which can be animate or inanimate.

  • Airborne: Pathogens carried through dust or droplet nuclei suspended in the air over longer distances.
  • Vector-borne: Transmission via living organisms like mosquitoes, ticks, or fleas.
  • Vehicle-borne: Spread through contaminated items such as water, food, or medical equipment.
  • Droplet: Large respiratory particles propelled short distances through coughing or sneezing.

Stages of Disease Progression and Host Susceptibility

Once a pathogen enters a host, the infection follows a predictable timeline. The incubation period spans from the initial entry of the pathogen to the appearance of the first symptoms. Following this, the prodromal period brings mild, non-specific signs of illness. The illness phase displays the full-blown, classic signs and symptoms specific to the pathology. Finally, the convalescent period marks recovery and tissue repair. Host susceptibility is influenced by age, nutritional status, underlying health conditions, and immune competence. Malnourished individuals or those with compromised immune systems face a much higher risk of severe disease progression.

Classification and Common Types of Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Nigeria carries a high burden of various communicable diseases that impact public health daily. These are broadly categorized by their causative agents and primary transmission routes. Malaria, caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, remains endemic. Tuberculosis, a bacterial infection affecting the lungs, spreads via airborne droplets. Cholera outbreaks occur periodically, linked to contaminated water and poor sanitation infrastructure. Vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles and polio require continuous immunization campaigns. Viral hemorrhagic fevers like Lassa fever present significant nosocomial and community hazards, particularly in areas with rodent reservoirs.

Epidemiological Surveillance and Disease Reporting

Surveillance is the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential for planning, implementation, and evaluation of public health practice. In Nigeria, the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework guides healthcare workers in tracking priority diseases. Nurses play a frontline role in identifying unusual disease patterns and reporting notifiable conditions to local health authorities. Prompt notification allows rapid containment and resource mobilization during outbreaks. Without accurate data collection, health ministries cannot allocate vaccines, medications, or personnel effectively.

Prevention and Control Strategies in Community Health Nursing

Community health nurses utilize multiple layers of prevention to combat communicable diseases. Primary prevention aims to prevent disease occurrence through health education, environmental sanitation, and routine immunization. Secondary prevention focuses on early diagnosis and prompt treatment through screening programs and contact tracing, which halts disease progression. Tertiary prevention minimizes the complications and long-term disabilities of established infections through effective clinical management and rehabilitation. Isolation and quarantine are vital administrative measures used to separate infected or exposed individuals from the general population during outbreaks.
