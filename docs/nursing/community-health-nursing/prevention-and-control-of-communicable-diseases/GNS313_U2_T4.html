Isolation of Patients

Introduction and Objectives of Patient Isolation

Patient isolation is a core infection prevention strategy used to separate infected or colonized patients from susceptible individuals. The primary objective is breaking the chain of infection in healthcare facilities, stopping pathogens from spreading to other patients, staff, and visitors. Effective isolation protects vulnerable populations, such as immunocompromised patients, from acquiring hospital-acquired infections.

Standard Precautions vs. Transmission-Based Precautions

Infection control relies on a two-tier system of precautions applied across all healthcare settings:

  • Standard Precautions: Applied to all patients receiving care regardless of their diagnosis. These assume that every body fluid, non-intact skin, and mucous membrane may contain transmissible infectious agents.
  • Transmission-Based Precautions: Designed for patients documented or suspected to be infected with pathogens spread by airborne, droplet, or contact routes. These are used in addition to standard precautions.

Categories of Transmission-Based Precautions

Transmission-based precautions target specific routes through which pathogens travel. Understanding these categories guides the selection of barriers and room designs:

  1. Contact Precautions: Used for infections spread by direct or indirect contact with the patient or their environment, requiring gloves and gowns.
  2. Droplet Precautions: Used for pathogens transmitted through close respiratory droplets, requiring surgical masks during care.
  3. Airborne Precautions: Used for tiny droplet nuclei that remain suspended in the air over long distances, requiring N95 respirators and negative pressure rooms.

Use and Management of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment serves as physical barriers between healthcare workers and infectious hazards. Correct sequencing during donning and doffing prevents self-contamination. Nurses must don PPE before entering the room and remove it carefully before exiting, followed immediately by hand hygiene.

Design and Environmental Control of Isolation Rooms

Isolation rooms utilize specialized ventilation and environmental engineering to control pathogen movement. Airborne infection isolation rooms use negative pressure to prevent contaminated air from escaping into corridors. Conversely, protective environments use positive pressure to keep outside air from entering and harming immunocompromised occupants.

Psychosocial Care and Rights of Isolated Patients

Isolation often leads to sensory deprivation, anxiety, depression, and feelings of punishment. Nurses must uphold patient rights by ensuring regular communication, providing entertainment, and explaining the clinical necessity of barrier measures. Compassionate interaction helps mitigate the psychological toll of restricted social contact.
