Sources of Health Care Financing in Nigeria

Overview of Health Care Financing in Nigeria

Health care financing involves raising, pooling, and allocating funds to cover health needs within a population. In Nigeria, the health system relies on a complex mix of funding mechanisms, leaving many citizens vulnerable to catastrophic health costs. The country operates a three-tier system of government—federal, state, and local—each with distinct responsibilities for health funding and service delivery. Despite various national policies, chronic underfunding remains a defining feature of the sector. Total health expenditure consistently falls far below the 15% Abuja Declaration target agreed upon by African Union member states. Understanding these financial streams is vital for nurses advocating for resource allocation and better patient care outcomes.

Government and Public Sector Financing

Public sector financing originates from government revenues generated through taxes, tariffs, and oil proceeds. These funds are distributed through budgetary allocations to federal, state, and local government health facilities. Tertiary teaching hospitals receive a significant portion of federal allocations, while primary health care centers often depend on struggling local government councils. Bureaucratic bottlenecks and delayed fund releases frequently hinder the effective use of these public resources. Consequently, public facilities often experience severe shortages of basic drugs, functional diagnostic equipment, and reliable power supplies.

Out-of-Pocket Expenditure and Its Implications

Out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure refers to direct payments made by individuals at the point of receiving health care services, without any prior prepayment or third-party coverage. In Nigeria, OOP constitutes the largest share of total health expenditures. This reliance on cash payments creates severe barriers to care, especially for low-income households.

  • Impoverishment: Sudden illness forces families to sell assets or borrow money, plunging them into poverty.
  • Delayed Care: Patients often postpone seeking medical attention until conditions become critical or life-threatening.
  • Inequity: Vulnerable groups like pregnant women and children miss essential preventive services due to cost.

Health Insurance Schemes: National and State Frameworks

Health insurance is designed to pool financial risks and protect individuals from sudden, heavy medical expenses. The National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) oversees formal sector coverage, mainly for federal government workers, though enrollment remains low among the informal sector. To bridge this gap, many state governments have established State Health Insurance Agencies (SHIAs) to drive universal health coverage at the sub-national level. Basic Minimum Package of Health Services (BMPHS) aims to guarantee essential care for vulnerable populations. However, mandatory compliance enforcement and public trust remain major hurdles for these schemes.

Donor Funding and International Development Assistance

External funding plays a massive role in sustaining specific disease control programs across Nigeria. International partners, including bilateral agencies, multilateral organizations, and global philanthropies, inject billions of naira into the health sector. These funds typically target priority areas such as HIV/AIDS, malaria, tuberculosis, and immunization campaigns. While this support saves countless lives, it introduces significant sustainability risks. When external grants phase out or donor priorities shift, national programs often struggle to maintain operations without domestic replacement funding.

Private Sector and Corporate Social Responsibility

The private health sector in Nigeria ranges from small proprietary clinics and retail pharmacies to large specialist hospitals. Private entities capture a substantial share of health care delivery, particularly in urban areas. In parallel, Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) initiatives by telecommunications, banking, and oil companies frequently fund health infrastructure, equipment donations, and medical outreaches. Although private providers offer prompt and often preferred services, care quality varies widely. Regulatory enforcement is crucial to ensure that private facilities maintain safety standards and do not exploit vulnerable patients.

Community-Based Health Financing and Prepaid Schemes

Community-based health insurance (CBHI) schemes are localized prepayment mechanisms designed for rural dwellers and informal sector workers who lack formal insurance. Members make small, regular financial contributions into a pooled community fund, which is then used to cover basic health care expenses when they fall ill. These schemes foster community ownership, solidarity, and trust. However, they often struggle with small risk pools, limited financial reserves, and difficulties in covering expensive secondary or tertiary treatments.

Challenges and Pathways to Sustainable Health Financing

Achieving sustainable health financing in Nigeria requires confronting systemic bottlenecks that drain resources and block universal access. Corruption, inefficient fund allocation, and poor infrastructure continue to undermine health sector gains. Expanding domestic resource mobilization through innovative taxes, strengthening public-private partnerships, and enforcing mandatory health insurance enrollment offer viable pathways forward. Nurses play a crucial role in advocating for transparent resource management and equitable care distribution at all levels of the health system.
