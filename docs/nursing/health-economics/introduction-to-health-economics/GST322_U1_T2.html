Supply and Demand in Health Services

Introduction to Health Economics and Market Dynamics

Health economics applies economic principles to the allocation of scarce resources in the health sector. Unlike standard consumer goods, healthcare involves high uncertainty, information asymmetry between providers and patients, and the presence of third-party payers like the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) in Nigeria. Market dynamics in healthcare dictate how services are produced, distributed, and consumed, balancing the needs of vulnerable populations with the financial sustainability of health facilities.

Concept of Demand in Healthcare Services

Demand in healthcare refers to the quantity of health services that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various price levels over a given time period. In clinical practice, a distinction exists between felt need, expressed demand, and actual utilization. Patients often demand healthcare not because they desire it for pleasure, but because they seek utility in the form of restored health and relief from suffering.

Determinants of Healthcare Demand

Several non-price factors influence how much healthcare a population demands. Understanding these drivers helps nurses and facility managers anticipate patient influxes:

  • Income level: Higher disposable income generally increases the demand for elective and preventative services.
  • Health status: Sicker individuals or aging populations inherently require more medical interventions.
  • Price and out-of-pocket costs: High user fees discourage utilization, whereas health insurance coverage increases demand.
  • Cultural beliefs: Traditional practices and perceptions about modern medicine shape health-seeking behavior.

Concept of Supply in Healthcare Services

Supply represents the total quantity of healthcare services—such as hospital beds, nursing hours, diagnostic tests, and consultations—that providers are willing and able to offer to the market at various prices. In public sector hospitals across Nigeria, supply is often determined by government budgetary allocations rather than pure market pricing. Private facilities, however, adjust their service output based on expected revenues and operational costs.

Determinants of Healthcare Supply

The availability of healthcare services depends on critical inputs and operational constraints. Key determinants include:

  • Technology and equipment: Access to modern diagnostic machines enhances facility capabilities.
  • Input costs: Prices of pharmaceuticals, consumables, and utility bills affect operational viability.
  • Human resources: The density and distribution of doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals restrict service volume.
  • Government policy: Regulations, licensing fees, and importation tariffs influence provider entry and expansion.

Market Equilibrium and Price Determination in Healthcare

Market equilibrium occurs when the quantity of healthcare services demanded equals the quantity supplied. In a theoretical free market, prices adjust until shortages or surpluses disappear. In reality, government price controls, subsidies, and non-profit healthcare missions prevent pure equilibrium from forming. When prices are artificially set too low by policy, demand outstrips supply, resulting in long waiting lists and overcrowded wards.

Peculiarities and Market Failures in Healthcare Supply and Demand

The healthcare market deviates significantly from standard economic models due to unique market failures. Externalities occur when one person's health action affects others, such as vaccination protecting the broader community. Monopoly power often arises when specialized tertiary hospitals face no local competitors, allowing them to charge high rates. Furthermore, severe information asymmetry leaves patients reliant on providers to decide both the need for care and its volume.

Implications for Nursing Practice and Healthcare Management

Recognizing supply and demand dynamics helps nurses optimize resource allocation at the ward level. Ward managers must schedule nursing staff according to peak demand hours rather than fixed traditional shifts. Understanding economic constraints also equips nurses to advocate for cost-effective care, minimize waste of consumables, and educate patients on utilizing primary healthcare centers before seeking expensive tertiary interventions.
