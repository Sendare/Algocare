Recognizing Data

Introduction to Research Data

Research data forms the empirical foundation of nursing science and evidence-based practice. In clinical studies, data represents the collected pieces of information, measurements, or observations gathered from patients, hospital records, or community settings. Without accurate data collection, nursing interventions cannot be properly evaluated for safety or effectiveness. Data can be as simple as a single blood pressure reading on a ward chart or as complex as a transcribed audio recording from an interview with a patient recovering from a stroke. Recognizing the nature of data helps nurses critically appraise journal articles and apply valid research findings to bedside care.

Quantitative versus Qualitative Data

Clinical data generally splits into two broad categories based on its form and how it is analyzed. Quantitative data deals with numbers, counts, and measurable quantities. Qualitative data deals with words, meanings, experiences, and descriptive observations.

  • Quantitative Data: Expressed numerically, such as body temperature in degrees Celsius, heart rate per minute, or a patient's pain score on a scale from zero to ten.
  • Qualitative Data: Captured through narrative descriptions, focus group transcripts, or field notes, such as a patient describing how it feels to live with chronic leg ulcers.

Levels of Measurement

Variables are measured at different levels, which dictate the mathematical operations you can perform on the data. These four levels range from basic categorization to precise numerical measurement with a true zero.

NominalCategories with no numerical rankingBlood type (A, B, AB, O), gender
OrdinalOrdered categories or ranksPain scale (mild, moderate, severe), NYHA heart failure class

Primary versus Secondary Data Sources

The source from which a researcher obtains data influences its reliability and relevance to the current study.

  • Primary Data: Collected firsthand by the researcher for the specific current study. Examples include administering a questionnaire about handwashing practices directly to ward nurses or taking direct blood pressure measurements in a clinic.
  • Secondary Data: Originally gathered by someone else for another purpose but used by the current researcher. Examples include reviewing patient admission registers, consulting national health statistics, or analyzing electronic health records from past years.

Identifying Variables and Data Types

Recognizing the type of variable in a study dictates how the data should be handled and analyzed. Independent variables are the presumed cause or intervention, while dependent variables represent the measured outcome or effect. A common misconception is that a variable is permanently classified as either independent or dependent; however, its role depends entirely on the research question. For instance, blood pressure might be the dependent outcome in a study testing a new antihypertensive drug, but it could be an independent variable in a study examining the risk of stroke.

Data Screening and Quality Verification

Before any statistical analysis begins, raw data must undergo rigorous screening to check for errors, missing values, and outliers. Data entry mistakes can severely skew study results and lead to false clinical conclusions. Nurses involved in research must inspect data sheets for impossible entries, such as a patient's age recorded as 250 years or a negative heart rate. Ensuring data integrity protects the validity of nursing research and prevents flawed evidence from entering clinical guidelines.
