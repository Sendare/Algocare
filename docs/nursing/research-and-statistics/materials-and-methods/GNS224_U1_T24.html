Interview

Introduction to Interviews in Nursing Research

Interviews are a fundamental data collection method in qualitative nursing research, allowing investigators to capture the lived experiences, perceptions, and values of patients, families, and healthcare professionals. Unlike quantitative surveys that rely on closed-ended numeric responses, interviews use open dialogue to explore complex health phenomena that numbers cannot fully capture. A novice researcher might assume interviews are simply informal conversations, but they are purposeful, systematic interactions requiring active listening, careful probing, and adherence to strict methodological frameworks. In Nigerian clinical settings, qualitative interviews help uncover culturally specific health beliefs, coping mechanisms, and barriers to care that standard questionnaires frequently miss.

Types of Research Interviews: Structured, Semi-Structured, and Unstructured

Research interviews vary along a continuum of structure, influencing the flexibility of data collection and the depth of qualitative insight.

  • Structured interviews: Feature a rigid set of pre-determined questions asked in the exact same order with no deviation, offering little room for spontaneous probing.
  • Semi-structured interviews: Utilize an interview guide with flexible open-ended questions, allowing the nurse researcher to explore emergent topics and follow interesting conversational paths.
  • Unstructured interviews: Begin with a single broad opening question and let the participant direct the narrative entirely, demanding high skill to keep the discussion relevant to the study aim.

Designing the Interview Guide and Question Formulation

An interview guide is a written list of topics, themes, and open-ended questions designed to keep the research conversation on track. Crafting effective questions requires avoiding leading or double-barreled prompts that confuse participants or bias their answers. For instance, asking 'Why do you refuse to take your hypertension medication?' assumes fault, whereas asking 'Can you walk me through your thoughts on taking your daily medication?' invites an open, non-judgmental response. Pilot testing the interview guide with a small group of colleagues or similar patients helps identify confusing terminology, awkward phrasing, and culturally inappropriate expressions before actual data collection begins.

Preparation and Recruitment of Participants in Healthcare Settings

Recruiting participants for health research demands sensitivity, proper clearance, and adherence to ethical guidelines. Researchers must obtain institutional approval from hospital management or ethics committees before approaching patients in wards or clinics. Selecting participants who have direct, rich experience with the phenomenon under study—known as purposive sampling—ensures the data collected is deeply informative. In busy Nigerian hospital environments, nurses acting as researchers must ensure recruitment conversations happen away from clinical duties, protecting patient privacy and making it clear that participation is entirely voluntary and distinct from their routine medical care.

Conducting the Interview: Techniques and Establishing Rapport

Building trust and establishing a comfortable environment are prerequisites for gathering honest qualitative data. Rapport begins with respectful greetings, clear explanations of the study purpose, and reassurance regarding confidentiality. During the conversation, active listening techniques—such as nodding, maintaining open body language, and using encouraging minimal verbal prompts like 'go on' or 'tell me more'—encourage elaboration. Researchers must manage their own biases and facial expressions, avoiding judgmental reactions to sensitive disclosures about traditional practices, non-adherence, or personal fears.

Ethical Considerations in Qualitative Health Interviews

Qualitative interviews carry unique ethical challenges because personal stories can easily touch on trauma, vulnerability, or illegal health practices. Informed consent must be an ongoing process, meaning participants can withdraw at any point without losing access to healthcare services. Protecting participant anonymity requires careful handling of audio recordings, transcripts, and field notes, replacing real names with pseudonyms and removing identifying demographic markers. When interviews explore distressing topics, the researcher must be prepared to provide psychological support or appropriate referrals to counseling services.

Data Recording, Transcription, and Management

Accurate capture of interview data is essential for credible qualitative analysis. Audio recording devices should be tested prior to the session, accompanied by detailed field notes that capture non-verbal cues, environmental context, and emotional tone. Transcription involves converting audio recordings into verbatim written text, a meticulous process that preserves exact local phrasing, pauses, and expressions. Managing qualitative data securely involves storing encrypted audio files and password-protected transcripts on secure servers to prevent unauthorized access and maintain participant confidentiality.

Advantages, Limitations, and Strategies to Ensure Rigor

Interviews provide rich, detailed data that quantitative tools cannot match, but they are also vulnerable to researcher bias, subjective interpretation, and high time consumption. To ensure rigor and trustworthiness, researchers apply strategies such as member checking—taking transcribed interpretations back to participants to confirm accuracy—and peer debriefing with senior colleagues. Maintaining an audit trail documenting every methodological decision helps prove that findings are grounded in participant data rather than researcher imagination, establishing the overall credibility of the nursing study.
