Qualitative Research Design

Introduction to Qualitative Research Design in Nursing

Qualitative research focuses on understanding human experiences, meanings, and behaviors within natural settings. Unlike quantitative research that relies heavily on numerical data and statistical analysis, qualitative studies use words, narratives, and observations. In Nigerian nursing practice, this design helps clinicians explore patient perspectives on illness, cultural beliefs surrounding health, and lived experiences of chronic conditions. It answers questions starting with 'how' and 'what' rather than 'how much'.

Major Qualitative Approaches: Phenomenology, Ethnography, and Grounded Theory

  • Phenomenology: Explores the lived experience of a specific phenomenon. A nurse researcher might use this to study what it feels like to be diagnosed with HIV in a rural Nigerian community.
  • Ethnography: Investigates cultural patterns, behaviors, and values of a specific group by immersing the researcher in their environment over time.
  • Grounded Theory: Focuses on generating a theory directly from data collected in the field, often explaining a psychosocial process such as how patients decide to seek orthodox versus traditional care.

Purposive and Theoretical Sampling Techniques

Qualitative sampling is rarely random. Instead, researchers select participants deliberately based on their specific knowledge, experience, or characteristics related to the study topic.

  1. Purposive sampling: Selecting individuals who have direct experience with the phenomenon of interest, such as nurses working in an intensive care unit.
  2. Theoretical sampling: Used primarily in grounded theory, where the collection of new data is guided by emerging concepts and categories from previous data analysis.
  3. Snowbreading/Snowball sampling: Asking current participants to refer other suitable candidates, useful when studying hidden or hard-to-reach populations.

Data Collection Methods: Interviews, Focus Groups, and Observations

Data collection in qualitative research requires active listening, patience, and strong communication skills.

  • In-depth interviews: One-on-one conversations using open-ended questions to allow participants to share detailed personal accounts.
  • Focus groups: Group discussions facilitated by the researcher to observe group dynamics, shared beliefs, and differing viewpoints on a health topic.
  • Observations: Watching participants in their natural environment to capture actual behaviors rather than just reported descriptions.

Trustworthiness and Rigor in Qualitative Studies

Because qualitative research does not use traditional quantitative measures like validity and reliability, it relies on the concept of trustworthiness established by Lincoln and Guba.

  • Credibility: Confidence in the truth of the findings, often achieved through member checking and peer debriefing.
  • Transferability: Showing that findings can be applied to other contexts or settings.
  • Dependability: Ensuring the research process is logical, traceable, and clearly documented.
  • Confirmability: Ensuring findings represent the participants' responses rather than the researcher's personal biases.

Thematic and Content Data Analysis Strategies

Qualitative data analysis transforms raw audio recordings and field notes into meaningful themes. The process generally begins with transcribing interviews verbatim. The researcher then reads and rereads the text to achieve immersion, codes segments of data, and groups similar codes into broader categories. Finally, these categories are refined into distinct themes that capture the essence of the phenomenon under study.

Ethical Considerations in Qualitative Nursing Research

Ethical principles are paramount when conducting qualitative research because interviews often delve into sensitive personal stories. Researchers must secure informed consent, protect participant anonymity, and maintain strict confidentiality, especially when publishing direct quotes. Furthermore, nurses must remain mindful of the dual role dilemma where a researcher might accidentally function as a clinician during data collection.
