Non-Experimental Research Design

Introduction and Purpose in Nursing Research

Non-experimental research design involves studying variables in their natural settings without any manipulation by the researcher. In nursing, you often cannot or should not alter a patient's condition, environment, or treatment just to see what happens. Instead, you observe, measure, and describe phenomena as they naturally occur. The main goal is to uncover relationships, describe clinical realities, or explore differences between patient groups without introducing an active intervention.

Descriptive Research Designs

Descriptive designs paint a picture of a situation, phenomenon, or population as it currently exists. They do not test hypotheses about cause and effect, nor do they look for relationships between variables. Common nursing examples include documenting the prevalence of pressure ulcers in a surgical ward or assessing the anxiety levels of mothers in a postnatal clinic.

  • Univariate Descriptive: Focuses on describing a single variable, such as measuring baseline body temperature.
  • Normative Research: Establishes normal values or benchmarks for a specific population over time.
  • Prevalence Studies: Measures the frequency of a condition within a defined group at a specific point in time.

Correlational and Associational Designs

Correlational research explores the relationship between two or more variables without establishing causation. A correlation coefficient ranges from -1.00 to +1.00, indicating the strength and direction of the association. A positive correlation means both variables move in the same direction, while a negative correlation means one increases as the other decreases.

A common clinical misconception is assuming that finding a strong correlation means one variable caused the other. For instance, a study might show a strong link between high patient fall rates and low nursing staffing numbers, but this association does not automatically prove understaffing is the sole direct cause.

Ex Post Facto and Causal-Comparative Designs

An ex post facto or causal-comparative design examines how an independent variable, present before the study, affects a dependent variable. The term 'ex post facto' translates to 'after the fact.' Because the event has already occurred, the researcher cannot manipulate the independent variable.

For example, a researcher might compare current lung function in patients who smoked for ten years versus those who never smoked. The smoking behavior happened in the past, and the researcher simply compares the groups now. The major limitation here is the inability to control for confounding variables that might have influenced the outcome.

Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Approaches

Timeframe plays a vital role in organizing non-experimental studies. Researchers choose between taking a quick snapshot or following a group over an extended period.

  • Cross-Sectional: Collects data at one specific point in time, offering quick results but missing developmental trends.
  • Longitudinal: Follows the same participants over multiple time points to observe changes, though it suffers from participant attrition.

Strengths and Limitations

Non-experimental research offers high external validity because studies occur in real-world clinical settings rather than artificial laboratories. They provide vast amounts of descriptive data and pave the way for future experimental trials. However, their lack of control over extraneous variables leaves them open to bias. Researchers cannot prove causality, meaning that even a well-designed correlational study only points toward associations rather than definitive causes.
