Introduction to Sampling in Nursing Research

Nursing research relies heavily on gathering data to improve patient care and clinical outcomes. Studying an entire population of interest is rarely practical due to time, cost, and accessibility constraints. Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of individuals from a larger group to represent that population accurately. A well-chosen sample allows researchers to draw valid conclusions about the broader clinical community without needing to observe every single patient or nurse.

Target Population Versus Accessible Population

Distinguishing between the groups under study prevents confusion in research design.

  • Target Population: The entire aggregate of cases that the researcher is interested in and wishes to generalize findings to.
  • Accessible Population: The portion of the target population that is actually available and reachable for the study.

For example, a researcher might target all diabetic patients in Nigeria, but the accessible population becomes all diabetic patients attending the outpatient clinic at Lagos University Teaching Hospital.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Establishing boundaries for participant selection ensures the study measures what it intends to measure safely and accurately.

  1. Inclusion Criteria: The specific characteristics that prospective participants must possess to be eligible for the study, such as age range, specific diagnosis, or ability to give informed consent.
  2. Exclusion Criteria: The conditions or characteristics that disqualify a prospective participant, such as severe cognitive impairment, concurrent critical illness, or unwillingness to participate, which might confound the study results.

Probability Sampling Techniques

Probability sampling relies on random selection, giving every member of the accessible population a known and equal chance of being chosen. This approach minimizes selection bias and strengthens the generalizability of the findings.

  • Simple Random Sampling: Every individual is assigned a number, and selection occurs purely by chance, such as through a lottery method.
  • Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into homogenous subgroups (strata), and random samples are drawn from each subgroup.
  • Systematic Sampling: Every *k*-th person is selected from a complete list of the population after a random starting point.

Non-Probability Sampling Techniques

When random selection is impractical or impossible, researchers use non-probability sampling. Participants are chosen based on non-random criteria, meaning not everyone has a chance of inclusion.

  • Convenience Sampling: Selecting readily available people who happen to be nearby and willing to participate.
  • Purposive Sampling: Hand-picking individuals who possess specific knowledge or characteristics relevant to the study.
  • Snowball Sampling: Existing study subjects recruit future participants from among their acquaintances.

Sample Size Determination and Power

Determining how many participants to include is critical for valid research. A sample that is too small may fail to detect real clinical differences, while an unnecessarily large sample wastes resources. Statistical power is the probability that a study will detect an effect when a true effect exists, usually set at 0.80 or higher. Factors influencing sample size include the chosen study design, expected effect size, significance level, and the anticipated rate of participant attrition.

Sampling Bias and Error

Research findings can be distorted by flaws in how participants are chosen. Sampling error is the natural variation that occurs when a sample does not perfectly mirror its population, which decreases as sample size increases. Sampling bias, however, occurs systematically when certain members of a population are systematically more likely to be included than others, undermining the validity and trustworthiness of the research conclusions.
