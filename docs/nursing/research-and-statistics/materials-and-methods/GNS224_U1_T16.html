Population

Introduction to Population in Nursing Research

In nursing research, a population refers to the entire aggregate of cases that meet a designated set of criteria. Unlike everyday usage where population means all people living in a country like Nigeria, a research population can consist of patients, pregnant women, practicing nurses, or even hospital records. Defining this group clearly is the very first step before planning any data collection in a community or clinical ward.

Target Population Versus Accessible Population

A target population is the entire aggregate of cases about which the researcher would like to generalize findings. For instance, all diabetic patients in Nigeria form a target population. However, studying every single one of them is practically impossible due to financial and geographical constraints.

An accessible population is the portion of the target population that is actually available to the researcher. If a nurse researcher in Lagos studies diabetic patients attending clinics at Lagos University Teaching Hospital, that specific clinic group is the accessible population.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Researchers set boundaries using specific criteria to ensure participant safety and study validity.

  • Inclusion criteria: The specific characteristics a subject must possess to be part of the study, such as being diagnosed with hypertension for at least two years.
  • Exclusion criteria: Characteristics that disqualify a potential participant, such as pregnancy or severe cognitive impairment, which might skew the study results or pose ethical risks.

Sampling Techniques and Frame Development

A sampling frame is the actual list of all elements or members in the accessible population from which the sample is drawn, such as an institutional register of nursing students. From this list, researchers apply various sampling techniques.

  1. Simple random sampling: Every member has an equal and independent chance of being chosen.
  2. Stratified random sampling: The population is divided into subgroups, and random samples are drawn from each.
  3. Convenience sampling: Participants are selected simply because they are easy to reach.

Determining Sample Size in Healthcare Studies

Choosing how many participants to recruit is a critical decision in nursing research. If a sample is too small, the study may lack statistical power to detect real differences in patient outcomes. Conversely, an excessively large sample wastes scarce resources and time. Researchers rely on power analysis formulas, anticipated effect sizes, and acceptable margin of error to calculate the optimal sample size.

Addressing Bias and Generalizability

Sampling bias occurs when the chosen participants do not accurately represent the wider accessible population, often due to poor recruitment methods like reliance solely on volunteers. When bias is minimized, generalizability (external validity) increases, allowing the researcher to safely apply study findings from a sample of patients to broader clinical populations across different healthcare settings.
