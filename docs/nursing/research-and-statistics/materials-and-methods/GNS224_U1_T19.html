Ethical Considerations in Research

Introduction and Historical Milestones in Research Ethics

Research ethics exists to protect human participants from harm, exploitation, and abuse. Throughout history, shocking violations in human experimentation forced the global scientific community to establish strict moral boundaries. Nurses participating in or conducting clinical studies must understand these foundational events.

  • Nuremberg Code: Created in 1947 after Nazi war crimes trials, establishing voluntary consent as an absolute requirement for human research.
  • Declaration of Helsinki: Developed in 1964 by the World Medical Association, providing ethical guidance for physicians and researchers involving biomedical research.
  • Belmont Report: Published in 1979 in the United States, outlining the core principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice.
  • CIOMS Guidelines: International ethical guidelines specifically tailored for biomedical research involving human subjects in developing countries.

Core Principles of Biomedical and Nursing Research

Ethical research relies on three pillars established by the Belmont Report, guiding every interaction between the nurse researcher and the participant.

  1. Respect for persons: Recognizing individuals as autonomous agents capable of making their own decisions, while protecting those with diminished autonomy.
  2. Beneficence: Obligating the researcher to secure the well-being of participants by maximizing possible benefits and minimizing potential harms.
  3. Justice: Ensuring a fair and equitable distribution of the burdens and benefits of research, preventing the exploitation of vulnerable groups.

The Informed Consent Process

Informed consent is an ongoing communication process, not merely a signature on a paper form. A valid consent process ensures the participant fully understands the study before agreeing to join.

  • Voluntariness: Agreement must be free from coercion, undue influence, or excessive financial inducement.
  • Comprehension: Information must be communicated in simple language, avoiding heavy medical jargon, and matching the literacy level of the community.
  • Disclosure: Researchers must clearly explain the purpose, procedures, potential risks, expected benefits, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

A common misconception is that informed consent ends once the form is signed. In clinical reality, nurses must continually gauge participant understanding and willingness throughout the study duration.

Ensuring Confidentiality and Privacy

Privacy refers to an individual's right to control when and how personal information is shared with others. Confidentiality involves the researcher's obligation to protect disclosed information from unauthorized access.

In clinical nursing research, patient records, interview transcripts, and biological samples must be strictly coded. Researchers replace real names with identification numbers stored separately from master lists. Data files require secure digital passwords, and physical documents must remain in locked cabinets. Breaching confidentiality destroys trust and can lead to social, economic, or legal harm for the participant.

Protecting Vulnerable Populations

Vulnerable populations are groups of individuals who may have diminished autonomy or a reduced ability to protect their own interests, putting them at higher risk of coercion or exploitation.

  • Children: Require parental permission along with the child's assent when developmentally appropriate.
  • Prisoners: Confined status limits voluntary choice; studies must not offer undue rewards for participation.
  • Patients with severe mental illness: Cognitive impairments may compromise their capacity to understand complex research protocols.
  • Students and subordinate employees: May feel pressured to participate out of fear of negative academic or occupational consequences.

Institutional Review Board and Ethical Clearance

Before any research involving human subjects begins, the protocol must receive formal approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or an Ethics Research Committee (ERC).

The IRB reviews study designs to ensure participant risks are minimized, informed consent documents are clear, and subject selection is equitable. Researchers cannot bypass this step, as conducting unapproved human research constitutes a severe ethical and legal violation. The committee holds the authority to approve, require modifications for, or outright reject proposed studies.

Scientific Integrity and Research Misconduct

Scientific integrity demands honesty, transparency, and accuracy throughout every stage of the research process. When researchers compromise these values, it damages public trust in science and healthcare.

  • Fabrication: Making up data or study results and recording them as factual.
  • Falsification: Manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing/omitting data so the results are not accurately represented.
  • Plagiarism: The appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results, or words without giving appropriate credit.

Nurses must maintain high ethical standards, reporting any suspected scientific misconduct through appropriate institutional channels without fear of intimidation.
