Chi-Square Test

Introduction to the Chi-Square Test

The Chi-Square test is a non-parametric statistical method used to analyze categorical data. Unlike parametric tests like the t-test that deal with continuous numerical measurements, Chi-Square evaluates frequencies, counts, and proportions. Nursing researchers often encounter data that fall into discrete categories, such as gender, triage category, smoking status, or the presence or absence of a pressure ulcer.

By comparing what is observed in a clinical study against what would be expected by mere chance, this test helps determine if an apparent relationship between variables is statistically meaningful. It deals primarily with contingency tables, also known as cross-tabulations, which display the distribution of variables simultaneously.

Underlying Assumptions and Requirements

For a Chi-Square test result to be valid, specific methodological conditions must be met:

  • Random sampling: Data must be drawn randomly from the target population to minimize bias.
  • Independent observations: Each participant or subject can contribute data to only one cell in the contingency table. A patient measured twice violates this rule.
  • Adequate cell counts: The expected frequency in any single cell should generally not fall below 5. When expected counts are too small, the test loses reliability.

Goodness-of-Fit vs. Test of Independence

Chi-Square analysis splits into two distinct variants depending on the research question:

  • Goodness-of-Fit Test: Uses a single categorical variable from a sample to test whether it matches a known or hypothesized theoretical distribution in the wider population.
  • Test of Independence: Uses two categorical variables on the same population to determine if an association exists between them, organized in a contingency table.

Formulating Hypotheses for Chi-Square Analysis

Hypothesis testing starts by setting up competing statements about the population.

The null hypothesis (H0) posits that no association exists between the variables in a test of independence, or that the sample fits the expected distribution perfectly in a goodness-of-fit test. Conversely, the alternative hypothesis (H1) states that a significant association or difference does exist.

Rejecting the null hypothesis does not immediately prove causation; it merely indicates that the observed pattern diverges significantly from what random chance would produce.

Calculating Expected and Observed Frequencies

The core calculation of the Chi-Square statistic relies on comparing observed data against expected data. Observed frequencies (O) are the actual counts collected from the clinical study. Expected frequencies (E) represent the counts we would anticipate if the null hypothesis were entirely true.

The expected frequency for any specific cell in a contingency table is calculated by multiplying its row total by its column total, then dividing by the grand total. The Chi-Square formula sums the squared differences between observed and expected values, divided by those expected values across every cell.

Interpreting Chi-Square Test Results and P-Values

Once the test statistic is computed, it is converted into a p-value using the degrees of freedom, which depend on the number of rows and columns in the table.

If the resulting p-value falls below the chosen significance threshold (typically set at 0.05), the researcher rejects the null hypothesis. A low p-value signals that the discrepancies between observed and expected frequencies are too large to write off as random sampling fluctuation. Conversely, a high p-value means any pattern seen in the data could easily happen by chance.

Application and Reporting in Nursing Research

Clear reporting of statistical tests ensures reproducibility and clinical relevance. When writing up a Chi-Square result in a nursing journal or research project, students must include the test statistic value, the degrees of freedom, and the exact p-value.

For example, reporting format often looks like: χ²(2, N = 150) = 6.42, p = 0.04. In clinical practice, understanding these findings helps nurses interpret epidemiological reports, evaluate hospital infection control data, and appraise evidence-based guidelines before changing patient care protocols.
