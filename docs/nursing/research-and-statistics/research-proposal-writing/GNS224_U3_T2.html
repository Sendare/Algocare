Steps in Research Proposal Writing

Introduction and Choosing a Research Topic

A research proposal is a formal, structured document that outlines your intended study before actual data collection begins. It serves as a roadmap for the researcher and convinces academic committees or ethical boards that the proposed study is valuable, feasible, and methodologically sound.

Selecting a viable topic is the foundational step. Good nursing research topics often stem from clinical observations in Nigerian healthcare settings—such as managing post-operative wound infections in under-resourced wards or assessing maternal attitudes toward exclusive breastfeeding. Once a broad interest is identified, it must be narrowed down using the FINER criteria: Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant.

Background and Statement of the Problem

The background section sets the stage by providing the broader context of the issue, moving from global and national statistics down to the specific local unit. It answers why the reader should care about the topic.

Following the background, the statement of the problem clearly articulates the exact gap in knowledge or clinical practice that the study intends to address. A strong problem statement avoids vague complaints and instead highlights the negative consequences if the current gap remains unaddressed—such as rising neonatal mortality rates or prolonged hospital stays due to poor catheter care.

Research Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

Once the problem is clear, you must translate it into precise operational goals.

  • Research Questions: Interrogative sentences that ask what relation exists between two or more variables.
  • Objectives: Declarative statements that begin with action verbs (such as *to determine*, *to assess*, *to explore*) indicating what the study will achieve.
  • Hypotheses: Tentative predictive statements about the relationship between variables, common in quantitative studies.

Literature Review and Conceptual Framework

A literature review involves critically analyzing existing scholarly works related to your topic. It proves that you are familiar with previous findings, prevents accidental duplication of past work, and helps identify appropriate methodologies.

In nursing proposals, this section often culminates in a conceptual framework—a visual or written diagram that maps out how the independent variables interact with the dependent variables. For instance, in studying nurse burnout, staffing levels and shift lengths might be independent variables, while medication errors represent the dependent variable.

Research Methodology and Study Design

Methodology is the strategy or overarching plan that dictates how the research will be executed.

  • Quantitative Design: Focuses on numerical data, statistical analysis, and objective measurement (e.g., descriptive surveys, randomized controlled trials).
  • Qualitative Design: Focuses on words, experiences, and meanings (e.g., phenomenological interviews, focus groups).
  • Mixed Methods: Combines both approaches for a comprehensive understanding of complex health issues.

Population, Sampling, and Data Collection

You cannot study an entire population due to time and resource constraints, making sampling essential.

  • Target Population: The entire group of individuals that meet the criteria for the study.
  • Sample: A subset of the target population chosen to participate.
  • Sampling Technique: Methods like simple random sampling or purposive sampling used to select participants.

Data collection instruments—such as structured questionnaires, interview guides, or physiological monitors—must be tested for validity (measuring what they are supposed to measure) and reliability (producing consistent results).

Data Analysis Plan and Ethical Considerations

A clear data analysis plan explains how raw data will be processed. Quantitative data often requires descriptive statistics (percentages, means) and inferential statistics (Chi-square, t-tests), while qualitative data undergoes thematic content analysis.

Equally important are ethical considerations. Every health research proposal involving human subjects must ensure:

  • Informed consent (voluntary participation without coercion).
  • Confidentiality and anonymity of participant records.
  • Minimization of harm (beneficence and non-maleficence).
  • Approval from an institutional ethics review committee.

Timeline, Budget, and Referencing

The final sections of a proposal ensure practical feasibility.

  • Project Timeline (Gantt Chart): A schedule breaking down project phases—such as proposal defense, data collection, analysis, and report writing—into realistic timeframes.
  • Budget: A detailed itemized list of projected expenses, including stationery, transport, printing, and data analysis software.
  • Referencing: Acknowledging sources using standard academic styles like APA or Vancouver, preventing plagiarism and academic dishonesty.
