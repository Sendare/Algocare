Problem Identification

Introduction to Problem Identification in Nursing Research

Problem identification marks the foundational phase of any nursing research journey. Before a nurse can design a study, test an intervention, or collect data, a clear clinical gap or question must be established. In Nigerian clinical settings, nurses encounter numerous daily challenges, ranging from high patient-to-nurse ratios to delayed wound healing or poor compliance with antihypertensive drugs. However, not every frustration in the ward qualifies as a scientific research problem. A genuine research problem indicates a measurable gap in knowledge, a contradiction in practice outcomes, or a persistent clinical difficulty that demands systematic investigation to improve patient care.

Characteristics of a Researchable Nursing Problem

A well-constructed nursing problem possesses specific attributes that make it worth investigating.

  • Significance: The issue must directly impact patient outcomes, nursing practice, or healthcare administration.
  • Ethical acceptability: The study must not violate human rights or cause physical or psychological harm to vulnerable patients.
  • Feasibility: Adequate time, resources, and access to data must exist to complete the study.
  • Clarity: The relationship between variables must be stated without ambiguity.

Sources of Nursing Research Problems

Inspiration for nursing research does not happen in a vacuum. Clinical practice provides a rich environment for identifying unsolved dilemmas.

  1. Clinical observations: Noticeable delays in patient recovery or recurring complications during bedside care.
  2. Previous research literature: Recommendations found in the discussion sections of published journal articles.
  3. Nursing theories: Existing conceptual models that require empirical testing in local hospital environments.
  4. Social issues: Broader societal trends, such as rising rates of maternal mortality or community reluctance toward vaccination.

Translating Clinical Observations into Research Questions

Moving from a vague clinical worry to a structured research inquiry requires logical refinement. For instance, noticing that many mothers in a rural primary health center stop exclusive breastfeeding early is a clinical observation. To translate this into a study, the nurse must isolate specific variables. What factors influence early cessation? Is it maternal age, lack of partner support, or cultural beliefs? Transforming the observation involves asking precise questions about the population, the phenomenon of interest, and the expected outcome.

Formulating the Problem Statement and Purpose Statement

Once the topic is clear, the nurse must write the problem statement and the purpose statement. The problem statement articulates the context, the scope of the harm, and the gap in current knowledge. It convinces the reader that an issue exists and requires urgent attention. Following this, the purpose statement declares the overall objective of the proposed study. While the problem statement highlights the negative situation, the purpose statement outlines what the research aims to achieve to rectify or understand that situation.

Feasibility Considerations in Problem Selection

A research topic can be fascinating yet completely impractical. Feasibility dictates whether a proposed study can realistically be carried out. Researchers must evaluate time constraints, availability of participants, financial costs, equipment access, and ethical approval hurdles. For instance, conducting a multi-centre clinical trial across Nigeria may sound ambitious, but a student nurse with limited time and funding will struggle without proper institutional backing. Assessing feasibility early prevents wasted effort and ensures project completion.

Refining and Narrowing Down the Research Topic

Beginner researchers often make the mistake of choosing a topic that is too broad, such as "malaria in children" or "nursing stress." Broad topics make literature searches unmanageable and data collection unfocused. Narrowing down involves adding specific parameters such as population, setting, and variable attributes. Instead of "nursing stress," a refined topic becomes "sources of occupational stress among night-shift nurses in a tertiary hospital in Lagos." This precision makes the research manageable and directly actionable.
