Research Hypotheses

Introduction to Research Hypotheses

A research hypothesis is a formal, tentative statement predicting the relationship between two or more variables. It translates a vague research question into a testable proposition that guides the entire scientific inquiry. In evidence-based nursing, formulating a clear hypothesis ensures that the researcher focuses on specific outcomes rather than collecting data aimlessly. The hypothesis emerges naturally from the literature review and theoretical framework, bridging the gap between what is already known and what needs to be discovered.

Characteristics of a Good Hypothesis

  • Testability: The variables must be measurable and capable of being observed in the real world.
  • Clarity: The language must be precise, avoiding ambiguous terms or vague assertions.
  • Plausibility: The prediction should logically align with existing nursing theory and scientific knowledge.
  • Specificity: It must clearly state the expected relationship between the independent and dependent variables.

Null vs. Alternative Hypotheses

Quantitative studies typically test two competing statements.

  • Null hypothesis (H0): States there is no significant relationship or difference between variables. Statistical testing aims to either reject or fail to reject this statement.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha): States there is a significant relationship or difference, reflecting the researcher's actual prediction.

Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not prove it is true; it simply means there is insufficient evidence in the sample to support the alternative.

Directional and Non-Directional Hypotheses

Alternative hypotheses take one of two structural forms based on existing evidence.

  • Directional hypothesis: Specifies the exact nature of the relationship, such as predicting an increase, decrease, higher, or lower outcome. For example, predicting that 'patients receiving preoperative education will report lower anxiety scores.'
  • Non-directional hypothesis: Acknowledges a relationship exists but does not specify its exact direction. For example, predicting that 'there is a difference in anxiety scores between patients who receive preoperative education and those who do not.'

Researchers choose directional hypotheses when previous studies strongly support a specific outcome.

Variables in Hypotheses

A well-constructed hypothesis clearly identifies the core elements being investigated. The independent variable is the presumed cause or intervention manipulated by the researcher, while the dependent variable is the outcome or effect being measured. For instance, in a study investigating whether music therapy reduces postoperative pain, music therapy is the independent variable, and pain score is the dependent variable. Extraneous variables must also be identified and controlled to prevent them from confounding the results.

Formulating Hypotheses from Research Questions

Developing a hypothesis begins by asking a focused research question. While a research question asks whether a relationship exists, a hypothesis offers a proposed answer that can be tested. For example, the research question 'Does regular nurse-led counselling reduce postpartum depression?' transforms into the hypothesis 'First-time mothers who receive weekly nurse-led counselling will exhibit significantly lower postpartum depression scores than those receiving routine care.' This transition moves inquiry from general curiosity to rigorous scientific testing.

Testing Hypotheses in Nursing Research

Hypothesis testing is the final step in determining whether collected empirical data supports the predicted relationship. Researchers apply statistical tests, such as t-tests or ANOVA, to calculate a p-value. If the p-value falls below the predetermined significance level (usually set at 0.05), the null hypothesis is rejected, supporting the alternative hypothesis. Nurses must understand this process to critically appraise published research and decide whether new interventions are safe and effective for clinical practice.
