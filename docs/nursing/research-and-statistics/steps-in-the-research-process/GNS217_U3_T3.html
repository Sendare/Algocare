Sources of Research Problems

Introduction to Research Problems in Nursing

A research problem is an area of concern, a gap in existing knowledge, or a condition that requires a systematic investigation to find a solution or understand better. In nursing, identifying a clear research problem is the foundational step of the entire research process. Without a well-defined problem, clinical studies lack direction and fail to generate evidence that can improve patient outcomes or nursing administration. Nurses encounter these gaps daily during routine tasks, ward rounds, and patient handovers, making practice environments rich sources for meaningful inquiry.

Clinical Practice and Patient Care Observations

Bedside nursing offers a direct view of unresolved clinical challenges. When standard nursing interventions fail to yield expected results, or when unexpected complications arise frequently on a ward, a potential research problem emerges.

  • Unexplained complications: High rates of pressure ulcers despite regular repositioning.
  • Patient complaints: Persistent breakthrough pain among postoperative patients managed with standard protocols.
  • Workflow inefficiencies: Delays in medication administration caused by congested shift handovers.

Existing Nursing Literature and Previous Studies

Published literature is a structured repository of past inquiries and current knowledge. Reviewing peer-reviewed journals, systematic reviews, and professional textbooks allows a researcher to see what has already been studied and what remains unknown. Most published research articles conclude with a recommendations section that explicitly points out gaps for future study. Examining these recommendations prevents duplication of effort and builds cumulative scientific knowledge in the nursing profession.

Professional Theories and Conceptual Frameworks

Nursing theories provide structured ways of looking at patient care phenomena, human responses, and health environments. When a theoretical model is applied to a real-world clinical setting, discrepancies often appear between the theoretical ideal and the practical reality. These gaps form fertile ground for research questions. For example, testing whether a specific self-care deficit theory improves blood pressure control among hypertensive outpatients turns abstract nursing philosophy into testable empirical investigation.

National and International Healthcare Priorities

Healthcare agendas set by government bodies, ministries of health, and global organizations like the World Health Organization highlight pressing population health challenges. Topics related to maternal mortality reduction, infectious disease outbreaks, immunization coverage, and non-communicable disease prevention are prioritized at national levels. Aligning nursing research with these macro-level priorities ensures that findings remain relevant to policy formulation, resource allocation, and public health advancement.

Personal Experiences and Professional Interactions

A nurse's personal career trajectory, interactions with multidisciplinary team members, and encounters during specialized training often spark intellectual curiosity. Interprofessional friction, communication breakdowns between doctors and nurses, or personal struggles with clinical burnout provide authentic starting points for exploratory studies. While personal experience is a valid catalyst, the researcher must eventually transition from subjective curiosity to objective, scientific inquiry.
