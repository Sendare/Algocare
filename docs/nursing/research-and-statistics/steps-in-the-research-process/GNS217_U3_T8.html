Research Questions

Introduction to Research Questions

A research question is the central query that a study seeks to answer. It transforms a broad clinical interest into a specific, manageable inquiry. Without a clear question, a nursing study lacks direction, making data collection unfocused and analysis impossible. Formulating this question marks the transition from identifying a gap in nursing knowledge to planning how to investigate it.

Characteristics of a Well-Formulated Research Question

A strong research question must be clear, specific, and answerable. Vague phrasing leads to ambiguous results.

  • Feasibility: Can the study be done with available time, money, and patients?
  • Clarity: Is the wording simple and unambiguous for anyone reading it?
  • Significance: Does the answer contribute new or useful knowledge to nursing practice?
  • Ethical soundness: Does the inquiry protect human participants from harm?

Types of Research Questions

Different nursing inquiries require different types of questions. Descriptive questions ask about the frequency or characteristics of a phenomenon, such as measuring the prevalence of pressure ulcers in a ward. Relational or associative questions explore connections between two or more variables, like examining the link between nurse staffing levels and patient falls. Causal questions test whether an intervention causes a change in an outcome.

The FINER and PICOT Frameworks

Frameworks help nurse researchers build robust questions. The FINER criteria evaluate whether a question is Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant. For clinical intervention studies, the PICOT format breaks the question down into Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Time. Using PICOT ensures that no critical element of clinical evidence-based practice is left out.

Developing Research Questions from Clinical Practice

Bedside nursing is a rich source of research questions. Daily challenges—such as managing breakthrough pain in postoperative patients or reducing catheter-associated urinary tract infections—highlight gaps in current protocols. Transforming a clinical puzzle into a research question starts with asking 'What is the best approach for this problem?' and checking existing literature to see what remains unanswered.

Relationship Between Research Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

These three elements work in sequence. The research question asks the core query. Objectives translate this question into action-oriented statements, usually starting with verbs like 'to determine' or 'to compare'. Hypotheses offer a tentative prediction of the answer, common in quantitative studies. A mismatch among these three causes confusion during data analysis and reporting.

Common Pitfalls in Formulating Research Questions

Certain errors frequently weaken research proposals. Questions that can be answered with a simple 'yes' or 'no' lack depth and limit analytical possibilities. Overly broad questions make data collection impossible to complete within standard timeframes. Additionally, presupposing the outcome within the question introduces bias before the study even begins.
