Sample Design

Introduction to Sample Design in Nursing Research

Sample design is the strategic plan for selecting a portion of a larger group to participate in a research study. In nursing research, studying an entire population of patients or nurses is usually impractical due to limited time, financial constraints, and vast geographical spreads across Nigeria. A well-constructed sample design ensures that the small group chosen shares the exact same characteristics as the wider target group. This allows researchers to draw accurate conclusions without needing to collect data from everyone. The validity of any research finding depends heavily on how carefully this selection plan was created and executed.

Key Terminology: Population, Sample, and Sampling Frame

Understanding sample design requires clear definitions of three foundational terms:

  • Target Population: The entire aggregation of cases that meet the researcher's specific criteria, such as all diabetic patients attending secondary health facilities in Lagos State.
  • Sampling Frame: The actual operational list or device from which the sample is drawn, like the hospital register of registered diabetic clinic attendees.
  • Sample: The specific subset of individuals selected from the sampling frame who actually participate in the study.

Probability Sampling Methods

Probability sampling gives every single member of the population a known, non-zero chance of being selected. This approach minimizes selection bias and allows researchers to calculate sampling error. Common techniques include:

  1. Simple Random Sampling: Every individual has an equal and independent chance of selection, often using a table of random numbers or lottery draws.
  2. Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into homogenous subgroups, such as nursing ranks, and random samples are drawn from each subgroup.
  3. Systematic Sampling: Every 'k-th' individual is selected from a ordered list after a random starting point.
  4. Cluster Sampling: Naturally occurring groups, such as primary health care clinics across different local government areas, are randomly selected.

Non-Probability Sampling Methods in Clinical Studies

Non-probability sampling relies on the researcher's subjective judgment rather than random selection. Not every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, which makes it harder to generalize findings to the wider public. However, these methods are often practical in clinical nursing research due to hard-to-reach patient groups.

  • Convenience Sampling: Selecting readily available patients who happen to walk into the clinic during the study period.
  • Purposive Sampling: Hand-picking participants who possess specific expert knowledge or unique clinical experiences relevant to the research topic.
  • Snowball Sampling: Existing study participants recruit future subjects from among their acquaintances, useful for stigmatized or hidden populations.
  • Quota Sampling: Selecting a specific number of cases from various subgroups until a preset quota is filled.

Determining Sample Size and Power

A common mistake in nursing research is enrolling too few participants, which leads to underpowered studies that fail to detect real clinical differences. Sample size calculation depends on the expected effect size, the chosen significance level, and statistical power (usually set at 80% or higher). Larger samples reduce sampling error and increase the precision of the findings. Researchers must balance statistical requirements with practical realities such as patient drop-out rates and available funding.

Addressing Sampling Bias and Error

Sampling error occurs naturally because data is collected from a subset rather than the whole population, and this can be minimized with larger sample sizes. Sampling bias, however, is a systematic error introduced when the selection process favors certain outcomes over others. For instance, interviewing only morning shift nurses introduces bias if night shift nurses experience different stress levels. Researchers must use rigorous random selection and clear inclusion criteria to safeguard the credibility of their clinical studies.

Ethical Considerations in Sample Recruitment

Recruiting human subjects for research requires strict adherence to ethical principles, particularly autonomy, beneficence, and justice. Prospective participants must receive full disclosure about the study's purpose, risks, and benefits before giving voluntary informed consent. Nurses must guard against coercive recruitment practices, especially when patients depend on them for ongoing clinical care. Vulnerable groups, such as prisoners or unconscious patients, require special protective safeguards and surrogate consent protocols.
