Research Objectives

Introduction and Purpose of Research Objectives

Research objectives are clear, concise statements that outline what a study aims to achieve. They act as the compass for the entire research project, guiding the methodology, data collection, and analysis. Without well-defined objectives, a nursing study risks gathering irrelevant data, wasting resources, and failing to answer the core clinical question. They transform a broad area of clinical interest into specific, manageable investigation targets.

General Versus Specific Objectives

  • General Objective: States the overall, long-term goal of the study. It usually starts with broad action verbs like 'to assess', 'to determine', or 'to evaluate' and addresses the main research problem.
  • Specific Objectives: Break down the general objective into smaller, logical, and operational milestones. Each specific objective targets a particular aspect of the study, such as examining a specific variable or demographic subgroup.

Applying the SMART Criteria

Good research objectives must be SMART to ensure feasibility and clarity:

  • Specific: Clearly states what will be done and where.
  • Measurable: Includes variables that can be quantified or observed.
  • Achievable: Realistic given the available time, skills, and resources.
  • Relevant: Addresses a genuine clinical or educational problem in nursing.
  • Time-bound: Specifies a clear timeframe for completion.

Connecting Questions, Hypotheses, and Objectives

Research objectives, research questions, and hypotheses are closely linked components of study design. A research question asks what the study wants to find out, an objective states the action taken to find the answer, and a hypothesis predicts the expected relationship between variables. When well-aligned, they use the exact same key variables, ensuring consistency from the introduction to the data analysis chapter.

Choosing Appropriate Action Verbs

Every objective must start with a measurable action verb. Avoid ambiguous or subjective verbs like 'to understand', 'to appreciate', or 'to know', because mental states cannot be directly measured. Instead, use precise verbs that point to observable behaviors or data analysis.

Verb TypeAcceptable ExamplesAvoided Examples
QuantitativeTo measure, to compare, to identifyTo understand, to know
QualitativeTo explore, to describe, to understandTo calculate, to measure

Common Pitfalls in Writing Objectives

Beginner researchers often make avoidable mistakes when drafting objectives. A frequent error is writing too many specific objectives, which fragments the study and dilutes its focus. Another mistake is turning objectives into procedural tasks, such as 'to distribute questionnaires', rather than research goals. Finally, objectives should never be written as questions; they must always be declarative statements beginning with an active verb.
