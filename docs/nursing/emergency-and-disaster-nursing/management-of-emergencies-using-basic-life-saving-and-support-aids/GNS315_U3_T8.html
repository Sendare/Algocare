Medical Evacuation

Introduction to Medical Evacuation

Medical evacuation, commonly called MEDEVAC, refers to the timely movement and en-route care of wounded, injured, or ill patients from the point of origin or a remote healthcare facility to a higher level of medical care. In the Nigerian context, this process bridges the vast gap between primary rural health centres with limited diagnostic tools and tertiary teaching hospitals equipped for specialized interventions. The primary goal is to prevent clinical deterioration by transporting the patient safely while providing continuous medical support. MEDEVAC differs from general patient transport because specialized medical personnel and equipment accompany the patient throughout the journey.

Indications and Criteria for Evacuation

Deciding to move a patient requires careful clinical judgment. Not every sick patient needs a transfer; moving an unstable person unnecessarily can worsen their prognosis. Clinicians use specific criteria to determine when the risk of staying outweighs the risk of travel.

  • Clinical Need: The required specialty care, such as neurosurgery or cardiothoracic intervention, is unavailable at the current facility.
  • Facility Capacity: The local hospital lacks the diagnostic or intensive care unit (ICU) resources needed to manage the worsening condition.
  • Stability for Travel: The patient's physiological status is robust enough to withstand the stress of movement and environmental changes.
  • Time Sensitivity: Delaying the transfer would significantly increase morbidity or mortality risks.

Triage and Pre-Evacuation Stabilization

Before any vehicle moves, the patient must be assessed and stabilized. Putting an unstable patient into an ambulance often leads to a preventable cardiac arrest on the road. Nurses and doctors utilize the ABCDE approach—Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure—to prioritize interventions.

Airway patency must be secured, supplemental oxygen administered to maintain adequate saturation, and large-bore intravenous access established for fluid resuscitation or emergency medication delivery. Wounds are dressed, fractures splinted, and catheters inserted if indicated. Triage ensures that when resources are limited, patients with life-threatening conditions are prioritized for immediate transport.

Modes of Medical Evacuation

Choosing how to move a patient depends on distance, urgency, terrain, and weather conditions.

ModeAdvantagesLimitations
Road AmbulanceAccessible to most rural clinics, cost-effective for short distancesVulnerable to traffic congestion, poor road networks, and delays
Fixed-Wing AircraftFast for long-distance inter-state or international transfersRequires paved runways, dependent on airport weather and landing permits
HelicopterReaches remote terrain quickly, bypasses bad roadsHigh cost, limited payload for bulky equipment, weather-sensitive

In Nigeria, road transport remains the most common mode, though poor road infrastructure often prolongs transit times and increases patient discomfort.

In-Transit Patient Monitoring and Care

Once the journey begins, the nurse's role shifts to continuous surveillance. Motion, engine noise, and vibration make clinical assessment harder than in a quiet hospital ward. Standard practice requires continuous monitoring of vital signs, including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation using a pulse oximeter.

Intravenous lines and catheters must be checked frequently to prevent dislodgement from vehicle movement. Clinicians must anticipate complications such as motion sickness, pain escalation, or sudden drops in blood pressure, ensuring emergency drugs and oxygen tanks are fully stocked and within arm's reach before departure.

Communication and Coordination Protocols

A successful evacuation relies heavily on clear communication between the sending facility, the transport team, and the receiving hospital. Reliable radio or mobile communication ensures the receiving emergency department can prepare trauma bays, blood products, or surgical teams before the ambulance arrives.

Protocols mandate giving an estimated time of arrival (ETA) update and reporting any sudden changes in the patient's clinical status mid-journey. If weather or mechanical failure delays the trip, the receiving team must be notified immediately to adjust their preparations.

Handover and Documentation at the Receiving Facility

The evacuation process concludes with a structured verbal handover and complete documentation transfer. Upon arrival, the transport nurse gives a concise report using a format like SBAR—Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation.

All monitoring logs, administered medications, fluid balance charts, and referral letters are handed directly to the receiving nurse or physician. Clear documentation protects patient safety, ensures continuity of care, and provides a legal record of interventions performed during transit.
