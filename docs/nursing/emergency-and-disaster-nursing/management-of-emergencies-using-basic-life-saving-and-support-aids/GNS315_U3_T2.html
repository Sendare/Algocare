Emergency Assessment

Introduction to Emergency Assessment in Nursing

Emergency nursing requires rapid, systematic evaluation of patients whose lives or limb functions are immediately threatened. Unlike routine clinical assessments where time permits a leisurely gathering of baseline data, emergency assessment prioritizes life over detail. The nurse must quickly identify and treat immediate threats to survival before moving on to less urgent diagnostic procedures. This structured approach prevents cognitive overload and ensures that critical signs of deterioration are never missed in chaotic environments.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE approach provides a standardized framework for the initial evaluation of any critically ill or injured patient. Every step must be assessed sequentially, and life-threatening abnormalities found in one step must be managed before proceeding to the next.

  • A - Airway: Check for patency, clear obstructions, and apply cervical spine stabilization if trauma is suspected.
  • B - Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, chest rise, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation.
  • C - Circulation: Evaluate pulse rate and quality, capillary refill, skin temperature, and control major bleeding.
  • D - Disability: Perform a rapid neurological check using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale and check blood glucose.
  • E - Exposure: Expose the patient fully to identify hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia with warm blankets.

Resuscitation and Immediate Life-Saving Interventions

Interventions during the primary survey are not delayed for diagnostic clarity. If the nurse identifies an obstructed airway, manual maneuvers or advanced adjuncts must be deployed instantly. Uncontrolled external hemorrhage demands direct manual pressure, pressure dressings, or tourniquets before checking lab results. Cardiopulmonary arrest requires immediate chest compressions and defibrillation. Resuscitation is an ongoing loop of intervention and immediate re-evaluation.

Obtaining a Focused Emergency History (SAMPLE & OPQRST)

Once the patient is stable and immediate life threats are managed, the nurse gathers a focused history. Two common mnemonic tools guide this process: SAMPLE for general background and OPQRST for current pain complaints.

  • S: Signs and symptoms
  • A: Allergies
  • M: Medications currently taken
  • P: Past medical history
  • L: Last oral intake
  • E: Events leading up to the illness or injury

For pain assessment, OPQRST details Onset, Provocation/Palliation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, and Time.

The Secondary Survey: Comprehensive Head-to-Toe Assessment

The secondary survey is a head-to-toe physical examination performed only after the patient is hemodynamically stable and the primary survey is complete. This phase uncovers injuries or medical issues that were not immediately fatal but require treatment. Nurses systematically inspect the head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, extremities, and posterior. Diagnostic tests, wound care, splinting, and definitive pain management take place during this comprehensive evaluation.

Triage Principles in Emergency Care Settings

Triage is the sorting of patients based on the severity of their condition to ensure that those with the most critical needs receive care first. In mass casualty or busy emergency departments, nurses use standardized systems like the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) or Manchester Triage System. Triage is a dynamic, continuous process; a waiting patient must be reassessed regularly because stable individuals can rapidly deteriorate.

Continuous Monitoring and Reassessment

Assessment in emergency nursing does not end with the initial check or secondary survey. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, level of consciousness, and pain levels is mandatory. Whenever a patient's condition changes, or after any therapeutic intervention like medication administration or fluid bolus, the nurse must perform a targeted reassessment to evaluate the effectiveness of the care provided.
