Triage System

Introduction and Definition of Triage

Triage is the systematic process of sorting patients based on the severity of their condition to ensure that limited medical resources are directed to those who need care most urgently. The word originates from the French term *trier*, meaning to sort or select. In busy Nigerian emergency departments where patient volume frequently overwhelms available bed space and staffing, triage prevents chaos by establishing a rational, objective entry point for all arriving cases.

The primary goal is not necessarily to treat everyone immediately, but to identify life-threatening conditions instantly and prioritize access accordingly. When multiple casualties present simultaneously, the triage system dictates who goes straight to the resuscitation room and who can safely wait in the general queue.

Core Principles and Objectives of Triage

Effective triage relies on standardized criteria rather than subjective guessing or first-come, first-served habits. The process functions continuously because a patient waiting in the queue can deteriorate at any moment, requiring prompt re-assessment.

  • Safety: Protecting both staff and patients by isolating infectious cases and securing violent individuals.
  • Efficiency: Maximizing the flow of patients through the emergency department without compromising clinical safety.
  • Equity: Treating all patients fairly based strictly on medical need, ignoring social status or wealth.

Types of Triage Systems (Routine vs. Disaster)

Triage operates differently depending on the operational context.

FeatureRoutine Emergency TriageDisaster / Mass Casualty Triage
--------->
Patient VolumeManageable daily loadOverwhelming numbers
Resource AvailabilityStandard hospital suppliesSeverely constrained
Primary GoalBest care for every individualGreatest good for the greatest number

During everyday hospital operations, the focus rests on individual clinical outcomes. In a mass casualty event—such as a major building collapse or a fuel tanker explosion on a highway—the paradigm shifts. Rescuers must abandon resource-heavy interventions for dying victims to preserve supplies for those with higher survival odds.

Common Triage Scales and Categorization Protocols

Several validated tools guide clinical sorting globally. The Manchester Triage System and the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale use five distinct levels, ranging from immediate resuscitation down to non-urgent routine complaints.

  • Level 1 (Resuscitation): Immediate, life-saving intervention needed.
  • Level 2 (Emergent): High risk, condition could deteriorate rapidly.
  • Level 3 (Urgent): Stable, but requires prompt care.
  • Level 4 (Semi-urgent): Age or pain factors require attention without high immediate risk.
  • Level 5 (Non-urgent): Minor ailments that can safely wait or be managed elsewhere.

In disaster settings, the SIMPLE Triage and Rapid Treatment (START) protocol uses four color-coded categories: Red (Immediate), Yellow (Delayed), Green (Minor), and Black (Expectant/Deceased).

The Role and Responsibilities of the Triage Nurse

The triage nurse acts as the crucial gatekeeper of the healthcare facility. This role demands an experienced clinician with advanced assessment skills, emotional intelligence, and quick critical thinking.

Responsibilities extend beyond assigning a numerical score. The triage nurse initiates immediate life-saving first aid, manages angry or anxious relatives in the waiting room, and coordinates patient flow with attending physicians. Because first impressions matter, the triage nurse also sets the standard for professional communication and patient reassurance during high-stress moments.

Triage Assessment and Rapid Decision-Level Making

Triage assessment must be fast, structured, and focused. Nurses rely on the ABCDE framework—Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure—to scan for life threats within seconds of patient contact.

Subjective complaints like chest pain or severe headache must be paired immediately with objective measurements such as vital signs, oxygen saturation, and level of consciousness. A common error is delaying triage categorization while taking a detailed medical history; the priority is stabilization and risk stratification, not exhaustive diagnosis.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Triage

Triage decisions carry heavy ethical and legal weight. Nurses must balance the ethical principle of beneficence—doing good for the individual—with distributive justice, ensuring fair allocation of scarce resources to the community.

Legally, a patient who presents to an emergency department has a right to a medical screening examination. Turning away a critically ill patient without proper triage or stabilization constitutes professional negligence. Documenting the exact time of arrival, initial assessment findings, and the assigned triage category protects both the patient and the healthcare institution.
