Management of Victim Enroute to Hospital

Pre-Transport Preparation and Stabilization

Before moving any injured person into an ambulance, critical life threats must be managed. Stabilization is not about curing the patient on the scene; it is about stopping active bleeding, securing the airway, and ensuring vital signs are stable enough to withstand the motion of transit. Rushing a crash patient into a vehicle without basic stabilization often leads to cardiac arrest on the road. Nurses must verify that all splints, bandages, and tubes are firmly fixed because road vibrations and cornering will dislodge poorly secured medical devices.

Airway, Breathing, and Circulation Monitoring in Transit

  • Airway: Continuously check that endotracheal tubes or oral airways remain in the correct position despite vehicle movement and bumps.
  • Breathing: Observe chest rise, monitor oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, and listen for equal breath sounds regularly.
  • Circulation: Palpate peripheral pulses, track capillary refill time, and check intravenous infusion sites for infiltration caused by vehicle motion.

Patient Positioning and Spinal Immobilization

Proper body alignment prevents secondary neurological and physical injury during transit.

  • Trauma Victims: Must remain flat on a rigid spine board with head blocks and cervical collars secured.
  • Unconscious, Breathing Victims: Placed in the recovery position to prevent aspiration of vomit or saliva.
  • Respiratory Distress: Nursed in a semi-Fowler position, provided spinal injury has been safely ruled out or adapted boards permit elevation.

Management of Equipment and Infusions on the Move

Infusion pumps run on battery power during transit, so checking battery levels before departure is non-negotiable. Gravity drips may flow erratically if the vehicle accelerates or brakes sharply; pressure infusers or manual regulation may be required. Loose oxygen cylinders present a major projectile hazard inside a moving cabin and must be strapped securely to the stretcher or vehicle wall at all times.

Detecting Clinical Deterioration Enroute

A stable patient can crash within minutes of departure. Nurses must look beyond monitors and observe skin color, temperature, and level of consciousness. A widening pulse pressure, increasing restlessness, or sudden diaphoresis often signals internal hemorrhage or impending shock long before blood pressure drops sharply. Frequent re-evaluation of vital signs every five to ten minutes is standard practice for unstable transit cases.

Handover Protocols with Receiving Facilities

Effective communication ensures a seamless transition of care upon arrival at the hospital. The transporting nurse uses the SBAR format (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to brief the emergency department team. Handover includes stating the patient's baseline vitals, interventions performed enroute, and any sudden changes that occurred on the road. Never leave the patient until verbal and physical handover is fully completed with the receiving medical team.
