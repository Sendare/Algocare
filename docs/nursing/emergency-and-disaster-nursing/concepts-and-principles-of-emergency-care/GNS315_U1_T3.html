Elements of Emergency and Disaster Care

Introduction to Emergency and Disaster Care

Emergency and disaster care encompasses the coordinated medical, nursing, and logistical interventions delivered during sudden, overwhelming events that disrupt community functioning. While daily emergency departments handle acute individual cases, disaster care scales up to manage surges where patient numbers drastically outstrip immediate local resources. Nurses working in Nigerian healthcare settings frequently encounter resource-constrained crises, ranging from mass transit accidents to disease outbreaks and communal conflicts. Understanding the foundational scope of this care ensures rapid mobilization, risk reduction, and preservation of life under chaotic conditions.

Core Principles and Triage Systems

Triage is the sorting of patients based on the severity of their injuries and their immediate chance of survival. In mass casualty incidents, standard treatment priorities shift entirely. The goal stops being immediate treatment for every individual and becomes providing the greatest good for the greatest number of victims.

  • Immediate (Red): Severe, life-threatening injuries like tension pneumothorax or major hemorrhage, but with a high probability of survival if treated instantly.
  • Delayed (Yellow): Serious injuries requiring medical care, but stable enough to wait a short time without immediate risk of death.
  • Minimal (Green): The 'walking wounded' with minor cuts, sprains, or abrasions who need basic first aid.
  • Expectant (Black): Catastrophic injuries where death is imminent even with maximal intervention, prioritizing comfort care.

The Disaster Management Cycle

Disaster management is an ongoing, cyclical process rather than a one-time response. Nurses must understand all phases to effectively contribute to community resilience and post-crisis recovery.

  1. Mitigation: Long-term measures taken to prevent or reduce the impact of hazards, such as enforcing building codes or public health education.
  2. Preparedness: Planning, training, and stockpiling supplies before an event occurs to ensure readiness.
  3. Response: Immediate actions taken during the disaster to save lives, control damage, and meet basic human needs.
  4. Recovery: Restoring the affected community back to its baseline or a safer, improved state, including infrastructure repair and psychological rehabilitation.

Emergency Assessment and Rapid Primary Survey

When confronting casualties in an emergency, healthcare providers rely on systematic rapid assessment frameworks to identify and immediately correct life threats. The classic ABCDE approach guides this swift evaluation:

  • Airway: Check for patency and clear obstructions, keeping cervical spine precautions in mind if trauma is suspected.
  • Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, chest rise, and breath sounds to spot tension pneumothorax or flail chest.
  • Circulation: Evaluate pulse quality, capillary refill, and control major external bleeding.
  • Disability: Perform a quick neurological check using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or pupillary response.
  • Exposure: Fully expose the patient to check for hidden injuries while protecting them from hypothermia.

Multidisciplinary Response and Incident Command

Disaster response requires seamless collaboration among nurses, doctors, paramedics, fire services, police, and government agencies. To prevent chaos, structured frameworks like the Incident Command System (ICS) establish a clear chain of command. In this structure, every worker reports to a single designated supervisor, eliminating confusion during chaotic multi-agency operations. Clear communication channels, shared terminology, and pre-established communication protocols ensure that resources flow efficiently to areas of greatest need without duplication of effort.

Resource Allocation and Mass Casualty Management

Managing mass casualties tests the limits of any healthcare infrastructure. When patient influx overwhelms facility capacity, triage officers and nurse administrators must make difficult decisions regarding equipment, medications, blood products, and staff deployment. Supplies must be rationed based on clinical utility and likelihood of survival rather than first-come, first-served principles. Establishing alternate care sites, mobilizing reserve personnel, and managing supply chains effectively prevent total system collapse during prolonged crises.

Psychological First Aid and Trauma Support

Survivors, witnesses, and healthcare workers alike experience profound psychological distress during and after disasters. Psychological First Aid (PFA) is a humane, supportive response focused on reducing initial distress and fostering adaptive coping. Unlike professional psychotherapy, PFA can be administered by any trained nurse or responder. It involves practical care and assessment of needs, listening without forcing victims to talk, comforting people, connecting them with loved ones, and linking them to essential services while protecting them from further harm.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Crisis Care

Crisis care forces nurses into morally distressing situations where standard legal and ethical frameworks face extreme pressure. Principles of autonomy, beneficence, and justice often conflict when resources run scarce. While normal standards of care may be ethically modified during catastrophic surges, nurses remain bound by professional codes of conduct, documentation standards, and human rights. Transparency in triage decisions, protection of vulnerable populations, and institutional support for healthcare workers dealing with moral injury are essential pillars of ethical disaster nursing.
