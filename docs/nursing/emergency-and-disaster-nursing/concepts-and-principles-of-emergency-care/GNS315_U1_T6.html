Natural Disasters

Introduction to Natural Disasters in Nursing Care

A natural disaster is a sudden, catastrophic event caused by natural forces that overwhelms local resources and causes widespread damage, injuries, and loss of life. In Nigeria, nurses frequently encounter environmental emergencies such as annual riverine flooding, prolonged droughts, windstorms, and occasional gully erosion events. These occurrences strain healthcare delivery systems, disrupt clean water access, and displace vulnerable populations. Nurses must understand that disaster nursing goes beyond standard hospital care. It requires adaptability, rapid clinical decision-making under resource constraints, and an understanding of community vulnerability. Healthcare facilities may lose power, running water, and road access, forcing nurses to rely on emergency protocols and improvised care strategies.

Classification and Types of Natural Disasters

Natural disasters are broadly categorized based on their geological, meteorological, or hydrological origins. Recognizing these distinct categories helps nursing teams anticipate specific injury patterns and health risks.

  • Hydrological Disasters: Floods, tsunamis, and dam breaks. Flooding is common in coastal and riverine regions of Nigeria, leading to stagnant water and waterborne diseases.
  • Meteorological Disasters: Tropical cyclones, severe storms, and extreme heatwaves. These cause blunt trauma from flying debris and respiratory complications.
  • Geological Disasters: Earthquakes, landslides, and volcanic eruptions. These result in crush injuries, fractures, and severe lacerations requiring urgent surgical triage.
  • Climatological Disasters: Droughts and wildfires. These precipitate severe malnutrition, dehydration, and respiratory distress from smoke inhalation.

Epidemiology and Health Impacts

The health impacts of natural disasters evolve in predictable epidemiological phases. Immediately following the event, trauma, fractures, and acute injuries dominate emergency rooms. Within days to weeks, the risk shifts toward communicable diseases due to overcrowded relief camps, contaminated water sources, and poor sanitation. Cholera, typhoid, and gastroenteritis are major concerns in tropical settings. Chronic disease management also collapses because patients lose their medications. Diabetics, hypertensives, and renal patients often present in acute crisis. Furthermore, vector-borne diseases like malaria surge as stagnant floodwaters provide breeding sites for mosquitoes.

Mitigation and Preparedness

Disaster management begins long before an emergency occurs. Mitigation involves structural and non-structural measures taken to lessen the severity of potential impacts, such as building stronger embankments, enforcing strict zoning laws, and educating communities on evacuation routes. Preparedness focuses on readiness, including stockpiling emergency medical supplies, establishing clear communication channels, and conducting regular hospital evacuation drills. Nurses play a crucial role in drafting facility emergency response plans, ensuring triage checklists are accessible, and participating in community health education campaigns to build local resilience.

Emergency Response and Triage

When a disaster strikes, normal healthcare workflows are abandoned in favor of crisis standards of care. Triage becomes the most critical nursing responsibility. The Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment (START) system is widely used to sort patients within seconds based on respiration, perfusion, and mental status. Patients are categorized using color-coded tags:

  • Red (Immediate): Life-threatening injuries requiring instant intervention to save life, such as airway obstruction or severe hemorrhage.
  • Yellow (Delayed): Serious injuries requiring medical care, but stable enough to wait a short time without imminent risk of death.
  • Green (Minor): Walking wounded who need basic first aid and reassurance.
  • Black (Expectant/Deceased): Deceased or injuries so severe that survival is improbable given available resources.

Clinical Assessment and Immediate Interventions

Initial clinical assessments in disaster zones prioritize airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs), followed by rapid stabilization of hemorrhage and fractures. Nurses must improvise clean dressings, splints, and traction devices when standard hospital equipment runs out. Wound care is paramount because contaminated floodwaters introduce debris and bacteria, raising the risk of tetanus and gas gangrene. Nurses check immunization records and administer tetanus toxoid boosters promptly. Fluid resuscitation must be managed carefully, especially in austere conditions where intravenous supplies are scarce and accurate intake-output monitoring is challenging.

Post-Disaster Recovery and Psychological First Aid

Recovery begins as immediate threats subside and communities begin rebuilding infrastructure and restoring health services. Beyond physical healing, nurses must address the profound psychological toll of disasters. Survivors frequently experience acute stress reactions, grief, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Psychological First Aid (PFA) is an evidence-based approach used by nurses to provide humane, supportive, and practical assistance. PFA involves listening without forcing survivors to talk, comforting distressed individuals, assessing basic needs, and connecting them with social supports. Nurses must also monitor their own mental health and watch for signs of compassion fatigue and burnout.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Disaster Nursing

Disaster nursing introduces complex ethical dilemmas, particularly when resource scarcity forces rationing of care. Nurses may face situations where ventilators, medications, or critical care beds are insufficient for all who need them. Decisions must be guided by fairness, transparency, and the greatest good for the greatest number, rather than the traditional rule of treating the sickest patient first. Legally, many jurisdictions invoke Good Samaritan laws or emergency declarations that protect healthcare workers who act in good faith outside their normal scope of practice. However, nurses must remain within their acquired competencies and document care thoroughly, even under chaotic conditions.
