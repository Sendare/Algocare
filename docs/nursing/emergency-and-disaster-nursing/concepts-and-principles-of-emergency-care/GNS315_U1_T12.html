Local Burden of Disasters

Introduction to Disasters and Local Context

A disaster is a sudden, calamitous event that seriously disrupts the functioning of a community or society, causing human, material, and economic losses that exceed the affected community's ability to cope using its own resources. In the Nigerian context, disasters carry a heavy burden due to infrastructural gaps, population density, and varying levels of community preparedness. Understanding the local context means looking beyond the physical event to examine poverty, housing quality, and geographic hazards that turn a natural hazard into a full-scale humanitarian emergency.

Epidemiology and Burden of Disasters in Nigeria

Nigeria faces a diverse profile of recurring emergencies, ranging from hydrometeorological events to man-made crises. Statistical tracking reveals high morbidity and mortality, particularly among rural and displaced populations.

  • Flooding: Driven by torrential rains and overflowing rivers, displacing millions annually in coastal and riverine states.
  • Epidemics: Outbreaks of cholera, Lassa fever, and measles peaking during specific seasonal shifts or post-flood periods.
  • Conflict and Displacement: Insurgency in the northeast and farmer-herder clashes in the middle belt creating massive populations of internally displaced persons (IDPs).
  • Technological Disasters: Frequent pipeline explosions, building collapses in urban centers, and major road traffic accidents.

Socio-Economic and Health System Impacts

When disasters strike, the ripple effects tear through local economies and healthcare infrastructure simultaneously. Markets close, farmlands wash away, and livestock perish, plunging survivors into deep poverty and food insecurity. Simultaneously, health facilities face structural damage, loss of power, and overwhelming patient surges. Healthcare workers find themselves treating trauma victims while managing supply chain breakdowns that leave clinics without basic antibiotics, intravenous fluids, or tetanus toxoid.

Vulnerable Populations and Risk Factors

Disasters do not affect everyone equally. Social inequalities, age, and physical condition dictate who suffers the most.

  • Children: Highly susceptible to dehydration from diarrhea, measles outbreaks, and psychological trauma.
  • Pregnant Women: Face interrupted antenatal care, higher risks of complicated deliveries, and emergency obstetric delays.
  • Elderly Individuals: Often have chronic illnesses, reduced mobility, and struggle to evacuate quickly.
  • Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs): Live in overcrowded camps with poor sanitation, making them prime targets for infectious disease.

The Role of Nurses in Disaster Risk Reduction

Nurses are often the first healthcare professionals present in community settings, placing them at the forefront of Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR). Nurses conduct vulnerability assessments, educate communities on hazard mitigation, and participate in early warning dissemination. In primary healthcare clinics, nurse-led initiatives focus on maintaining vaccination schedules, teaching safe water storage, and training local volunteers in basic first aid and triage.

Local Disaster Management and Emergency Response

Emergency response in Nigeria relies on a multi-agency framework involving the National Emergency Management Agency (NEMA), State Emergency Management Agencies (SEMAs), and local volunteers. When a disaster occurs, healthcare teams implement triage systems such as START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) to categorize casualties based on urgency. Establishing makeshift clinics, ensuring safe potable water distribution, and maintaining cold chains for vaccines are immediate operational priorities.

Challenges in Post-Disaster Recovery and Nursing Care

Post-disaster recovery presents profound logistical and clinical hurdles. Nurses must manage chronic disease flare-ups among displaced populations while addressing widespread mental health issues like Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and acute grief. Resource scarcity, damaged transport routes, and insecurity in remote areas complicate follow-up care. Furthermore, nurse burnout is common due to overwork, lack of rest, and exposure to traumatic human suffering.
