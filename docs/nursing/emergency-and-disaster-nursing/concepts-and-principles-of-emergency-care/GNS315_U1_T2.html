Aims of Emergency and Disaster Care

Introduction to Emergency and Disaster Care

Emergency and disaster care encompasses specialized medical and nursing interventions delivered during acute medical crises or large-scale calamities. While everyday emergency care focuses on individual stabilization—such as managing a sudden myocardial infarction in a hospital casualty unit—disaster care deals with mass-casualty events that overwhelm local healthcare infrastructure. Disasters can be natural, like floods in coastal Nigerian states, or man-made, such as industrial accidents or structural collapses. The primary philosophy across both settings centers on achieving the greatest good for the greatest number of people when resources are severely strained.

Preservation of Life and Prevention of Further Injury

The absolute priority in any emergency is keeping the patient alive. Nurses utilize the systematic ABCDE approach—Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure—to rapidly identify and manage life-threatening conditions. Once immediate mortality risks are managed, healthcare providers must prevent secondary injuries. This involves careful immobilization of suspected spinal injuries, gentle wound dressing to prevent infection, and removing victims from hazardous environments like burning buildings or rising floodwaters.

Mitigation of Suffering and Pain Management

Alleviating physical pain and psychological distress is a fundamental ethical duty in emergency nursing. Severe pain triggers a physiological stress response characterized by tachycardia, hypertension, and heightened anxiety, which can worsen clinical shock. Nurses administer analgesics promptly based on protocols, apply splints to broken limbs, and use calm, reassuring communication to lower a patient's panic levels.

Restoration of Physiological Stability

Stabilizing vital organ function prevents deterioration into multi-organ failure. Nurses monitor and support hemodynamic parameters by securing intravenous access, infusing isotonic crystalloid fluids for volume depletion, and administering supplemental oxygen to maintain adequate cellular perfusion. Frequent reassessment of vital signs allows early detection of subtle clinical declines.

Triage and Resource Allocation in Disasters

Triage is the sorting of patients based on the severity of their injuries and their chance of survival with available resources. In mass-casualty events, standard care practices shift from individual benefit to community survival. Nurses use rapid sorting systems, such as the START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) protocol, which categorizes victims into immediate, delayed, minor, and expectant groups using color codes like red, yellow, green, and black.

Facilitating Safe Evacuation and Transfer

Moving patients out of danger zones or between healthcare facilities requires careful coordination. A successful transfer involves communicating accurate clinical handover reports, ensuring equipment like oxygen tanks remain functional during transit, and selecting appropriate transport vehicles. Nurses must stabilize the patient as much as possible before movement because motion and vibration can worsen unstable fractures, traumatic brain injuries, or shock states.

Psychological Support and Crisis Intervention

Disasters inflict deep emotional trauma alongside physical wounds. Survivors often experience acute stress reactions, panic, shock, or grief. Nurses provide psychological first aid by offering a calm presence, listening without judgment, reconnecting victims with family members, and shielding them from further traumatic sights. Early emotional support helps prevent long-term mental health conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder.
