Human-Made Disasters

Introduction and Classification of Human-Made Disasters

Human-made disasters are catastrophic events caused directly by human action, negligence, or technological failures, distinct from natural hazards like earthquakes or floods. These crises often strike suddenly, overwhelming local healthcare facilities and straining community resources.

  • Technological disasters: Industrial failures, structural collapses, and major transport crashes like train derailments or tanker explosions.
  • Complex humanitarian emergencies: Civil unrest, wars, and terrorism that lead to mass displacement and infrastructural breakdown.
  • Environmental degradation: Large-scale deforestation, oil spills, and industrial pollution that threaten public health over extended periods.
  • Biological and chemical incidents: Accidental or intentional release of toxic agents into the water supply, air, or food chain.

Risk Factors and Vulnerability in Communities

Communities face varying degrees of vulnerability when exposed to potential human-made hazards. Rapid urbanization without adequate zoning laws often places residential areas dangerously close to fuel depots or heavy manufacturing plants. Infrastructure decay, weak regulatory enforcement, and poor occupational safety standards further amplify these risks. In many resource-limited settings across Nigeria, poverty forces populations to settle near high-risk industrial zones or pipeline routes, significantly increasing casualty rates when containment fails.

Triage and Rapid Assessment Systems

When a mass casualty incident occurs, standard hospital triage must shift immediately to disaster triage models. Nurses utilize the START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) method to categorize patients within seconds based on respiration, perfusion, and mental status.

  • Red (Immediate): Life-threatening injuries requiring urgent intervention to survive, such as severe airway obstruction or massive hemorrhage.
  • Yellow (Delayed): Serious injuries that require medical care but can wait safely for a short period without immediate threat to life.
  • Green (Minor): Walking wounded with superficial injuries who can assist in their own care or wait until major casualties are stabilized.
  • Black (Expectant/Deceased): Patients with catastrophic injuries incompatible with life given available resources.

Hazardous Materials and Decontamination Protocols

Chemical, biological, or radiological exposures demand strict decontamination protocols to protect both victims and healthcare personnel. Contaminated patients entering an emergency department without decontamination will quickly incapacitate the medical staff and contaminate the facility. Decontamination involves removing contaminated clothing, which eliminates a vast majority of the hazardous agent, followed by thorough washing with copious amounts of water. Nurses must wear appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) tailored to the specific hazard level before engaging in direct patient contact within the hot zone.

Nursing Management and Emergency Response

Emergency nursing management during a human-made crisis relies on structured incident command frameworks and clear communication. Nurses must establish a clear chain of command, prioritize airway and circulation management across multiple casualties, and manage resource allocation efficiently. Maintaining accurate documentation despite chaotic conditions remains vital for tracking patient identities and treatment timelines. Collaboration with security personnel, public health authorities, and rescue teams ensures a coordinated institutional response to the disaster.

Psychological First Aid and Mental Health Support

Survivors of human-made disasters frequently experience acute psychological trauma, panic, grief, and survivor guilt. Psychological First Aid (PFA) is an evidence-based, humane modular approach designed to reduce initial distress and foster adaptive coping. Unlike formal psychotherapy, PFA involves practical care, listening without pressuring victims to talk, connecting people with basic needs, and shielding them from further harm. Nurses play a frontline role in identifying acute stress reactions, panic attacks, or severe depressive states, ensuring timely referral to psychiatric specialists when necessary.

Prevention, Mitigation, and Disaster Preparedness

Mitigating human-made disasters requires proactive community engagement, stringent regulatory policies, and regular simulation drills. Healthcare institutions must maintain updated disaster response plans, stockpile essential medical supplies, and conduct routine staff training. Inter-agency collaboration between fire services, civil defense, and public health nurses creates a robust safety net that minimizes loss of life when industrial or technological failures occur.
