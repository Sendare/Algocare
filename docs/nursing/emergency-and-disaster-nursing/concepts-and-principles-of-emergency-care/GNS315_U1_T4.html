Principles of Emergency and Disaster Care

Introduction to Emergency and Disaster Nursing

Emergency and disaster nursing requires rapid critical thinking and decisive action under intense pressure. Nurses in these settings operate in environments where resources are often limited, patient loads are high, and clinical presentations are acute and unpredictable. Unlike routine hospital care, emergency nursing prioritizes stabilization over complete diagnosis. Disaster nursing expands this scope to community-level events, focusing on population health, resource allocation, and maintaining safety amidst infrastructural collapse.

Core Principles and Ethics of Emergency Care

Ethical dilemmas frequently arise during crises when patient needs exceed available resources. The guiding principle shifts from providing the greatest benefit to an individual patient to achieving the greatest good for the greatest number of people.

  • Autonomy: Patient right to refuse treatment, though limited during public health emergencies that threaten community safety.
  • Beneficence: Acting in the best interest of the patient or the collective group.
  • Justice: Fair and equitable distribution of scarce medical supplies and personnel.
  • Non-maleficence: Doing no harm, even when treatment options carry severe risks.

The Disaster Management Cycle: Mitigation and Preparedness

Disaster management is an ongoing cycle rather than a single event response. Mitigation and preparedness form the proactive phases that occur before a disaster strikes.

Triage Principles in Mass Casualty Incidents

When patient numbers overwhelm local healthcare facilities, traditional triage systems are replaced by rapid sorting methods such as START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment). The goal is to identify those who need immediate life-saving interventions versus those whose injuries are minor or, conversely, unsalvageable.

  • Red (Immediate): Critical patients with airway, breathing, or severe circulatory compromise that can be corrected quickly.
  • Yellow (Delayed): Serious injuries requiring care, but stable enough to wait without immediate threat to life.
  • Green (Minor): Walking wounded who can wait hours for medical attention.
  • Black (Expectant/Deceased): Dead or injuries so severe that survival is unlikely even with maximal care.

Rapid Primary and Secondary Patient Assessment

Emergency assessment relies on structured mental frameworks to prevent missing life threats. The primary survey follows the ABCDE approach: Airway (with cervical spine protection), Breathing, Circulation, Disability (neurological status), and Exposure/Environment.

Once immediate threats are managed, the secondary survey begins. This involves a systematic head-to-toe physical examination, obtaining a focused history using the SAMPLE mnemonic (Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading to the incident), and baseline vital signs.

Emergency Resuscitation and Life Support Guidelines

Basic and advanced life support protocols provide standardized algorithms for managing cardiac arrest, severe shock, and respiratory failure. Immediate chest compressions and early defibrillation remain the cornerstone of survival in sudden cardiac arrest. Emergency nurses must coordinate team roles effectively, ensuring high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation with minimal interruptions while preparing advanced airway equipment and emergency medications.

Disaster Response and Incident Command Systems

Chaos is the primary enemy during a large-scale disaster. The Incident Command System (ICS) provides a standardized management structure that integrates facilities, equipment, personnel, and communications under a unified leader. Every responding healthcare worker must understand their reporting chain of command to prevent duplication of effort and ensure clear communication across police, fire, and medical teams.

Post-Disaster Recovery, Evaluation, and Psychosocial Support

Once the acute emergency phase concludes, the recovery phase begins, aiming to restore community infrastructure and psychological well-being. Nurses play a crucial role in recognizing and addressing trauma, grief, and burnout among both survivors and fellow healthcare workers. Debriefing sessions help evaluate response performance, identifying gaps in planning to improve future preparedness.
