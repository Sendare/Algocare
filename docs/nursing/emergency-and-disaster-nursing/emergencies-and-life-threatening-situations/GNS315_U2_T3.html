Causes of Emergencies

Introduction to Emergency Causes in Nursing Practice

An emergency is a sudden, urgent, and usually unforeseen event that threatens health, life, property, or environment, requiring immediate clinical intervention. In nursing practice, understanding the root cause of an emergency dictates the speed, direction, and type of care delivered. Nurses in Nigerian hospitals frequently encounter acute crises born from diverse origins, ranging from sudden physiological failures to severe physical trauma and public disasters. Recognizing the precipitating factor prevents wasted time during the golden hour of resuscitation. Etiology guides clinical decision-making, ensuring that interventions target the actual breakdown in body function rather than just managing surface symptoms.

Classification of Emergencies: Medical vs. Traumatic

Clinical emergencies are broadly separated into two categories based on their primary origin: medical and traumatic.

  • Medical Emergencies: Arise from internal physiological failure, disease progression, or metabolic collapse. Examples include acute myocardial infarction, severe asthma status asthmaticus, diabetic ketoacidosis, and cerebrovascular accidents.
  • Traumatic Emergencies: Result from external physical forces applied to the body. Examples include road traffic crashes, gunshot wounds, falls from height, and severe burns.

A common misconception is that medical and traumatic cases require completely unrelated skill sets; however, a severe traumatic crash can instantly trigger a medical emergency like cardiac arrest, demanding simultaneous dual-focus resuscitation.

Environmental and Natural Disaster Causes

Environmental emergencies stem from human exposure to extreme elements or natural disasters. In the Nigerian context, seasonal shifts bring specific acute crises.

  • Extreme Heat: Causes severe dehydration, heat exhaustion, and heatstroke, especially during the dry season or in poorly ventilated crowded spaces.
  • Flooding: Occurs during heavy torrential rains, leading to widespread displacement, waterborne disease outbreaks like cholera, and severe soft tissue injuries from debris.
  • Harmattan Dust: Worsens chronic respiratory conditions, triggering acute severe asthma attacks and acute bronchitis in vulnerable populations.

Nurses must maintain high vigilance for environmental patterns because public health emergencies often arrive in clusters during specific weather seasons.

Man-Made and Accidental Causes of Emergencies

Human activities, industrial expansion, and societal challenges generate a significant volume of acute hospital admissions. Road traffic crashes remain a leading cause of mass casualty events on Nigerian highways, driven by vehicle failures, poor road maintenance, and speeding. Industrial accidents introduce chemical burns, crush injuries, and toxic inhalations. Domestic mishaps, such as kerosene explosions from adulterated fuel, cause extensive deep-partial and full-thickness burns, particularly affecting women and children in residential areas. These man-made crises often overwhelm local emergency response systems, requiring nurses to execute triage protocols rapidly.

Underlying Pathophysiological Triggers

Every clinical emergency manifests because normal homeostatic mechanisms have failed. Understanding these underlying triggers prevents nurses from merely treating symptoms.

  • Hypoxia: Inadequate cellular oxygenation leads to anaerobic metabolism, lactic acidosis, and eventual multi-organ failure if airways remain compromised.
  • Hypovolemia: Severe fluid or blood loss drops mean arterial pressure, reducing perfusion to vital organs like the brain and kidneys.
  • Electrical Malfunction: Cardiac dysrhythmias disrupt the heart's pumping efficiency, causing sudden circulatory arrest.
  • Severe Sepsis: Uncontrolled systemic infection triggers widespread vasodilation, capillary leak, and septic shock.

Recognizing these core physiological breakdowns allows the nurse to prioritize life-saving interventions such as oxygen administration, fluid resuscitation, and crash cart deployment.

Risk Factors and Vulnerable Populations in the Nigerian Context

Certain groups carry a disproportionately high risk of experiencing medical and traumatic emergencies due to socioeconomic, environmental, and biological factors.

  • Children Under Five: Highly vulnerable to severe dehydration from diarrheal diseases, acute respiratory infections, accidental poisonings, and malaria-induced severe anemia.
  • Pregnant Women: Face acute risks such as postpartum hemorrhage, eclamptic fits, and obstructed labor, which can quickly turn fatal without prompt obstetric care.
  • Low-Income Urban Dwellers: Reside in overcrowded settlements with poor sanitation, making them prime targets for infectious disease outbreaks and structural fire disasters.
  • The Elderly: Prone to falls resulting in hip fractures, acute stroke, and exacerbations of underlying heart and kidney diseases.

Nurses must account for these demographic vulnerabilities when conducting community outreach and emergency room triage.

Rapid Assessment and Identification of Etiology

When an emergency patient arrives, the nurse must instantly transition from a detailed medical history to a swift, systematic assessment. The standard Primary Survey using the ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) helps identify life threats regardless of the underlying cause. Once immediate threats are managed, the secondary survey explores the etiology through focused history-taking from the patient, relatives, or witnesses. Nurses look for clues such as medication bottles, trauma marks, abnormal breath odors, or environmental exposure signs. Quick identification prevents misdirected treatment and ensures the clinical team targets the actual pathology.

Initial Nursing Management Based on Underlying Causes

Management strategies must directly mirror the identified cause of the emergency. If the trigger is hypovolemia from severe bleeding, the priority is establishing large-bore intravenous access and initiating fluid resuscitation or blood transfusion. If the cause is an airway obstruction from anaphylaxis, immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine and airway support takes precedence. For traumatic injuries involving suspected spinal damage, strict immobilization prevents secondary spinal cord injury. Tailoring nursing actions to the etiology optimizes patient stabilization and sets a strong foundation for ongoing medical care.
