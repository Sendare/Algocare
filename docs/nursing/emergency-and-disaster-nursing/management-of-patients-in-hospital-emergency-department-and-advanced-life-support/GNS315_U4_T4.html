Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS)

Introduction and Chain of Survival in ACLS

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support builds upon basic resuscitation principles to treat life-threatening cardiac emergencies. The Chain of Survival represents a sequence of coordinated events that dramatically improve survival outcomes when a patient suffers a cardiac arrest. In the Nigerian clinical setting, prompt recognition and immediate activation of emergency response systems remain critical challenges due to resource limitations.

  • Early Recognition: Immediate identification of cardiac arrest prevents delays in care.
  • Early CPR: High-quality chest compressions buy time by maintaining minimal blood flow to the brain and heart.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Shocks delivered within minutes terminate lethal arrhythmias like ventricular fibrillation.
  • Advanced Life Support: Specialized clinical interventions stabilize the patient further.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Comprehensive hospital care optimizes neurological recovery.

Basic Life Support Foundation and High-Quality CPR

High-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation forms the core of every successful resuscitation attempt. Without adequate chest compressions, any advanced drug or airway intervention is far less effective. Rescuers must push hard and fast, allowing complete chest recoil after every compression. Minimizing interruptions in chest compressions directly correlates with higher rates of return of spontaneous circulation.

In crowded hospital wards or emergency departments, fatigue sets in quickly. Healthcare providers should rotate compressor roles every two minutes to maintain compression quality. Avoid leaning on the chest between compressions, as this prevents the heart chambers from filling adequately with blood.

Cardiac Arrest Algorithms: Shockable vs. Non-Shockable Rhythms

Rapid rhythm analysis dictates the entire flow of the ACLS cardiac arrest algorithm. Cardiac rhythms are broadly split into two distinct categories that guide treatment decisions.

Rhythm CategorySpecific Rhythms IncludedImmediate Action Required
ShockableVentricular Fibrillation (VF), Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT)Immediate defibrillation followed by CPR
Non-ShockableAsystole, Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA)Immediate CPR and epinephrine administration

A common error during rhythm checks is spending too much time analyzing the monitor. Rhythm checks must never exceed ten seconds.

Advanced Airway Management and Ventilation Strategies

Securing an advanced airway—such as an endotracheal tube or supraglottic device—is no longer the immediate priority in early resuscitation. Chest compressions and early defibrillation take precedence. Once an advanced airway is in place, providers discontinue synchronized pauses. Compressions continue uninterrupted at a continuous rate, while ventilations are delivered at a rate of one breath every six seconds.

After insertion, nurses and doctors must confirm proper tube placement using physical assessments like auscultation alongside reliable confirmation tools like waveform capnography. Esophageal intubation remains a fatal error if left unrecognized.

Pharmacological Agents and Emergency Vascular Access

Medications support electrical and mechanical therapies during cardiac arrest. Epinephrine remains the primary vasopressor, given every three to five minutes to constrict peripheral blood vessels and boost coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. For refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, antiarrhythmics like amiodarone or lidocaine are administered after shocks.

Establishing vascular access should not delay chest compressions. Peripheral intravenous lines in the antecubital fossa are preferred initially. If peripheral access fails during an emergency, intraosseous access provides a rapid, reliable alternative route for drug and fluid delivery.

Management of Symptomatic Bradycardia and Tachycardia

Unstable tachycardias and bradycardias require prompt intervention based on clinical stability. A patient is considered unstable when arrhythmias cause acute altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure.

  • Bradycardia (Heart Rate < 60/min): Assess for signs of instability. First-line medical therapy is atropine. If ineffective, transcutaneous pacing or an epinephrine infusion is initiated.
  • Tachycardia (Heart Rate > 150/min): Determine if the rhythm is narrow or wide, regular or irregular. Unstable tachycardias require immediate synchronized cardioversion.

Never delay synchronized cardioversion in an unstable tachycardiac patient to attempt pharmacological conversion.

Post-Cardiac Arrest Care and Targeted Temperature Management

Return of spontaneous circulation marks the beginning of post-resuscitation care. The goal shifts toward protecting vital organs, particularly the brain and myocardium, from reperfusion injury. Clinicians manage blood pressure rigorously, avoiding hypotension which worsens ischemic brain injury.

Targeted temperature management is implemented for adult patients who remain unresponsive after resuscitation. Maintaining a constant core temperature between thirty-two and thirty-six degrees Celsius for at least twenty-four hours improves neurological outcomes. Nursing care during this phase involves precise temperature monitoring, shivering management, and frequent neurological assessments.

Team Dynamics and Leadership in Resuscitation

Technical skills alone cannot ensure a successful resuscitation. Effective team dynamics, clear communication, and defined leadership are equally vital. During a cardiac arrest, every team member must understand their specific assigned role—compressor, airway manager, medication administrator, and recorder.

Closed-loop communication prevents dangerous errors. The team leader gives an order, the team member repeats the order back to confirm understanding, and reports once the task is completed. Mutual respect and constructive intervention allow team members to voice concerns if a medication dose or procedure seems incorrect, protecting the patient from medical errors.
