Conflict Management Strategies

Introduction to Conflict in Nursing and Healthcare

Conflict in healthcare is an inevitable reality driven by high-stress environments, tight schedules, and close collaboration among diverse professionals. In Nigerian clinical settings, disagreements frequently arise between nurses and physicians, among nursing staff, or between healthcare providers and patients' families. Unresolved friction damages team morale, impairs communication, and directly threatens patient safety and care quality. Recognizing early warning signs of tension allows nurses to intervene before minor disagreements escalate into hostile disputes.

Root Causes and Triggers of Interprofessional Conflict

Understanding why conflicts start is the first step toward effective resolution. Common triggers in hospital wards include:

  • Role ambiguity: Confusion over task ownership between nurses, doctors, and auxiliary staff.
  • Resource scarcity: Competition for limited supplies, working equipment, or bed space during emergencies.
  • Communication breakdowns: Poor handover notes, verbal orders misunderstood, or perceived disrespect.
  • Personality clashes: Differing values, communication styles, and cultural backgrounds among team members.

The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument

The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument categorizes human behavior in conflict situations along two basic dimensions: assertiveness (satisfying one's own concerns) and cooperativeness (satisfying the other person's concerns). Mapping responses onto these dimensions yields five distinct conflict-handling modes: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, and accommodating. Nurses utilize these modes flexibly depending on whether the issue involves critical patient safety, interpersonal relationships, or time-sensitive emergencies.

Collaborating and Compromising Approaches

Collaboration is a win-win strategy that dives deep into root causes to find a solution satisfying all parties fully. It requires time, trust, and open dialogue, making it ideal for complex clinical workflow redesigns. Compromising, by contrast, is a give-and-take approach where each party gives up something to reach a middle ground. While compromise is faster than collaboration, it often leaves underlying problems partially unaddressed since both sides settle for partial satisfaction.

Competing and Accommodating Strategies

Competing is a win-lose approach characterized by high assertiveness and low cooperativeness. A nurse manager uses a competing style during critical emergencies, such as a fire outbreak or sudden disease outbreak, where quick, authoritative decisions save lives. Conversely, accommodating involves high cooperativeness and low assertiveness, where one party neglects their own needs to satisfy the other. Accommodating is useful when preserving a harmonious relationship matters more than winning a minor argument.

Avoiding as a Temporary Conflict Tactic

Avoiding involves sidestepping an issue or postponing discussion until a better time. Many view avoidance negatively, but seasoned charge nurses recognize it as a valid tactical choice. When emotions run dangerously high, stepping back prevents verbal abuse and allows everyone to cool down. However, chronic avoidance allows minor grievances to fester into major operational dysfunction. Avoidance works best as a temporary cooling-off period, not a permanent resolution method.

De-escalation and Communication Techniques in Practice

Effective verbal de-escalation prevents professional disagreements from turning into disruptive confrontations. Nurses employ active listening, maintain calm vocal tones, and use non-threatening body language. Acknowledging the other person's perspective—even without agreeing with it—defuses defensiveness immediately. Using 'I' statements instead of 'You' accusations keeps discussions focused on objective behaviors rather than personal attacks.

Evaluating Outcomes and Sustaining a Collaborative Ward Culture

Resolving a specific conflict does not guarantee long-term harmony unless nurses actively evaluate outcomes and maintain a supportive ward culture. Nurse leaders must follow up with involved parties to ensure agreed-upon changes are working in practice. Fostering a just culture—where staff report errors and conflicts without fear of unfair punishment—encourages transparency and continuous learning. Celebrating collaborative successes reinforces positive behaviors across the entire healthcare team.
