Organising

Introduction to Organising in Nursing Management

Organising is the second phase of the management process, coming right after planning. In a nursing context, it involves arranging human and material resources to execute the set care plans effectively. Nurse managers take the goals established during planning and build a structural framework to achieve them. This step bridges the gap between what a hospital or ward intends to do and how the nursing staff actually carries it out. Without proper organisation, even the best nursing care plans fall apart due to confusion over duties and wasted resources.

Organizational Structures in Healthcare Settings

An organizational structure defines how job tasks are formally divided, grouped, and coordinated in a hospital. It outlines reporting relationships and clarifies communication channels across different clinical departments.

  • Functional Structure: Groups employees based on specialized skills, such as placing all theatre nurses in one department and all pediatric nurses in another.
  • Service-Line Structure: Organizes departments around specific patient populations or diseases, such as a dedicated maternal and child health unit encompassing all related care roles.
  • Matrix Structure: Combines functional and project-based lines, often seen in large teaching hospitals where nurses report to both a nursing director and a clinical project lead.

Division of Labour, Specialisation, and Coordination

Division of labour breaks down complex healthcare tasks into smaller, manageable job assignments. This practice leads to specialisation, allowing nurses to develop deep expertise in specific clinical areas like intensive care, wound management, or nephrology. However, high specialisation creates silos where different units stop communicating effectively. Coordination is the administrative glue that binds these specialised roles back together through routine meetings, clear protocols, and collaborative multidisciplinary ward rounds, ensuring patient care remains holistic.

Authority, Responsibility, and Delegation in Nursing

Authority is the formal power granted to a nursing position to make decisions and give orders. Responsibility is the obligation of the nurse to complete assigned tasks satisfactorily. When a nurse manager assigns duties to a subordinate, delegation occurs. A vital rule in nursing management states that while tasks can be delegated, ultimate accountability remains with the person who made the delegation. Effective delegation requires matching the task complexity with the competency level of the recipient, such as assigning basic vital signs to community health extension workers while reserving complex assessments for registered nurses.

Span of Control and Chain of Command

Span of control refers to the number of nursing subordinates reporting directly to a single manager. A narrow span involves fewer subordinates, allowing close supervision, whereas a wide span covers many workers, promoting staff autonomy. The chain of command is the unbroken line of authority running from the highest nursing executive down to the newest staff nurse on the ward. Following this chain ensures proper communication flow and prevents administrative chaos, protecting both staff and patients during disputes or policy changes.

Centralisation versus Decentralisation in Nursing Services

Centralisation concentrates decision-making authority at the top executive level of the hospital hierarchy, such as the Director of Nursing Services making all staffing and supply choices. This approach ensures strict standardisation and tight control. Decentralisation pushes decision-making down to frontline nurse managers and ward teams who understand daily clinical realities. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, moderate decentralisation often improves response times because ward heads can resolve supply shortages or patient placement issues immediately without waiting for clearance from distant headquarters.

Designing Effective Rosters and Workflow Systems

Staff rostering is a critical organising function that ensures adequate nursing coverage across morning, afternoon, and night shifts. A poorly designed roster leads to severe staff fatigue, high absenteeism, and compromised patient safety. Nurse managers must balance staff preferences, skill mix, and patient acuity when building schedules. Effective workflow systems also integrate handovers, meal breaks, and equipment availability so that clinical routines run smoothly without unnecessary bottlenecks.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Organising in Nigerian Healthcare

Nigerian healthcare institutions often face unique organisational challenges, including severe staff shortages, erratic power supplies, obsolete equipment, and bureaucratic bottlenecks. These obstacles disrupt workflow systems and frustrate nursing managers trying to coordinate care. Overcoming these barriers requires innovative local solutions, such as flexible shift adjustments, task-shifting frameworks, transparent resource tracking, and robust advocacy for increased healthcare funding. Nurse leaders must adapt structural designs to fit local realities rather than blindly copying foreign models.
