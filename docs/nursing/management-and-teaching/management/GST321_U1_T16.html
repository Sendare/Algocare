Authority

Definition and Concept of Authority in Nursing Management

Authority is the formal, legitimate right of a manager to direct staff, make decisions, and allocate resources within an organization. In nursing management, this concept stems directly from one's official position within the hospital or clinic hierarchy rather than personal charm or clinical expertise. A ward manager possesses the authority to create duty rosters, approve leave, and assign patient loads because the institution grants them that mandate. Without clear institutional backing, a nurse leader cannot effectively coordinate patient care or enforce hospital policies. This formal backing ensures that orders given by superiors are recognized as binding by subordinates.

Sources and Types of Authority in Healthcare Organizations

Healthcare institutions operate using different types of authority to maintain order and clinical excellence.

  • Traditional Authority: Based on established customs, long-standing hospital traditions, and seniority.
  • Charismatic Authority: Derived from an individual's personal appeal, exceptional interpersonal skills, and inspiring leadership style.
  • Legal-Rational Authority: Rooted in formal rules, laws, job descriptions, and professional nursing regulations, which is the primary form used in modern hospitals.
  • Line Authority: The direct vertical relationship where a superior directs a subordinate, such as a Director of Nursing supervising a Chief Nursing Officer.
  • Staff Authority: An advisory role providing specialized support without direct command, such as a hospital infection control officer guiding ward nurses.

Authority Versus Power and Responsibility

Terms like authority, power, and responsibility are often used interchangeably, yet they represent distinct concepts in nursing leadership.

TermDefinitionNursing Context
AuthorityThe formal right to give orders and expect obedienceAssigned by the hospital administration to a charge nurse
PowerThe ability to influence others and control resourcesCan be held by any staff member, formal or informal
ResponsibilityThe obligation to perform assigned tasks and achieve resultsBorne by whoever accepts the duty, regardless of rank

A critical principle in management is that authority must equal responsibility. If a nurse is held responsible for ward outcomes, they must be given the corresponding authority to make necessary operational decisions.

Delegation of Authority in Nursing Practice

Delegation allows registered nurses to transfer the authority to perform a specific nursing task to competent junior staff or auxiliary workers while retaining overall accountability for the patient outcome. Effective delegation reduces workload, promotes professional growth among younger staff, and ensures efficient use of hospital resources. The registered nurse must assess the patient's stability, the complexity of the task, and the capability of the delegate before assigning any duty. Tasks requiring specialized nursing judgment, complex assessment, and initial patient evaluation can never be delegated.

Barriers to Effective Authority and Delegation

Many nurse managers struggle with exercising authority and delegating tasks effectively due to internal and external constraints. Some managers exhibit micromanagement tendencies because of perfectionism or a deep distrust of subordinate competence. On the other side, junior staff may demonstrate resistance to delegation due to fear of failure, lack of proper training, or unclear instructions from superiors. Organizational bottlenecks, such as rigid bureaucratic structures and poor communication channels, further cripple the smooth flow of authority across hospital departments.

Maintaining Professional Accountability and Control

Control mechanisms ensure that delegated authority is exercised properly and that nursing standards are maintained across the healthcare facility. Nurse leaders use audits, incident reports, peer reviews, and direct observation to monitor performance and correct deviations promptly. Professional accountability means that every nurse must answer for their actions and omissions in clinical practice, adhering strictly to the code of ethics set by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria. Balancing administrative control with professional autonomy fosters a disciplined yet supportive working environment.

Application of Authority in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Exercising authority within Nigerian healthcare facilities presents unique dynamics influenced by professional hierarchies, resource shortages, and cultural contexts. Head nurses and nursing officers often manage overcrowded wards with limited supplies, requiring decisive yet collaborative leadership. Interprofessional friction between doctors and nurses can sometimes challenge nursing authority, necessitating strong assertiveness grounded in the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria regulations. Understanding local administrative structures enables nurse leaders to navigate red tape, advocate for patient safety, and secure necessary support from hospital management boards.
