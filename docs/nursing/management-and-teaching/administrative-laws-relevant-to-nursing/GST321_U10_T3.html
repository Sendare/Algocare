Public Accountability, Checks and Balances

Introduction to Public Accountability in Nursing Administration

Public accountability in nursing administration means that nurse leaders must answer for their actions, resource usage, and clinical outcomes to patients, the government, and the general public. In Nigerian public hospitals, a nurse manager holds a position of public trust. Every decision made—from staffing rosters to the distribution of consumables—impacts public resources and community health. When leaders lack accountability, drugs disappear from wards, equipment breaks down due to neglect, and patient care suffers severely. True accountability goes beyond avoiding theft; it requires transparency, stewardship, and a commitment to delivering safe, effective nursing care with the resources provided by the state.

Legal Frameworks Governing Nigerian Healthcare Administration

Healthcare administration in Nigeria is guided by specific laws, acts, and institutional policies that set boundaries for professional practice and facility management. The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) Act empowers the council to set training standards, regulate practice, and discipline erring practitioners. Additionally, public hospitals operate under civil service rules, financial regulations, and health acts enacted by the National Assembly or state houses of assembly. Nurse leaders must understand these legal frameworks to ensure their administrative decisions comply with national laws, protecting both the institution and the patients they serve.

The Principle of Checks and Balances in Public Health Institutions

Checks and balances prevent any single individual or department in a public health institution from holding absolute, unchecked power. In a typical Nigerian teaching hospital or general hospital, authority is distributed among the management board, the director of nursing services, medical directors, and internal audit units. For instance, the person who orders medical supplies is usually not the same person who approves the payment or receives the goods into the store. This separation of duties reduces opportunities for fraud and ensures that operational decisions undergo review by multiple competent authorities.

Financial Integrity, Budgeting, and Resource Allocation

Financial integrity requires nurse administrators to manage funds and material assets honestly, transparently, and strictly according to approved budgets. Public health institutions receive allocations from government subventions, internally generated revenue (IGR), and donor agencies. Nurse leaders must participate in budgeting processes to ensure that clinical units—such as maternal and child health wards—receive adequate funding for consumables, equipment maintenance, and staffing needs. Common financial malpractices include budget padding, diversion of hospital revenue, and procurement inflation, all of which undermine healthcare delivery.

Mechanisms for Auditing, Monitoring, and Compliance

  • Internal Audits: Periodic checks conducted by hospital staff to review financial records, store ledgers, and cash flow before external bodies arrive.
  • External Audits: Independent evaluations carried out by the Office of the Auditor-General of the Federation or State to verify public accounts.
  • Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E): Routine tracking of clinical programs, drug distribution, and staff attendance to ensure services align with set targets.
  • Regulatory Compliance Inspections: Unannounced visits by professional boards and health ministries to ensure safety standards and personnel licensing are up to date.

Ethical Responsibilities and Professional Conduct of Nurse Leaders

Nurse leaders carry heavy moral burdens because their behavior sets the cultural tone for entire health facilities. Upholding the NMCN code of ethics means displaying fairness, truthfulness, and respect for patients and junior colleagues. When leaders engage in favoritism during duty rostering, demand bribes for ward bed space, or cover up clinical negligence, they destroy institutional integrity. Ethical leadership requires courage to report wrongdoing, protect whistleblowers, and advocate for vulnerable patients who cannot speak for themselves within the bureaucratic machinery of the hospital.

Managing Maladministration, Corruption, and Public Grievances

Maladministration and corruption manifest as staff absenteeism, diversion of relief drugs, extortion of patients, and delayed service delivery. Public health institutions must establish functional grievance redress mechanisms, such as suggestion boxes, complaint desks, and anti-corruption transparency units. When patients or staff report extortion or abuse of office, nurse administrators must handle investigations fairly without victimizing the complainant. Addressing grievances promptly restores public trust and demonstrates that the health system operates under the rule of law rather than impunity.
