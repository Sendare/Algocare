Negligence and Malpractice

Defining Negligence and Professional Malpractice

Negligence is a failure to use the care that a reasonably prudent nurse would under similar circumstances, resulting in unintended harm. It is fundamentally an unintentional act of carelessness. Professional malpractice, often called professional negligence, is a more specific term applied to the actions of licensed professionals, including nurses. While negligence can be committed by anyone, malpractice requires a breach of the professional standard of care established by the nursing profession. In Nigerian clinical practice, failing to monitor a patient in labor or administering the wrong medication due to rushing are typical examples of falling below this required standard.

The Four Elements of Malpractice

To successfully sue a nurse for malpractice, the plaintiff must prove four specific elements in a court of law. If any single element is missing, the case fails.

  1. Duty: The nurse owed a legal duty of care to the patient.
  2. Breach: The nurse failed to meet the standard of care.
  3. Causation: The nurse's breach directly caused the patient's injury.
  4. Damages: The patient suffered actual physical, financial, or emotional injury.

Common Clinical Scenarios Leading to Negligence

Certain situations in busy Nigerian healthcare facilities frequently set the stage for nursing errors and subsequent legal action. Recognizing these high-risk areas allows nursing staff to maintain heightened vigilance.

  • Medication Errors: Incorrect dosage, wrong route, or failing to check patient identity before administration.
  • Failure to Monitor: Neglecting to observe patients post-surgery or ignoring vital sign trends indicating deterioration.
  • Equipment Failures: Using faulty monitors or suction machines without checking them first.
  • Communication Breakdowns: Failing to report critical lab results or patient complaints to the attending physician.

Legal and Professional Consequences

When malpractice is proven, the repercussions extend across legal, professional, and financial dimensions. The affected patient or family may file a civil lawsuit seeking monetary compensation for medical bills, pain, and suffering. Simultaneously, the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) can institute disciplinary hearings leading to the suspension or permanent revocation of the nurse's practicing license. In extreme cases involving gross negligence or recklessness, criminal charges such as involuntary manslaughter may be brought against the healthcare worker in a court of law.

Defenses Against Negligence Claims

When facing a malpractice allegation, nurses and their legal counsel can present several defenses to refute liability or reduce damages. A common defense is comparative or contributory negligence, where the patient's own actions—such as removing an intravenous line against medical advice—contributed to their injury. Another defense is the statuten of limitations, which means the lawsuit was filed after the legally allowed time window expired. Additionally, proving that the care provided strictly followed established clinical guidelines helps demonstrate that no breach of duty occurred.

Risk Management and Prevention Strategies

Proactive risk management protects both patients from harm and nurses from litigation. Accurate, contemporaneous documentation remains the single best defense; if a nursing action is not charted, legally it was never done. Nurses must also maintain professional competence through continuous education, strictly adhere to hospital protocols, and foster open, honest communication with patients and families. Practicing within the defined scope of nursing practice ensures that complex medical tasks are left to authorized practitioners.
