Communication Skills

Introduction to Communication in Nursing

Communication is the core of safe and effective nursing practice. Every interaction between a nurse and a patient shapes the healing environment, builds trust, and influences health outcomes. In busy Nigerian healthcare settings, where staff shortages are common, clear communication prevents medication errors, reduces patient anxiety, and ensures proper informed consent. Nurses communicate not only with patients and their relatives but also coordinate care with doctors, pharmacists, and laboratory scientists.

The Communication Process and Key Elements

Communication is a dynamic, two-way exchange of information. Understanding its building blocks helps nurses troubleshoot breakdowns when messages get misunderstood.

  • Sender: The person who initiates the message, such as the nurse explaining a procedure.
  • Message: The actual information being spoken, written, or expressed non-verbally.
  • Channel: The medium used to send the message, including spoken words, written charts, or visual gestures.
  • Receiver: The person who interprets the message, usually the patient or a colleague.
  • Feedback: The response from the receiver that lets the sender know the message was understood.

Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication Techniques

Words carry clinical facts, but body language often reveals true feelings. Both forms must align for clear messaging.

Verbal communication relies on spoken or written words. Nurses must use simple, clear language, avoiding medical jargon that confuses patients. Tone of voice and pacing matter just as much as the words chosen.

Non-verbal communication includes facial expressions, posture, eye contact, and gestures. A nurse standing with crossed arms may appear unapproachable, whereas an open posture invites dialogue. Cultural norms dictate acceptable eye contact and touch across different ethnic groups in Nigeria, so nurses must remain culturally sensitive.

Therapeutic vs. Social Communication

Not all conversations in a hospital setting serve the same purpose. Nurses must distinguish between social chats and goal-directed therapeutic interactions.

FeatureTherapeutic CommunicationSocial Communication
Primary GoalFocuses on patient health and emotional needsMutual pleasure, friendship, and social bonding
BoundariesStrictly professional, patient-centeredReciprocal, sharing personal opinions equally
DurationLimited to the healthcare encounterOngoing and unstructured

Therapeutic communication uses techniques like active listening, open-ended questioning, and reflection to help patients cope with illness.

Barriers to Effective Communication

Many obstacles can disrupt the message between a nurse and a patient. Recognizing these barriers is the first step toward correcting them.

  • Language Differences: Speaking different native languages without an interpreter leads to grave errors in patient assessment.
  • Environmental Distractions: Noisy wards, poor lighting, and lack of privacy prevent open conversation.
  • Pain and Anxiety: High levels of physical pain or emotional distress reduce a patient's cognitive capacity to process information.
  • Sensory Impairments: Hearing or visual loss makes standard verbal communication difficult if not adapted.

Strategies for Overcoming Communication Barriers

Nurses must actively bridge gaps to ensure safe care delivery. When language differences arise, facility-approved translators or family interpreters should be utilized, though caution is needed when using relatives for sensitive health disclosures. Minimizing noise by closing curtains or moving to a quiet space helps patients with hearing challenges. Giving patients extra time to respond when they are in pain or anxious prevents frustration. Using the teach-back method—asking patients to repeat instructions in their own words—confirms true understanding before discharge.
