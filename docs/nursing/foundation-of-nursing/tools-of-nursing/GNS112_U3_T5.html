Taking and Handing Over

Introduction to Taking and Handing Over in Nursing

Taking and handing over refers to the formal transfer of professional responsibility and accountability for patient care from one nursing shift to the incoming shift. This clinical communication event ensures continuity of care, prevents gaps in treatment, and safeguards patient safety. During this process, outgoing nurses share critical clinical updates, current conditions, and pending tasks with the incoming team.

Purposes and Objectives of Shift Reports

  • Continuity: Prevents fragmentation of nursing care across shift changes.
  • Safety: Highlights immediate risks, deterioration signs, and pending critical interventions.
  • Prioritization: Helps the incoming nurse plan their workload and time management for the shift.
  • Communication: Bridges the information gap between multidisciplinary team members.

Types of Handover Communication Methods

Handovers occur through various formats depending on hospital policy and ward layout:

  • Verbal Face-to-Face: Traditional spoken exchange, prone to omissions if unstructured.
  • Written / Audio-Recorded: Tape recordings or written logs, allowing independent review but lacking immediate clarification.
  • Bedside Handover: Conducted directly at the patient's bedside, promoting direct observation and inclusion.

Essential Components of a Patient Handover

A standard clinical handover must contain specific core data elements to remain safe and effective. Omitting identification details or recent clinical changes puts the patient at risk.

ComponentDescription
Patient IdentificationFull name, age, hospital number, and bed number.
Diagnosis & HistoryPrimary medical condition and relevant surgical background.
Current StatusVital signs trends, pain scores, and recent changes.
Plan of CarePending medications, lab investigations, and scheduled procedures.

The SBAR Framework in Clinical Handover

The SBAR acronym stands for Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation. It provides a standardized mental model for clinical communication, minimizing misinterpretations during shift changes or emergency escalations. Nurses use it to structure reports logically, ensuring that critical facts are stated first without unnecessary narrative clutter.

Bedside Handover: Best Practices and Patient Involvement

Bedside handover empowers patients by allowing them to hear and contribute to their care discussions. Nurses must maintain professional boundaries and patient privacy, especially in open-bay wards. Staff should introduce the incoming nurse, review key equipment like IV pumps or drains together, and invite questions from the patient before leaving the bedside.

Legal, Ethical, and Confidentiality Considerations

Patient handovers involve highly sensitive health data. Breaching confidentiality during shift reports violates ethical codes and professional nursing regulations. Nurses must ensure handover discussions cannot be overheard by unauthorized persons, visitors, or non-clinical staff. Furthermore, accurate documentation of the handover process protects the nurse legally by proving accountability and continuity of care.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Handing Over

Common barriers to effective handovers include staff fatigue, background ward noise, interruptions, and lack of standardization. Nurses can overcome these challenges by starting handovers on time, utilizing quiet designated areas or standardized tools like SBAR, and actively managing distractions such as ringing phones or family inquiries.
