Problem Solving Techniques

Introduction to Problem Solving in Nursing

Problem solving is a daily reality in clinical nursing. Nurses encounter unexpected equipment failures, sudden changes in patient stability, and resource shortages in hospital wards across Nigeria. At its core, problem solving means identifying an undesirable situation and taking purposeful action to bridge the gap between the current state and a safe, desired outcome.

Clinical problems range from simple mechanical issues, like a blocked intravenous line, to complex interpersonal dilemmas, such as managing a conflict between nursing staff and a patient's relatives. Developing strong problem-solving skills helps nurses reduce errors, lower patient anxiety, and build professional confidence.

The Nursing Process as a Problem-Solving Framework

The nursing process is a systematic, five-step problem-solving model designed to guide clinical care. It provides a structured method for addressing patient needs.

  • Assessment: Systematic collection and validation of subjective and objective patient data.
  • Diagnosis: Clinical judgment regarding actual or potential health problems.
  • Planning: Setting measurable goals and selecting specific nursing interventions.
  • Implementation: Executing the planned nursing interventions at the bedside.
  • Evaluation: Determining whether the patient goals have been met and revising the plan if needed.

Critical Thinking and Analytical Skills

Critical thinking in nursing goes beyond memorizing medical facts. It involves disciplined, self-directed thinking that questions assumptions, evaluates evidence, and considers alternative viewpoints before making clinical decisions. An analytical nurse does not simply accept a high temperature reading at face value; they investigate concurrent symptoms, check environmental factors, and consider hydration status.

Developing analytical skills requires curiosity and intellectual humility. Nurses must recognize when their own knowledge is incomplete and seek guidance from senior colleagues or evidence-based guidelines.

Common Problem-Solving Models and Techniques

Structured models help nurses break down complicated clinical challenges into manageable parts. Popular techniques include:

  • Root Cause Analysis (RCA): Used after an adverse event to investigate the underlying systemic failures rather than just blaming an individual nurse.
  • Trial and Error: Testing a solution, observing the result, and trying another approach if it fails; this should be used cautiously with patient care.
  • Brainstorming: Gathering the healthcare team to generate multiple creative solutions for a persistent ward issue.
  • Decision Trees: Following a branching flowchart of yes-or-no questions to guide clinical actions under protocols.

Barriers to Effective Problem Solving in Healthcare

Several obstacles can hinder a nurse's ability to solve problems efficiently in a busy clinical environment. Fatigue and sleep deprivation impair cognitive function and slow down reaction times during emergencies. Hierarchical workplace cultures can also create fear, making junior nurses hesitant to speak up when they spot a safety hazard.

Other common barriers include tunnel vision—focusing on one symptom while ignoring the whole patient—and confirmation bias, which is the tendency to interpret new data as confirmation of an initial, possibly incorrect, assumption.

Evaluating Outcomes and Reflective Practice

Problem solving does not end when an action is taken. Nurses must evaluate whether their intervention achieved the intended goal. If a patient's pain score remains high after administering prescribed analgesia, the problem-solving cycle must restart with reassessment.

Reflection is the final pillar of professional growth. By looking back on clinical encounters—either alone or in team debriefs—nurses analyze what went well and what could be improved next time. This turns everyday clinical experiences into lifelong professional wisdom.
