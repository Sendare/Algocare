Infection Prevention and Control in Relation to Injection Safety

Introduction to Infection Prevention and Control in Nursing

Infection prevention and control forms the bedrock of safe nursing practice across all healthcare settings in Nigeria, from tertiary teaching hospitals to primary health clinics. Nosocomial infections, also known as healthcare-associated infections, threaten patient recovery, lengthen hospital stays, and increase financial burdens on families. Nurses act as the primary defense against these pathogens by breaking the chain of infection at various points. Every clinical interaction carries a potential risk of microbial transmission if basic safety rules are ignored. Recognizing vulnerable patient populations helps nurses apply protective measures effectively before pathogens take hold.

Core Principles of Injection Safety

Injection safety protects patients, healthcare workers, and the community from harm associated with parenteral medication administration. Unsafe injections transmit deadly bloodborne viruses like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV through contaminated equipment or poor technique. A safe injection must not harm the recipient, must not expose the provider to avoidable risks, and must not result in dangerous waste for the community.

  • One needle, one syringe, only one time: Never reuse needles or syringes for different patients or even for drawing up multiple doses from a single-dose vial.
  • Aseptic preparation: Medications must be prepared in a clean area where contamination by blood, body fluids, or unsterile equipment is impossible.
  • Sharps disposal: Used sharps must go directly into a puncture-resistant container immediately after use.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene

Standard precautions assume that every patient's blood, body fluids, secretions, and non-intact skin carry infectious agents. Hand hygiene stands out as the single most effective intervention to prevent cross-contamination in clinical wards. Nurses must wash hands with soap and water when visibly soiled or use alcohol-based hand rubs when hands are clean. Gloves must be worn when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin, but gloves never replace the need for hand hygiene.

Aseptic Technique During Injection Preparation

Maintaining sterility during medication preparation prevents the introduction of microorganisms directly into the vascular system or deep tissues. The nurse must clean the rubber septum of medication vials with an alcohol swab using friction and allow it to air dry before piercing. Sterile syringes and needles must remain protected in their packaging until the exact moment of use. Touching the needle shaft, the inner barrel of the syringe, or the sterile tip of the plunger instantly contaminates the equipment and requires immediate disposal.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps

Needles and syringes pose severe physical and biological hazards if managed improperly after use. The nurse who uses the sharp is strictly responsible for its immediate disposal. Safety boxes must be puncture-resistant, leak-proof, and placed within arm's reach of the injection site. Never recap needles using a two-handed technique, and avoid recapping altogether whenever safety-engineered devices are available. Containers must be sealed and replaced when they reach the three-quarters full mark to prevent overfilling and accidental punctures.

Prevention of Needle-Stick Injuries and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis

Despite careful practices, accidental needle-stick injuries still happen in busy clinical environments. When an exposure occurs, the nurse must immediately wash the affected area with soap and running water, avoiding harsh scrubbing or bleaching. The incident requires prompt reporting to the hospital infection control unit or occupational health officer for risk assessment. If the source patient is positive for HIV or hepatitis, post-exposure prophylaxis must be initiated rapidly—often within hours—to minimize the likelihood of seroconversion.

Role of the Nurse in Patient Education and Safety Advocacy

Nurses extend infection prevention beyond clinical walls by educating patients and advocating for safe practices in community healthcare. Patients often request injections believing they work faster than oral medications, creating a demand that drives unsafe practices in informal settings. Nurses must explain the risks of unsterile injections and promote oral alternatives when clinically appropriate. Empowering patients to ask if their syringe is new and sterile transforms them into active partners in their own safety.
