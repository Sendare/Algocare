WHO Recommendations on Injection Safety

Introduction and Global Burden of Unsafe Injections

Unsafe medical injections pose a massive public health threat across developing healthcare systems, including Nigeria. Billions of injections are administered annually worldwide, and a significant portion involves reused syringes or needles without proper sterilization. This practice transmits bloodborne pathogens, primarily hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Beyond viral infections, unhygienic practices lead to local bacterial abscesses and systemic sepsis. The World Health Organization defines a safe injection as one that does not harm the recipient, does not expose the provider to any avoidable risks, and does not result in waste that is dangerous for other people.

Core Principles of WHO Injection Safety Guidelines

The WHO framework rests on three fundamental pillars designed to eliminate transmission risks during therapeutic or immunization injections.

  • Elimination of unnecessary injections: Prescribing oral medications whenever equivalent clinical efficacy exists.
  • Procurement of adequate supplies: Ensuring continuous availability of single-use disposable syringes and safety boxes.
  • Community and waste management: Educating patients while enforcing safe sharps disposal protocols.

A common misconception in busy clinical wards is that intramuscular administration requires an older glass syringe boiled for reuse; modern standards mandate sterile, single-use devices for every patient contact.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene serves as the single most critical intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Before preparing any injection, nurses must perform hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based rub or soap and water if hands are visibly soiled. Aseptic technique must be maintained when touching medication vials, ampoules, and needle hubs. Touching the needle shaft or the rubber septum of a multidose vial with bare fingers contaminates the sterile field. Gloves should be worn if there is a risk of exposure to blood or body fluids, though gloves never replace the necessity of cleaning hands between patients.

Use of Engineered Devices and Rejection of Reuse

Reusing disposable syringes or needles—even on the same patient or utilizing the same needle for drawing up medication and injecting—carries severe infection risks. To combat intentional or accidental reuse, the WHO advocates for Auto-Disable (AD) syringes for immunization and fixed-dose therapeutic injections. An AD syringe automatically locks or breaks after a single plunger depression, rendering it physically impossible to use a second time. Nurses must inspect packaging integrity before use and immediately discard any device showing compromised seals.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps Waste

Proper disposal of sharps is the final line of defense against community and occupational needle-stick injuries.

  • Puncture-resistant containers: Sharps boxes must be leak-proof, puncture-resistant, and yellow-colored with biohazard labeling.
  • Filling limits: Containers should never be filled beyond the three-quarters (75%) full mark to prevent overspill.
  • No recapping: Used needles must never be recapped using two hands, as this is the leading mechanism of accidental injury.

Discarding sharps into ordinary refuse bins or open waste cartons exposes domestic workers, waste pickers, and children to life-threatening infections.

Prevention and Immediate Management of Needle-Stick Injuries

Despite strict protocols, accidental sharps injuries still occur in clinical environments. When a needle-stick injury happens, the nurse must act immediately to minimize pathogen transmission.

  1. Wash thoroughly: Clean the wound immediately with soap and running water; do not scrub or bleach the site.
  2. Encourage bleeding: Gently squeeze the puncture site to express blood, but do not suck on the wound.
  3. Report incident: Immediately notify the infection control unit or nursing supervisor.
  4. Evaluate exposure: Assess the source patient's infection status and initiate Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV when indicated within hours.
