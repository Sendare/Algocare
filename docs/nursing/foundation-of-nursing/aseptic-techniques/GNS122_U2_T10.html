Use of Gloves

Medical Asepsis and the Role of Gloves

Medical asepsis, often called clean technique, involves practices aimed at reducing the number of microorganisms and preventing their spread in healthcare settings. Gloves serve as a primary physical barrier during these tasks. They protect both healthcare workers and patients from the transmission of infectious agents. Hand hygiene remains the foundational step, and gloves never replace handwashing. Wearing gloves creates a false sense of security if hand hygiene is neglected before putting them on and immediately after taking them off.

Indications for Wearing Gloves

Gloves are worn whenever contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin is anticipated. Nurses must assess each clinical procedure to determine if barrier protection is necessary.

  • Blood and Body Fluids: Required during phlebotomy, wound care, and handling specimens.
  • Non-Intact Skin: Indicated when touching broken skin or rashes during physical assessments.
  • Mucous Membranes: Necessary for oral care, catheter insertion, or suctioning.
  • Contaminated Items: Required when handling soiled linens, instruments, or waste.

Types and Material Selection of Gloves

Selecting the correct glove type and material depends on the nature of the task and patient allergies. Healthcare facilities typically stock examination and surgical gloves made from different polymers.

Material choices include latex, which offers high elasticity and tactile sensitivity but carries allergy risks; nitrile, known for excellent puncture resistance and chemical durability; and vinyl, an economical option for short, low-risk tasks.

Procedure for Proper Glove Donning

Putting on gloves correctly ensures the barrier remains intact and uncontaminated before patient contact.

  1. Perform Hand Hygiene: Wash hands with soap and water or use an alcohol-based hand rub, allowing them to dry completely.
  2. Select Correct Size: Choose gloves that fit snugly without being overly tight, which causes hand fatigue and tearing.
  3. Inspect the Package: Check the wrapper for tears, punctures, or moisture that compromises integrity.
  4. Pull on the Gloves: Insert hands into the openings, pulling the cuffs up over the wrists to cover skin completely.

Safe Removal and Doffing Techniques

Removing gloves incorrectly is a frequent cause of self-contamination. Pathogens accumulated on the outer surface easily transfer to skin or clothing if doffing is rushed.

  • Grip the First Glove: Pinch the outside of the glove near the cuff, avoiding bare skin contact.
  • Peel and Roll: Pull the glove downward, turning it inside out as it comes off the hand.
  • Hold and Slide: Hold the removed glove in the gloved hand, slide fingers of the bare hand under the remaining cuff, and peel it off inside out over the first glove.
  • Dispose and Clean: Discard immediately into an appropriate waste container and perform hand hygiene.

Common Errors and Preventing Cross-Contamination

Improper glove use defeats their protective purpose and spreads pathogens across the hospital environment. Common mistakes include wearing the same pair for multiple patients, touching environmental surfaces with contaminated gloves, and failing to wash hands after removal. Gloves do not provide absolute protection against needle-stick injuries. Furthermore, washing or using alcohol rubs on disposable gloves breaks down the material and is strictly discouraged.
