Theatre Sterile Supply Unit (TSSU)

Introduction to Theatre Sterile Supply Unit (TSSU)

The Theatre Sterile Supply Unit, commonly known as TSSU, is a specialized hospital department responsible for the safe processing, sterilization, and distribution of surgical instruments and medical devices. Operating as the biological heart of surgical safety, this unit bridges the gap between general hospital supplies and the operating theatre. A breakdown in its workflow directly exposes vulnerable patients to life-threatening nosocomial infections during surgery.

Zoning and Layout of a Standard TSSU

To prevent cross-contamination, a standard TSSU follows a strict unidirectional workflow. The physical layout is divided into distinct zones based on cleanliness levels:

  • Decontamination Zone: The receiving area where dirty, used instruments arrive. It operates under negative pressure to contain aerosols.
  • Preparation and Packaging Zone: A clean area where dried, inspected instruments are assembled into trays and wrapped.
  • Sterilization Zone: The chamber housing autoclaves where packaged items undergo terminal sterilization.
  • Sterile Storage Zone: A secure, positive-pressure environment where sterile packs are kept until required for surgery.

The Decontamination Process: Cleaning and Decontamination

Decontamination is the essential first step that removes bioburden, blood, and tissue debris from instruments. Skipping or rushing this stage renders subsequent sterilization ineffective because dried organic matter protects microorganisms from sterilizing agents.

  1. Sorting: Instruments are sorted carefully to separate fragile items from heavy retractors.
  2. Manual or Automated Washing: Ultrasonic cleaners use cavitation bubbles to dislodge debris from tiny box locks and serrations, followed by washer-disinfectors.
  3. Rinsing and Drying: Demineralized water rinses away chemical residues before thorough drying.

Inspection, Assembly, and Packaging of Instruments

Once cleaned and dried, instruments are transferred to the clean packing area. Staff inspect every item under magnifying lights to check for residual stains, hairline cracks, or dull cutting edges. Surgical sets are then meticulously assembled according to standardized checklists.

Packaging materials must allow penetration of the sterilizing agent while maintaining a sterile barrier after the cycle finishes. Common options include woven wrappers, non-woven sheets, peel pouches, and rigid container systems equipped with filter valves.

Sterilization Methods and Quality Assurance

Sterilization destroys all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial spores. Steam under pressure, or autoclaving, remains the gold standard for heat-tolerant instruments. For heat-sensitive items like flexible scopes, alternative methods such as ethylene oxide gas or hydrogen peroxide plasma are utilized.

Quality assurance requires continuous monitoring using three indicator types:

  • Physical monitors: Gauges, digital printouts, and time-temperature graphs.
  • Chemical indicators: Tape or strips that change color when exposed to specific sterilizing parameters.
  • Biological indicators: Spore vials containing Geobacillus stearothermophilus that prove the killing power of the cycle.

Storage, Distribution, and Inventory Management

Sterile items must be stored under controlled environmental conditions to maintain their integrity. Shelving units should be kept away from direct sunlight, moisture, and heavy foot traffic. The storage room maintains positive air pressure and cleanable surfaces.

Stock rotation follows the First-In, First-Out (FIFO) principle to prevent items from expiring on the shelf. Transport carts used for distribution must protect sterile goods from dust and moisture during transit to the operating suites.

Infection Control and Safety Protocols in TSSU

Working in a TSSU exposes personnel to sharp injuries, chemical fumes, and biological hazards. Strict adherence to occupational health standards protects staff and patients alike. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) in the decontamination area includes heavy-duty aprons, waterproof gloves, face shields, and eye protection.

Standard operating procedures also cover safe handling of chemical disinfectants, prompt reporting of needlestick injuries, and correct disposal of biohazardous waste generated during the cleaning process.
