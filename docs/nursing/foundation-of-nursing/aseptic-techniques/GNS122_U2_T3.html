Standard Precautions

Core Principles of Standard Precautions

Standard precautions form the primary tier of infection prevention in healthcare settings. These measures apply to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosed or suspected infection status. The fundamental assumption behind standard precautions is that all body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes may contain transmissible infectious agents.

  • Blood and body fluids: Treat all blood, secretions, and excretions except sweat as potentially infectious.
  • Universal application: Use safety measures consistently across every patient encounter in every clinical ward.
  • Risk reduction: Break the chain of transmission between patients, healthcare workers, and the hospital environment.

Hand Hygiene Practices and Techniques

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms transfer easily between patients and healthcare workers through transient hand contamination.

  1. Alcohol-based hand rub: Preferred for routine clinical tasks when hands are not visibly soiled, taking 20 to 30 seconds.
  2. Handwashing with soap and water: Mandatory when hands are visibly dirty, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like Clostridioides difficile, lasting 40 to 60 seconds.
  3. Friction and coverage: Ensure every surface of the hands, including fingertips, thumbs, and web spaces, makes contact with the cleaning agent.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and potential pathogens. Selecting the correct item depends on the anticipated mode of transmission during a specific task.

PPE ItemPrimary Clinical Purpose
GlovesProtect hands from blood, body fluids, and non-intact skin
GownsProtect clothing and exposed skin from splashes of fluids
Masks and Eye ProtectionShield mucous membranes of the nose, mouth, and eyes from splashes
Respirators (N95)Filter airborne particles during aerosol-generating procedures

Donning and doffing sequences matter significantly. Gloves are always removed first because they carry the highest microbial load, followed by hand hygiene.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene controls the source of pathogens at the point of entry and exit in healthcare settings. Patients, visitors, and staff must manage respiratory secretions to limit droplet spread.

  • Cover the mouth and nose with a tissue when coughing or sneezing.
  • Dispose of used tissues immediately into the nearest waste receptacle.
  • Perform hand hygiene after contact with respiratory secretions.
  • Maintain spatial separation of at least one meter from others in waiting areas if coughing.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps

Sharps injuries pose a severe occupational threat for nursing personnel due to the risk of blood-borne pathogen transmission like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Safe handling practices protect both the nurse and support staff.

  • Never recap needles: Recapping causes a high percentage of accidental puncture wounds.
  • Immediate disposal: Drop used needles, lancets, and scalpel blades directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container immediately after use.
  • Capacity limits: Never fill a sharps container past the designated fill line, usually three-quarters full, to prevent protruding items.

Environmental Cleaning and Decontamination

Hospital surfaces act as reservoirs for microbial pathogens that survive for extended periods. Routine environmental cleaning removes organic soil and reduces pathogen load.

  • High-touch surfaces: Bed rails, call buttons, bedside tables, and door handles require frequent disinfection because they are touched constantly.
  • Detergent and disinfectant: Cleaning with water and detergent must precede chemical disinfection, as organic matter inactivates many germicidal agents.
  • Contact time: Disinfectants require a specific wet contact time specified by the manufacturer to kill target organisms effectively.

Management of Patient Care Equipment and Linen

Reusable medical devices and soiled textiles require structured handling protocols to prevent indirect contact transmission.

  • Single-use items: Never reuse items labeled for single use, such as disposable syringes and certain tubing.
  • Reprocessing: Clean and sterilize or high-level disinfect reusable equipment like blood pressure cuffs and thermometers between patients.
  • Soiled linen: Handle used linen with minimal agitation to prevent microbial aerosolization, and place it directly into designated leak-proof laundry bags without sorting in patient rooms.

Prevention of Occupational Exposure and Needle-Stick Injuries

Accidental exposure to blood and body fluids demands immediate, standardized post-exposure management to minimize infection risks.

  • Immediate first aid: Wash needle-stick injuries and cuts immediately with soap and water. Flush splashes to the eyes or mucous membranes with copious amounts of clean water or normal saline.
  • Reporting: Report the incident instantly to the designated supervisor or occupational health unit to initiate evaluation.
  • Prophylaxis: Assess the source patient's infection status and administer post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV or Hepatitis B when indicated within optimal time windows.
