Care of Drainage Tubes

Introduction and Purpose of Drainage Tubes

Drainage tubes are hollow devices placed in wounds, body cavities, or hollow organs to remove fluid, blood, or air. Their primary clinical purpose is to prevent the dangerous accumulation of serous fluid, pus, or hematomas that can delay wound healing and promote bacterial growth.

Common clinical scenarios requiring drainage include post-operative abdominal surgeries, cardiothoracic procedures to evacuate chest air and blood, and deep abscess debridement. By keeping dead space collapsed and removing contaminants, these tubes support optimal tissue recovery.

Types of Drainage Systems and Mechanisms

Drainage systems operate either passively or actively depending on the clinical need of the patient.

  • Passive Drains: Rely on gravity and capillary action to move fluid from areas of high pressure to low pressure. Examples include the Penrose drain, which allows open drainage onto a dressing.
  • Active Drains: Utilize negative pressure suction to continuously pull fluid out of a body cavity. Examples include the Jackson-Pratt (JP) bulb and Hemovac drain, both of which use manual compression to create a gentle vacuum.
  • Chest Drainage Systems: Specialized water-seal or digital suction systems designed specifically to restore negative intrapleural pressure in the thoracic cavity.

Routine Assessment and Monitoring

Regular nursing assessment ensures the drainage system functions properly and identifies early signs of complications. Inspect the insertion site every shift for redness, swelling, foul odor, or excessive tenderness. Check all tubing connections to ensure they remain secure, airtight, and free of kinks or accidental looping that could obstruct flow. Verify that the collection device is hanging below the level of the insertion site for gravity systems, or that suction is active for closed systems.

Measuring, Recording, and Assessing Output

Accurate quantification of drainage output provides vital data regarding hemostasis and healing. Measure output at the end of every nursing shift, or more frequently if ordered for high-output states.

  1. Emptying: Wear clean gloves, open the pour spout without contaminating it, and drain fluid into a graduated cylinder.
  2. Assessing Character: Note the color, consistency, and odor. Normal progression moves from sanguinous (bright red) to serosanguinous (pink) and finally to serous (clear yellow).
  3. Recording: Document the exact volume in milliliters on the fluid balance chart alongside the physical characteristics.

A sudden spike in bright red blood or a complete stop in output warrants immediate medical notification.

Infection Control and Dressing Changes

Drain insertion sites represent direct portals of entry for pathogens into sterile body cavities. Perform meticulous hand hygiene and use aseptic technique during all dressing changes. Inspect the skin around the suture line for signs of localized infection such as warmth, purulent discharge, or spreading erythema. Cleanse the site using sterile normal saline, working in a circular motion from the center outward away from the drain site. Apply a sterile split-drain dressing snugly around the tubing to absorb minor exudate and protect the skin.

Troubleshooting Common Complications

Drainage tubes can present mechanical or clinical challenges that require prompt nursing intervention.

  • Accidental Dislodgement: If a drain falls out, apply immediate sterile pressure to the site and notify the surgeon. Never attempt to reinsert a partially or fully dislodged tube.
  • Clot Occlusion: If clots block the lumen, verify if milking or stripping is ordered, or report to the doctor for possible flushing.
  • Tissue Ingrowth: Long-term drains can adhere to surrounding tissue, causing pain and resistance during removal.

Removal of Drainage Tubes and Post-Removal Care

Drain removal occurs when output decreases to minimal levels, typically less than 30 mL per 24 hours, indicating the cavity has closed. Gather necessary supplies including suture removal kits, sterile dressings, and biohazard bags. Medicate the patient for pain approximately 30 minutes before the procedure if prescribed. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath and hold it (Valsalva maneuver) during removal to prevent air embolism and ease discomfort. Pull the tube smoothly and steadily, immediately applying an occlusive dressing to the site. Monitor the site closely afterwards for persistent leakage or hematoma formation.
