Decontamination

Introduction to Decontamination in Nursing

Decontamination is the foundation of patient safety in any healthcare facility across Nigeria, from busy tertiary teaching hospitals to rural primary health centres. It refers to the removal or destruction of harmful microorganisms to render an item or environmental surface safe for use or handling. Without proper decontamination, inanimate objects and surfaces easily act as vehicles for cross-contamination, spreading hospital-acquired infections among vulnerable patients. Nurses carry the direct responsibility of ensuring that every instrument, piece of equipment, and surrounding space meets strict hygienic standards before patient contact. Integrating these practices into daily shift routines breaks the chain of infection and protects both staff and community members.

Definitions and Key Terminology: Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization

  • Cleaning: The physical removal of organic material, soil, and foreign matter using water, detergents, and mechanical action. This step is mandatory because dried blood or proteins shield microorganisms from subsequent chemical agents or heat.
  • Disinfection: A process that eliminates many or all pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, except for bacterial spores, using liquid chemicals or pasteurization.
  • Sterilization: A validated process used to render an object free from all forms of viable microorganisms, including highly resistant bacterial endospores.

The Spaulding Classification System for Patient Care Items

The Spaulding classification categorizes medical devices based on the degree of risk for infection involved in their use.

  1. Critical Items: Instruments that enter sterile tissue or the vascular system, such as surgical scalpel blades, needles, and intravascular catheters. These demand absolute sterilization.
  2. Semi-critical Items: Equipment that comes into contact with non-intact skin or mucous membranes, such as respiratory therapy tubes and gastrointestinal endoscopes. These require high-level disinfection.
  3. Non-critical Items: Objects touching only intact skin but not mucous membranes, such as blood pressure cuffs, stethoscope tubing, and bedside tables. These need low-level to intermediate-level disinfection.

Step-by-Step Decontamination Process for Instruments and Surfaces

Effective decontamination follows a strict chronological workflow to prevent accidental injury and recontamination.

  1. Point of Use Preparation: Wipe gross soil off instruments immediately after use and keep them moist so debris does not dry.
  2. Transport: Move contaminated items in leak-proof, puncture-resistant containers to the utility room.
  3. Cleaning: Submerge items in warm water mixed with enzymatic detergent, scrubbing meticulously while wearing heavy-duty gloves.
  4. Rinsing and Drying: Rinse thoroughly with clean water to remove soap residues, then dry completely to prevent rust or dilution of disinfectants.
  5. Disinfection or Sterilization: Subject the item to the appropriate thermal or chemical process based on its classification.
  6. Storage: Store processed items in a clean, dry, dust-free environment away from sinks and moisture.

Selection and Proper Use of Chemical Disinfectants

Choosing the right chemical disinfectant depends on the nature of the surface, contact time required, and the target microorganisms. Common agents include chlorine-based compounds, hydrogen peroxide, and alcohols. Chlorine solutions, such as sodium hypochlorite, are widely available in Nigerian wards but degrade rapidly when exposed to light, heat, or organic dirt. Nurses must prepare fresh dilution batches daily and verify the concentration using test strips whenever possible. Always adhere strictly to the manufacturer's recommended contact time—the surface must remain visibly wet for the entire duration to ensure pathogens are actually inactivated.

Occupational Health and Safety Measures during Decontamination

Decontamination exposes healthcare workers to concentrated chemicals, sharp instruments, and infectious body fluids. Personal protective equipment (PPE) is non-negotiable and includes heavy-duty utility gloves, plastic aprons, eye protection, and closed footwear. A common error is wearing sterile examination gloves for washing equipment; these tear easily against sharp edges and do not protect the forearms. If chemical splashes occur in the eye, immediate flushing with clean water at an eyewash station or tap for at least fifteen minutes is mandatory. Staff should also receive vaccinations against Hepatitis B to protect against bloodborne transmission risks in the utility room.

Management of Healthcare Waste and Spillages

Safe disposal of waste is an extension of the decontamination process. Healthcare facilities utilize color-coded segregation systems—typically black for general domestic waste, yellow for infectious and pathological waste, and brown or red for sharps and chemical containers. Sharps boxes must never be filled beyond the designated fill line to prevent needle-stick injuries. For bodily fluid spillages on floors or surfaces, staff must restrict access immediately, wear full PPE, apply absorbent material, and disinfect the area using a high-concentration chlorine solution from the periphery inward.
