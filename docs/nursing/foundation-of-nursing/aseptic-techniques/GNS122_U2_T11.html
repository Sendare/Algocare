Wound Dressing

Introduction and Purpose of Wound Dressing

A wound dressing is a sterile pad or application applied directly to a wound to promote healing and protect the area from further harm. Dressing a wound creates an optimal environment that accelerates tissue repair while keeping external contaminants away.

Main objectives of wound dressing include:

  • Protection: Shields the vulnerable tissue from mechanical trauma and environmental microbes.
  • Absorbency: Manages excessive exudate, pus, or blood to prevent skin maceration around the wound edges.
  • Moisture balance: Maintains a moist wound bed, which encourages epithelial cell migration, unlike dry environments that form hard scabs.
  • Hemostasis: Controls bleeding through direct pressure or specialized materials.

Types of Wound Dressing Materials

Different wounds require specific dressing materials depending on depth, location, and exudate levels. Selecting the correct material prevents tissue damage and speeds up recovery.

Aseptic Technique and Infection Control

Wound dressing procedures demand strict adherence to aseptic technique to prevent introducing hospital-acquired pathogens into compromised tissue. Every item crossing the sterile field must remain uncontaminated.

Key infection control practices include:

  • Performing thorough hand hygiene before and after patient contact.
  • Utilizing sterile forceps or sterile gloves when touching the inner wound bed.
  • Cleaning the wound from the center outward, moving from the cleanest area to the dirtiest area.
  • Disposing of contaminated materials immediately in appropriate biohazard bins.

Step-by-Step Wound Dressing Procedure

Executing a proper wound dressing procedure requires methodical preparation, execution, and cleanup.

  1. Preparation: Verify the medical order, explain the procedure to the patient, ensure privacy, and gather all necessary sterile supplies.
  2. Removal: Put on clean gloves, gently remove the old dressing, and inspect the drainage on the material. Discard gloves and perform hand hygiene.
  3. Assessment: Inspect the wound for size, depth, color, odor, and signs of infection.
  4. Cleansing: Open the sterile tray, don sterile gloves, and clean the wound using prescribed antiseptic solutions or normal saline.
  5. Application: Apply the new primary and secondary dressings securely without restricting circulation.
  6. Cleanup: Discard all waste materials safely and document the procedure accurately.

Complications and Documentation

Improper wound management or failure to recognize early warning signs can lead to severe local and systemic complications. Nurses must monitor for adverse changes and record findings accurately.

Common complications include:

  • Wound infection: Characterized by increasing pain, localized warmth, purulent discharge, and foul odor.
  • Dehiscence: Partial or complete separation of outer wound layers, common in abdominal surgical sites.
  • Evisceration: Protrusion of visceral organs through a disrupted wound opening, constituting a surgical emergency.
  • Maceration: Softening and breakdown of skin due to prolonged exposure to moisture or heavy exudate.

Documentation must include the exact location, dimensions, appearance of the wound bed, character and amount of drainage, patient tolerance, and type of dressing applied.
