Use of Masks

Introduction to Respiratory Protection in Nursing

Respiratory protection is a cornerstone of infection prevention and control in clinical practice. Pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, influenza viruses, and SARS-CoV-2 spread primarily through the air via droplets or aerosols generated when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks. Nurses stand at the front line of patient care, making proper barrier protection vital to prevent both occupational acquisition and nosocomial transmission. Using a mask creates a mechanical filter that intercepts infectious particles before they enter the upper or lower respiratory tract.

Types of Medical and Surgical Masks

Medical and surgical masks are loose-fitting devices designed to create a physical barrier between the mouth and nose of the wearer and potential contaminants in the immediate environment. They are regulated by fluid resistance, filtration efficiency, and breathability.

  • Surgical Masks: Flat or pleated disposable barriers secured with ear loops or ties, primarily protecting the sterile field from the wearer's respiratory secretions.
  • Procedure Masks: Similar to surgical masks but typically secured with ear loops, often used during general patient examinations.
  • Fluid-Resistant Masks: Specialized layers added to withstand splashes of blood or bodily fluids, critical during surgical or traumatic procedures.

Understanding Respirators (N95 and Higher)

Unlike loose-fitting surgical masks, respirators like the N95, FFP2, or PAPR are tight-fitting filtering devices engineered to achieve a very close facial seal and efficient filtration of airborne particles. An N95 respirator blocks at least 95 percent of very small test particles, including bacteria and viruses. They require a rigorous fit test to ensure no gaps exist along the facial seal. Nurses caring for patients with airborne isolation precautions—such as pulmonary tuberculosis, measles, or chickenpox—must don a certified respirator rather than a standard surgical mask.

Indications for Mask Usage in Healthcare Settings

Clinical judgment dictates when masks are necessary based on transmission routes and hospital protocols. Standard precautions require masks whenever there is a risk of splashing or spraying of blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions. Transmission-based precautions divide usage into droplet precautions (surgical masks within one meter of the patient) and airborne precautions (respirators upon entering the room of a suspected airborne infection). Nurses also wear masks to protect severely immunocompromised patients from microbes shed by the healthcare worker.

Proper Technique for Donning and Fit Testing

The effectiveness of any mask depends heavily on correct application and sealing. Before touching a mask, perform hand hygiene. Inspect the device for any tears, defects, or damaged straps. Position the mask over the nose and mouth, secure the ties or elastic bands behind your head or ears, and mold the flexible metal nosepiece firmly against the bridge of your nose. For respirators, perform a user seal check: inhale deeply to feel the respirator collapse slightly, and exhale sharply to check for air leaks around the edges.

Safe Removal and Disposal Procedures

Removing personal protective equipment incorrectly can contaminate the hands and face. Untie or stretch the bottom straps first, followed by the top straps, without touching the front filter surface of the mask. Handle the mask solely by its fasteners to lift it away from the face. Immediately discard disposable masks into designated infectious waste bins or safety boxes. Never let a mask hang around the neck or tuck it into a pocket for later use. Conclude the removal process by performing thorough hand hygiene.

Common Mistakes and Infection Control Pitfalls

Clinical observations reveal frequent deviations from proper mask protocols that undermine infection control. Common errors include wearing masks below the nose, leaving the chin exposed, reusing single-use disposables across multiple patient shifts, and touching the front of the mask constantly while talking or providing care. Wet, soiled, or damaged masks lose their filtration integrity and must be discarded immediately. Maintaining strict discipline regarding mask protocol safeguards both the healthcare worker and vulnerable hospital populations.
