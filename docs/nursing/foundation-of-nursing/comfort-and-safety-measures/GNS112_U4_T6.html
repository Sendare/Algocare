Methods of Lifting Patients

Body Mechanics and Nurse Safety

Proper body mechanics protect nurses from chronic lower back injuries during patient handling tasks. By keeping the center of gravity low and using major muscle groups in the legs rather than the back, healthcare workers maintain stability. The base of support should be wide, achieved by placing feet shoulder-width apart. Twisting the spine while lifting must be avoided at all times, as this places immense pressure on the intervertebral discs. Pivot with the feet instead of turning the waist when changing direction.

Mobility Assessment and Planning

Before attempting any lift or transfer, the nurse must evaluate the patient's physical capabilities and cognitive status. Assessing weight-bearing ability, upper body strength, and ability to follow instructions prevents unexpected falls.

  • Independent: Patient moves safely without assistance.
  • Minimal Assist: Patient performs 75% or more of the effort.
  • Moderate Assist: Patient performs 50% to 74% of the effort.
  • Maximum Assist: Patient contributes less than 50% of the effort.

Preparation and Clear Communication

Preparation minimizes accidents during patient transfers. Clear communication aligns the nursing team and the patient so everyone acts simultaneously.

  1. Clear the pathway: Remove IV poles, stray wires, and footstools from the floor.
  2. Lock all wheels: Secure both the bed brakes and the wheelchair or stretcher brakes.
  3. Explain the steps: Tell the patient what will happen and how they can help.
  4. Establish a count: Use a synchronized countdown like 'One, two, three, lift' to initiate movement.

Bed-to-Chair and Pivot Transfers

Transferring a patient from a bed to a chair requires careful positioning and a reliable pivot technique. The chair should be placed at a 45-degree angle to the bed on the patient's strong side whenever possible.

  • Lower the bed to its lowest position and ensure feet flat on the floor.
  • Stand close to the patient, place arms around their waist or use a transfer belt.
  • Block the patient's knees with your own knees to prevent buckling.
  • Pivot smoothly on the foot furthest from the chair, guiding the patient down into the seat.

Mechanical Lifts and Assistive Devices

When a patient is completely dependent, morbidly obese, or unable to bear any weight, mechanical lifts are mandatory. Floor-based hydraulic lifts and ceiling lifts protect both the patient and staff from injury. Always inspect the sling for tears or frayed straps before use. Never leave a patient unattended in a mechanical sling once hoisted.

Documentation and Post-Transfer Safety

Recording the transfer details ensures continuity of care and monitors changes in patient mobility. Document the type of transfer performed, the level of assistance required, how the patient tolerated the procedure, and any complications like dizziness or pain. Ensure the call bell is within easy reach before leaving the room.
