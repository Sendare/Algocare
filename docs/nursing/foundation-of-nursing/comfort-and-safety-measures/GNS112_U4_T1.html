Care of Hospital Equipment

Nursing Responsibility in Equipment Care

Nurses are the primary daily users of medical devices in hospital wards, making equipment stewardship a core clinical duty. Proper handling directly impacts patient safety, diagnostic accuracy, and hospital operating costs. Every piece of apparatus used at the bedside, from basic thermometers to complex infusion pumps, requires careful manipulation to prevent premature wear and mechanical failure. When nurses inspect, operate, and store tools according to standard operating procedures, they minimize unexpected device breakdowns during critical medical emergencies. Ward management also depends on nurses identifying misuse early and reporting functional anomalies before patient care gets compromised.

Classification and Types of Hospital Equipment

Medical devices in Nigerian healthcare facilities span various categories based on their function, frequency of use, and composition. Recognizing these groups helps determine appropriate handling, cleaning, and storage methods.

  • Diagnostic equipment: Tools like stethoscope, sphygmomanometer, and glucometer used to assess patient health status.
  • Therapeutic equipment: Apparatus such as nebulizers, suction machines, and infusion pumps used to treat illnesses.
  • Consumable equipment: Single-use items like syringes and infusion sets discarded immediately after one procedure.
  • Durable equipment: Long-lasting assets like hospital beds, wheelchairs, and drip stands used repeatedly across multiple patients.

General Principles for Handling and Using Equipment

Operating medical devices requires strict adherence to manufacturer instructions and hospital protocols. Never pull an electrical cord from the wall by the wire itself; always grasp the molded plug to prevent internal wiring damage. Keep electronic monitors away from excessive moisture and direct sources of intense heat. When transporting heavy apparatus like oxygen cylinders or mobile X-ray units, engage safety locks and use designated ramps instead of stairs. Clean surfaces immediately after every patient contact using approved ward disinfectants to prevent organic soil accumulation.

Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization Protocols

Infection control hinges on the proper decontamination reprocessing cycle. Cleaning must always precede disinfection and sterilization because dried organic matter shields microorganisms from chemical agents and heat.

Surrounding staff must wear heavy-duty utility gloves during manual scrubbing stages to prevent accidental needle pricks and chemical burns.

Maintenance, Storage, and Inventory Control

Systematic inventory and climate-controlled storage extend the usable lifespan of hospital assets. Sensitive electronic devices degrade rapidly in humid environments, making cool, dry, dust-free storage rooms essential across Nigerian facilities. Regular servicing schedules prevent sudden mechanical failures during busy shifts. Nurses participate in stock audits by cross-checking physical items against ledger books or digital spreadsheets, identifying missing or misplaced units early. Equipment should be stored neatly with accessories attached, avoiding tangled cables or piled-up heavy components that cause structural stress.

Safety Measures and Prevention of Cross-Infection

Shared medical equipment serves as a primary vector for nosocomial pathogens if infection control protocols break down. Healthcare-associated infections spread easily when thermometers, blood pressure cuffs, and glucometers move between patients without undergoing proper decontamination. Barrier precautions, hand hygiene before and after touching shared apparatus, and single-patient-use accessories interrupt this transmission chain. Common misconceptions suggest that wiping a dirty surface with a dry cloth removes germs; however, true decontamination requires wet friction and specific chemical contact times.

Management of Defective or Damaged Equipment

Discovering a faulty medical device requires immediate action to protect patients and staff from harm. Tag the malfunctioning apparatus with a clear, highly visible warning sign indicating it is out of order, and remove it from the active ward area immediately. Document the specific defect in the ward maintenance logbook and notify the biomedical engineering department promptly. Never attempt DIY electrical repairs on hospital machinery unless qualified; unauthorized tampering invalidates warranties and creates severe electrocution or fire hazards.
