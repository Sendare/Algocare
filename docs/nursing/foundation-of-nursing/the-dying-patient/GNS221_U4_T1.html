Total Care of the Very Ill Patient

Introduction to the Very Ill Patient

A very ill patient is one experiencing acute physiological instability, multiple organ dysfunction, or life-threatening trauma requiring constant observation and complex medical intervention. In Nigerian hospital settings, managing these cases often strains resources, demanding high clinical acuity and prioritization from nurses. The primary objective is preventing deterioration and restoring baseline health through systematic evaluation and swift action.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

  • Airway and Breathing: Check respiratory rate, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation continuously.
  • Circulation: Monitor pulse strength, capillary refill, blood pressure, and urine output.
  • Neurological Status: Assess level of consciousness using the Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • Trend Analysis: Compare hourly vital signs against baseline data to catch subtle declines early.

Maintaining Physiological Stability and Airway Management

Ensuring a patent airway is the absolute first priority in critical care. Obstruction leads rapidly to hypoxia, brain damage, and cardiac arrest. Nurses must keep suction machines ready, position unconscious patients in a lateral recovery or semi-Fowler's posture to prevent aspiration, and administer supplemental oxygen as prescribed. Hemodynamic stability also requires monitoring intravenous fluid infusion rates closely to prevent fluid overload in compromised hearts.

Nutrition, Hydration, and Elimination Care

Metabolic demands remain high during severe illness, yet patients cannot always eat normally. Enteral nutrition via nasogastric tube or parenteral nutrition supports catabolic states. Hydration is tracked meticulously through strict intake and output measurement. Elimination care involves managing urinary catheters and attending to bowel function to prevent complications like paralytic ileus or severe constipation.

Hygiene, Skin Integrity, and Prevention of Complications

Immobility breaks down the body's natural defenses, leading rapidly to pressure ulcers, contractures, and hypostatic pneumonia. Nurses must turn patients every two hours, keep skin clean and dry, and provide passive range-of-motion exercises. Regular mouth care prevents oral infections and maintains mucous membrane moisture when patients breathe through their mouths.

Pain Management and Comfort Measures

Pain is subjective and often under-reported by very ill patients who cannot communicate verbally. Nurses observe non-verbal signs such as grimacing, restlessness, and elevated vital signs. Administering analgesics promptly as prescribed, adjusting bed positioning, and ensuring a quiet environment promote physical relief and psychological calm.

Psychosocial and Spiritual Support for Patient and Family

Critical illness isolates patients from their normal routines and instills deep fear in their relatives. Nurses act as a bridge by providing honest, compassionate updates, allowing family presence when safe, and respecting cultural or religious practices. Even unresponsive patients should be spoken to gently, as hearing is often the last sense to fade.

Ethical Considerations and End-of-Life Care

When recovery is no longer possible, the focus shifts to palliative care and dignified dying. Nurses navigate complex ethical issues like respecting advance directives, managing pain aggressively despite respiratory depression risks, and supporting grieving families. Ensuring a peaceful, pain-free passage remains a fundamental duty of professional nursing practice.
