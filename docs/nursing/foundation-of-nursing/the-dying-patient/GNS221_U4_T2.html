Care of the Dying Patient

Introduction to Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care focuses on improving the quality of life for patients and families facing serious, life-threatening illnesses. Unlike curative treatments, it addresses physical, psychological, and spiritual distress. End-of-life care is a specific subset of palliative care delivered when a cure is no longer possible and life expectancy is measured in months, weeks, or days. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where family structures heavily influence decision-making, nurses must bridge the gap between hospital protocols and family expectations. Early integration of palliative principles prevents unnecessary suffering and helps patients navigate terminal illness with dignity.

Physiological and Psychological Changes in the Dying Patient

As the body approaches death, multiple organ systems gradually shut down. Recognizing these biological markers helps nurses prepare families and tailor comfort measures.

  • Cardiovascular changes: Peripheral circulation fails, leading to cold, mottled extremities and a drop in blood pressure.
  • Respiratory patterns: Cheyne-Stokes respirations, characterized by cycles of deep breathing followed by periods of apnea, often develop.
  • Neurological shifts: Patients experience altered levels of consciousness, ranging from drowsiness to unresponsiveness, sometimes accompanied by terminal delirium.
  • Sensory decline: Vision narrows and hearing is frequently the last sense to fade, making it vital to speak to patients even when they appear comatose.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment at End of Life

Assessing a dying patient requires a shift from diagnostic investigation to comfort monitoring. Nurses focus on identifying distressing symptoms before they escalate. Pain is evaluated regularly, using behavioral cues like facial grimacing or restlessness for patients who cannot speak. Assessment also includes evaluating skin integrity, hydration status, and bladder function, as urinary retention or incontinence frequently occurs. Equally important is assessing the family's readiness and coping capacity, which directly influences how well they can support their dying loved one.

Symptom Management and Comfort Measures

Managing physical distress requires both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Opioids remain the gold standard for managing terminal pain and dyspnea, administered regularly to maintain a steady therapeutic blood level. Nurses must manage side effects such as opioid-induced constipation proactively. For noisy respiratory secretions, often called the death rattle, repositioning the patient and administering anticholinergic drugs helps reduce secretions without causing excessive suctioning trauma. Oral care with moist swabs prevents painful mucous membrane dryness when fluid intake ceases.

Psychosocial and Spiritual Support for Patient and Family

Dying involves profound emotional and spiritual turmoil. Patients often process grief through the classic stages of denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance, though not always in a linear sequence. Listening actively and offering a comforting presence validates their feelings. In many Nigerian communities, spiritual and religious beliefs heavily shape how individuals accept mortality. Collaborating with hospital chaplains, imams, or traditional spiritual leaders chosen by the family provides immense comfort. Nurses must remain culturally sensitive and non-judgmental, allowing space for prayers, rituals, and reconciliation among relatives.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in End-of-Life Care

End-of-life care frequently raises complex ethical dilemmas, including decisions regarding withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, managing artificial nutrition, and administering high-dose analgesics that may hasten death under the principle of double effect. Nurses advocate for patient autonomy, ensuring that expressed wishes are respected whenever possible. In settings where formal advance directives are uncommon, family consensus often guides surrogate decision-making. Nurses must navigate these moral challenges by adhering to professional codes of conduct and institutional guidelines.

Post-Mortem Care and Supporting the Bereaved Family

Post-mortem care begins immediately after a physician confirms death. Nurses prepare the body respectfully, maintaining the dignity of the deceased. Medical devices and tubes are removed unless an autopsy or coroner's inquest is required. The body is bathed, aligned in a natural supine position with eyes closed, and clean dressings are applied. Following cultural and religious customs in Nigeria, family members are often allowed to participate in washing or viewing the body. Bereavement support continues after death, as nurses offer condolences, guide families through administrative discharge processes, and provide resources for grief counseling.
