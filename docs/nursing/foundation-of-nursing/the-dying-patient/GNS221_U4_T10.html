Last Offices

Introduction and Verification of Death

Last offices refer to the care given to a body immediately after death has been officially pronounced. Nurses play a crucial role in transitioning from active patient care to dignified post-mortem care. Before any physical preparation begins, formal verification of death must be carried out by a qualified medical practitioner. The nurse must never presume death based solely on the cessation of breathing or absence of a pulse without physician confirmation, except in clear-cut cases of decapitation or rigor mortis. Once death is certified, the exact time is recorded in the patient's records.

Legal and Cultural Considerations in Nigeria

Providing last offices in Nigeria requires deep sensitivity to the country's diverse religious and cultural beliefs. Muslim patients require immediate burial rites, often necessitating swift completion of last offices and prompt release of the body to family members. Conversely, many Christian denominations and traditional practices involve specific funeral arrangements that take longer to coordinate. Nurses must also be aware of medico-legal cases, such as sudden, suspicious, or unnatural deaths. In such instances, the body must not be washed or dressed, and invasive tubes must remain intact until a coroner or police clearance is granted.

Standard Precautions and Preparation

The body of a deceased person can still transmit infectious agents. Universal precautions remain mandatory throughout the last offices procedure.

  • Hand Hygiene: Perform thorough handwashing before and after the procedure.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Wear clean gloves, plastic aprons, and masks to protect against body fluid exposure.
  • Equipment Assembly: Gather cotton wool, bandages, basin of warm water, soap, shroud or clean bedsheet, identification tags, and a body bag before approaching the bed.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Last Offices

Carrying out last offices demands dignity, privacy, and methodical care.

  1. Privacy: Screen the bed and ensure other patients and visitors are respectfully managed.
  2. Alignment: Place the body in a supine position, flat on the back, with arms straight at the sides or crossed over the abdomen. Close the eyes by gently holding the lashes down for a few seconds.
  3. Removal of Tubes: Carefully remove intravenous lines, catheters, and drains unless it is a coroner's case. Dress puncture sites with waterproof dressings.
  4. Hygiene: Wash the body gently with warm water and soap, drying thoroughly. Comb the hair.
  5. Orifices: Place cotton wool in natural orifices (rectum, vagina) if there is risk of leakage due to sphincter relaxation.
  6. Identification: Attach identification tags securely to the wrist and ankle before wrapping.

Handling Valuables and Documentation

Managing a deceased patient's belongings requires strict accountability to prevent loss and build trust with the family. All jewelry, money, phones, and religious items must be carefully removed in the presence of another staff nurse or witness. These items are itemized, logged in a valuables register, and signed for by both the nurse and the family member receiving them.

Documentation in the nursing care plan and ward register must include the time of death, the name of the certifying doctor, notifications made to relatives, disposition of valuables, and the exact time the body was transferred to the mortuary.

Transfer to the Mortuary

The final stage of last offices involves transporting the deceased from the ward to the mortuary. The body should be covered respectfully with a clean sheet or shroud and placed on a trolley. Moving the body must be done discreetly to avoid distressing other patients and visitors in the hospital corridors. Accompanying the transfer is the mortuary tag, matching the hospital identification details, along with the case notes and completed mortuary handover form. Upon arrival, the ward nurse hands over the body and documentation to the mortuary attendant, ensuring a countersigned receipt is obtained.
