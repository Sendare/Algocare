Principles and Rules of Drug Administration

Introduction to Medication Administration in Nursing Practice

Medication administration stands as one of the most critical daily duties for a nurse working in any Nigerian health facility, ranging from busy tertiary teaching hospitals to rural primary health centres. Nurses act as the final safety barrier between a prescribed pharmacological agent and the patient. This responsibility requires absolute vigilance, sound clinical judgment, and strict adherence to established protocols. A mistake at this stage can lead to severe adverse drug reactions, prolonged hospital stays, or even patient mortality. Understanding the foundational principles ensures that patient safety remains the core focus of every clinical shift.

The Fundamental Rights of Drug Administration

The traditional rights of medication administration provide a systematic framework to prevent errors. While literature often expands these, mastery of the core rights is non-negotiable for safe nursing practice.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using two unique identifiers, such as checking the hospital identification band and asking the patient to state their full name.
  • Right Drug: Compare the medication label against the medication administration record at least three times.
  • Right Dose: Double-check calculations, especially for pediatric patients or weight-based drugs, and have a colleague verify high-alert medications.
  • Right Route: Confirm the method of delivery matches the medical order, whether oral, intravenous, intramuscular, or topical.
  • Right Time: Administer drugs within the prescribed window to maintain therapeutic blood levels.
  • Right Documentation: Record the medication immediately after administration, never before.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before administering any medication, the nurse must evaluate the clinical readiness of the patient. This involves checking recent laboratory results, assessing current vital signs, and screening for known allergies. For instance, giving an antihypertensive drug without checking the current blood pressure can precipitate dangerous hypotension. Patient preparation also involves health education. The nurse must explain what the drug is, why it is prescribed, and what common side effects to expect. Clear communication builds trust and improves treatment compliance, particularly in outpatient or long-term care settings.

General Rules and Safety Protocols for Drug Handling

Safe drug handling minimizes contamination and accidental administration errors. Nurses must perform the three safety checks: checking the drug label against the medication sheet when retrieving it, when preparing it, and just before administering it or returning the container to storage. Never administer a medication prepared by someone else. Sterile technique must guide the preparation of injectables to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Furthermore, medications showing signs of discoloration, particulate matter, or expired shelf life must be discarded according to institutional policy rather than used.

Routes of Drug Administration and Associated Techniques

The route of administration dictates how fast a drug reaches systemic circulation and takes effect. Common routes include oral, sublingual, topical, inhalation, and parenteral routes such as subcutaneous, intramuscular, and intravenous injections. Each route requires specific anatomical site selection and procedural precision. For instance, intramuscular injections require identifying proper landmarks like the ventrogluteal or deltoid sites to avoid nerve injury. Intravenous administration demands rigorous aseptic technique to prevent introducing pathogens directly into the bloodstream.

Documentation and Reporting Standards

Accurate clinical documentation forms a legal and professional record of nursing care. Every medication entry must include the exact drug name, dose, time, route, and the signature or electronic stamp of the nurse. If a medication is omitted or refused by a patient, the nurse must document the reason clearly in the nursing notes and report it to the attending physician. Falsifying documentation or recording a drug before administration constitutes professional misconduct and compromises patient safety by creating false clinical records.

Prevention and Management of Medication Errors

Despite rigorous safeguards, medication errors still occur due to systemic flaws, fatigue, or communication breakdowns. When an error happens, the immediate priority is patient safety: assess the patient's clinical condition, monitor vital signs, and notify the physician promptly. After stabilizing the patient, the nurse must complete an incident report according to institutional guidelines. Blaming individuals rarely solves systemic issues; instead, healthcare facilities must analyze root causes to improve safety systems, enhance double-checking cultures, and reduce environmental distractions during drug preparation.
