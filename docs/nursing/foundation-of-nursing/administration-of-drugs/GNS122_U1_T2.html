Oral Route of Drug Administration

Introduction and Principles of the Oral Route

The oral route involves taking medication by mouth for absorption along the gastrointestinal tract, primarily in the stomach and small intestine. It remains the most common, cost-effective, and patient-friendly method of drug delivery. Once swallowed, the drug dissolves in gastrointestinal fluids, crosses the mucosal lining, and enters the portal circulation where it passes through the liver before reaching systemic circulation. This journey means oral drugs are subject to the first-pass metabolism, which can significantly reduce the active concentration of the drug reaching the target site. Nurses must understand that absorption rates vary wildly depending on gastric pH, stomach contents, intestinal motility, and the presence of food.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Oral Medication

Administering drugs orally offers distinct clinical benefits alongside notable limitations that dictate when this route is inappropriate.

  • Convenience: Patients can self-administer pills easily without specialized clinical training.
  • Economy: Manufacturing and distributing tablets and syrups costs significantly less than injectables.
  • Safety: The intact gastrointestinal tract provides a natural barrier, and accidental overdoses can sometimes be mitigated via activated charcoal or gastric lavage.
  • First-pass effect: Liver metabolism can render certain drugs entirely ineffective before they reach systemic circulation.
  • Delayed onset: Absorption takes time, making this route unsuitable for acute, life-threatening emergencies.
  • GI irritation: Drugs like non-steroidal anti-inflammatories can cause nausea, vomiting, and mucosal ulceration.

Common Forms of Oral Medications

Oral medications come in various solid and liquid formulations designed to control release rates and improve palatability.

  • Tablets: Compressed powders that may be scored for splitting or film-coated to mask bitter tastes.
  • Capsules: Gelatin shells enclosing powder, liquid, or granules; enteric-coated versions resist stomach acid and dissolve in the alkaline intestine.
  • Syrups and Elixirs: Concentrated sugar solutions or hydroalcoholic liquids suitable for children or patients unable to swallow solids.
  • Suspensions: Undissolved solid particles dispersed in a liquid medium that require thorough shaking before pouring to ensure an accurate dose.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Preparation

Before giving any oral medication, the nurse must verify the patient's capability to swallow safely. Check for a history of dysphagia, stroke, neurological impairment, or depressed level of consciousness. Review the medication order against the patient's chart, checking for allergies and potential drug-food interactions. When preparing solid doses, pour tablets directly from the container into the medicine cap or cup without touching them with bare hands to maintain aseptic technique. Measure liquid medications on a flat surface at eye level using a calibrated measuring device rather than standard household spoons, which vary widely in volume.

The Rights of Medication Administration

Adhering to the standard medication safety rights prevents clinical errors. While traditionally cited as five rights, modern practice expands this to ensure absolute safety. The core components include the right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Nurses must check the patient's identification band and ask them to state their full name and birth date where possible. Cross-reference the medication label with the prescription chart three times: when retrieving the medication, when preparing it, and just before administration at the bedside.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Oral Medications

Execute the administration process systematically to ensure safety and comfort. Position the patient in an upright sitting position at a 90-degree angle to prevent aspiration. Offer water before giving the medication to moisten the oral cavity. Hand the medicine cup to the patient or place pills directly into their mouth if they use assistive devices. Ensure the patient swallows every tablet or capsule before leaving the bedside; never leave medication unattended on a bedside table for self-administration later unless specifically ordered for self-medication programs.

Special Considerations for Vulnerable Patient Groups

Vulnerable groups such as young children, older adults, and unconscious patients require tailored approaches. Pediatric patients often reject bitter pills, requiring liquid formulations mixed safely with small amounts of jam or juice, avoiding essential food items like milk to prevent lifelong food aversions. Older adults may experience polypharmacy, altered pharmacokinetics, and decreased swallowing efficiency, necessitating smaller pills, crushed alternatives where safe, or oral liquid forms. Never force medications into the mouth of an uncooperative or unconscious patient, as this guarantees aspiration and severe respiratory compromise.

Documentation and Post-Administration Monitoring

Accurate record-keeping completes the medication administration cycle. Document the medication, dose, route, exact time, and your signature immediately after administration, never before. If a patient refuses a drug or vomits shortly after taking it, record the reason and report it to the physician. Monitor the patient for therapeutic responses and adverse drug reactions depending on the medication given, such as checking blood pressure after an antihypertensive or assessing pain levels after an analgesic.
