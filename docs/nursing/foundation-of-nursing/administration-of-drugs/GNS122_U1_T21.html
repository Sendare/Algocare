Dilution of Suspensions

Understanding Suspensions and the Need for Dilution

A suspension is a liquid dosage form containing solid drug particles dispersed throughout a liquid vehicle in which they are not truly soluble. Because the active drug settles over time, uniform distribution relies heavily on physical agitation. In clinical nursing practice, stock suspensions often require dilution to achieve a lower concentration suitable for pediatric administration, fine-tuned geriatric dosing, or administration via enteral feeding tubes that easily clog with thick sediment. Diluting a suspension alters its physical volume without changing the total mass of the active pharmaceutical ingredient already measured out, which requires strict adherence to mathematical and pharmacological principles to prevent underdosing or toxicity.

Mathematical Calculations for Dilution

Accurate calculation prevents medication errors when modifying drug concentrations. The fundamental formula relies on conservation of mass: $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$, where concentration multiplied by volume remains constant before and after adding a diluent.

  1. Identify Stock Strength: Note the concentration of the available drug on the manufacturer label.
  2. Determine Target Dose: Locate the ordered dose and desired final volume.
  3. Compute Diluent Volume: Subtract the original volume of the medication from the total calculated final volume.

Failing to account for the volume occupied by the suspended solid particles can lead to miscalculations in highly concentrated mixtures, though minor discrepancies are clinically negligible in dilute aqueous systems.

Equipment, Reconstitution, and Preparation

Proper preparation demands clean workspace organization and appropriate measuring tools. Household spoons must never be used for measurement because their bowl sizes vary wildly.

  • Measuring Devices: Oral syringes, calibrated medicine cups, or graduated oral dosing pipettes.
  • Diluents: Sterile water, purified water, or specific vehicle diluents recommended by the drug manufacturer.
  • Mixing Vessels: Clean glass beakers or graduated cylinders if compounding outside the original packaging.

Dry powder suspensions require reconstitution before any further dilution takes place. Tap water should be avoided due to potential microbial contamination and mineral interactions with active drug compounds.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Dilution

Executing the physical dilution requires methodical actions to guarantee homogeneity and dosage accuracy.

  1. Perform hand hygiene and verify the medication order against the patient chart.
  2. Invert and shake the original suspension container vigorously to re-suspend any settled cake at the bottom.
  3. Measure the exact required volume of the stock suspension using a calibrated oral syringe.
  4. Transfer the measured suspension into a clean mixing container or secondary bottle.
  5. Measure the prescribed volume of the approved diluent.
  6. Combine the diluent with the suspension, swirling gently to mix thoroughly without introducing excessive air bubbles.
  7. Label the final container with the drug name, new concentration, preparation date, and time.

Safety Protocols, Storage, and Stability

Once a suspension is diluted, its chemical stability and shelf-life often decrease dramatically compared to the dry or concentrated stock form. Many reconstituted or diluted liquid antibiotics degrade rapidly at room temperature and require continuous refrigeration between 2°C and 8°C. Nurses must check manufacturer guidelines regarding expiration windows, which can range from 7 to 14 days post-dilution.

Expired or unstable suspensions may show signs of caking that cannot be redispersed, color changes, or abnormal phase separation. Discard any mixture showing these signs immediately. Aseptic technique remains vital during manipulation to prevent introducing bacterial pathogens that thrive in aqueous drug vehicles.
