Subcutaneous Injection

Introduction and Purpose of Subcutaneous Injections

A subcutaneous (SC) injection is administered into the layer of connective tissue directly beneath the dermis and above the muscle layer. Because adipose tissue has fewer blood vessels than muscle, medication absorption is significantly slower and more sustained compared to intramuscular routes. This steady absorption rate makes the subcutaneous route ideal for drugs that require a prolonged, controlled effect rather than a rapid, immediate action.

Common medications administered subcutaneously include insulin for glycemic control, low molecular weight heparins like enoxaparin for anticoagulation, and certain immunizations. Doses given via this route are typically small, usually ranging between 0.5 mL and 1.5 mL, to avoid sterile abscesses, tissue damage, and severe patient discomfort.

Anatomy and Physiology of Subcutaneous Tissue

The subcutaneous layer, or hypodermis, consists primarily of loose connective tissue and adipose cells. This layer acts as a thermal insulator, stores energy, and provides a cushioning shock absorber for deeper structures.

Nerve endings and small blood vessels course through this layer. When administering an injection, hitting a nerve causes sharp, localized pain, while nicking a blood vessel can lead to localized hematoma formation. The depth of the subcutaneous layer varies widely depending on the patient's nutritional status, age, body habitus, and the specific anatomical site chosen.

Selecting and Assessing Injection Sites

Selecting an appropriate injection site ensures consistent drug absorption and prevents localized tissue trauma. Common sites include:

  • Outer posterior aspect of the upper arms: Accessible, though self-administration can be awkward.
  • Abdomen: Extends from below the costal margins to the iliac crests, staying two inches away from the umbilicus; offers the most rapid and consistent insulin absorption.
  • Anterior thighs: Easily reached by patients for self-injection.
  • Scapular areas of the upper back: Used less frequently, primarily when other sites are unavailable.
  • Ventral and dorsogluteal areas: Alternative posterior sites with adequate fat deposition.

Nurses must inspect chosen sites for bruising, inflammation, edema, scars, or lipohypertrophy. Consistently using the exact same spot leads to hardened lumps that impair medication absorption, making site rotation a mandatory clinical practice.

Equipment and Medication Preparation

Gathering the correct equipment prior to the procedure minimizes patient discomfort and maintains sterile technique.

  1. Syringe: Typically a 1 mL to 3 mL syringe, calibrated precisely if administering small doses like insulin.
  2. Needle: Usually 25 to 27 gauge, with a length of 3/8 to 5/8 inch depending on the patient's size and insertion angle.
  3. Antiseptic swab: Alcohol swab for skin disinfection.
  4. Gloves: Clean non-sterile gloves.
  5. Sharps container: For immediate disposal of used needles without recapping.

When preparing the medication, verify the order against the patient's prescription chart using standard medication safety checks. Ensure no air bubbles compromise the dose accuracy, particularly with insulin syringes.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Performing a subcutaneous injection requires a methodical approach to ensure safety and precision.

StepAction
1Perform hand hygiene and verify the patient identity using two identifiers.
2Clean the selected site with an alcohol swab in a circular motion, moving outward, and let it dry completely.
3Remove the needle cap and pinch or spread the skin fold depending on needle length and patient size.
4Insert the needle quickly at a 45-degree or 90-degree angle with a smooth, dart-like motion.
5Release the skin pinch if it was used, then inject the medication slowly and steadily.
6Withdraw the needle at the same angle it entered, apply gentle pressure with a dry swab, and discard the syringe directly into the sharps box without recapping.

Aspiration is generally no longer recommended for routine subcutaneous injections like insulin or heparin, as it causes unnecessary tissue trauma.

Complications and Post-Injection Monitoring

Although generally safe, subcutaneous injections can lead to localized or systemic complications if technique or site care is flawed.

  • Hematoma: Bleeding under the skin caused by puncturing a small blood vessel; managed by applying firm pressure.
  • Lipodystrophy: Atrophy or hypertrophy of subcutaneous fat resulting from repeated injections in the exact same spot.
  • Infection: Localized redness, warmth, swelling, or purulent discharge indicating poor aseptic technique.
  • Allergic reactions: Hives, rash, or systemic anaphylaxis due to the injected medication.

Nurses must monitor the patient for adverse drug reactions, particularly after administering medications with rapid systemic effects. Document the procedure immediately, noting the exact site used, date, time, and medication details.
