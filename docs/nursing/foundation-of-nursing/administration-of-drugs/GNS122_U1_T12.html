Eye Irrigation

Introduction and Purpose of Eye Irrigation

Eye irrigation is a fundamental emergency and routine nursing procedure involving the continuous flushing of the conjunctival sac with a sterile solution. The primary goal is to wash away chemical splashes, foreign bodies, toxic substances, or excessive discharge that could permanently damage the cornea. Swift and effective irrigation dilutes harmful agents and restores the normal physiological pH of the ocular surface, protecting the patient's vision.

Indications and Contraindications

Nurses must carefully evaluate patients before starting ocular irrigation to ensure it is appropriate and safe.

  • Indications: Splashes of acids, alkalis, or irritating liquids, loose particulate foreign bodies, and copious purulent discharge in severe conjunctivitis.
  • Contraindications: Penetrating globe injuries, severe lacerations of the eyeball, and suspected rupture of the globe. Forcing fluid into a breached globe drives contaminants inside and causes internal eye damage.

Using plain tap water is discouraged unless sterile solutions are completely unavailable during an immediate field emergency, due to the risk of corneal water logging or introducing waterborne microorganisms.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before beginning, assess visual acuity if the patient's condition permits, and inspect the eyelids and surrounding facial skin for burns or embedded debris. Explain every step to reduce anxiety, especially if the patient is panicked or in pain. Position the patient comfortably, either sitting upright or lying supine with the head tilted slightly toward the affected side. This ensures runoff fluid drains away from the unaffected eye and prevents cross-contamination.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Eye Irrigation

  1. Hand hygiene and PPE: Wash hands thoroughly and put on clean gloves and protective eyewear.
  2. Positioning: Place a waterproof towel across the patient's chest and hold a kidney basin firmly against the cheek.
  3. Exposure: Gently retract the eyelids using your thumb and index finger, placing pressure on the bony orbital rim rather than the eyeball itself.
  4. Flushing: Hold the irrigation nozzle or syringe about two to three centimeters from the eye. Direct the stream of solution gently toward the inner canthus, allowing it to flow across the conjunctiva toward the outer canthus.
  5. Duration: Continue flushing for the prescribed time—often 15 to 30 minutes for chemical splashes, or until the conjunctival pH normalizes.

Post-Procedure Care and Evaluation

Once irrigation is complete, gently dry the skin around the eye with sterile gauze pads, moving from the inner corner outward. Remove soiled gloves and perform hand hygiene. Reassess the patient's visual acuity, check the conjunctiva for ongoing irritation, and document the solution used, volume, duration, and patient response. Instruct the patient to report any new pain, blurring, or redness immediately.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although safe when performed correctly, eye irrigation can sometimes lead to complications. Corneal abrasion can occur if the nozzle touches the eye surface or if particulate matter scrapes the epithelium during flushing. Patients may also experience temporary blurred vision, photophobia, or water retention in the conjunctival tissues (chemosis). Nurses must monitor for signs of worsening infection or persistent pain and report unusual findings to the attending physician right away.
