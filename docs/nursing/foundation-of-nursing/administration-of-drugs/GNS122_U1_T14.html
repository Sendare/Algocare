Oral Drug Administration Procedure

Principles and Pre-Administration Assessment

Oral medication administration remains the most common, convenient, and cost-effective method of delivering drugs to patients. Nurses must verify the medical order against the patient chart before touching any medication stock. Patient assessment involves checking the ability to swallow safely, assessing the level of consciousness, and noting any history of gastrointestinal distress or bowel obstructions.

Contraindications for the oral route include severe nausea, vomiting, continuous gastric suctioning, or an absent gag reflex. A common clinical mistake is assuming a fully conscious patient can automatically swallow large tablets safely without checking their actual functional status first.

The Rights of Medication Administration

Safeguarding patients from medication errors relies on strictly adhering to the established safety checks. While traditionally known as five rights, contemporary nursing practice expands these to ensure absolute safety.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using at least two unique identifiers such as name and hospital number.
  • Right Drug: Check the medication label three times against the prescription order.
  • Right Dose: Calculate dosages carefully and double-check high-alert medications with a colleague.
  • Right Time: Administer drugs within the prescribed window to maintain therapeutic blood levels.
  • Right Route: Confirm the delivery method matches the order, ensuring it is safe for oral use.

Dispensing Techniques and Equipment Preparation

Preparing oral medications requires cleanliness and attention to detail. Tablets and capsules are usually dispensed into a medicine cup without touching them directly with bare hands. Unit-dose packaging should remain sealed until the nurse is at the patient's bedside.

Liquid medications require different tools. Use a calibrated oral syringe or medicine cup at eye level to measure doses accurately. Never use a standard household kitchen teaspoon, as variation in spoon sizes leads to incorrect dosing. If crushing tablets is necessary for a patient with dysphagia, verify with a pharmacist that the specific drug formulation allows crushing, as enteric-coated or sustained-release drugs must remain intact.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Executing the drug round smoothly protects both patient and nurse. Position the patient in an upright sitting position at a 90-degree angle to reduce aspiration risks. Offer water to moisten the mouth before giving the pills. Hand the medicine cup to the patient or place pills directly into their mouth if they are physically impaired, ensuring they swallow each pill before leaving.

Never leave medication unattended on a bedside table for the patient to take later. If a patient refuses medication, explore the reason, document the refusal, and report it promptly to the attending physician or nurse in charge.

Special Populations and Clinical Challenges

Administering oral medications to pediatric and geriatric patients requires specialized techniques. Children often accept liquid formulations better when mixed with small amounts of non-essential food, though nurses must avoid mixing drugs into staple foods like milk in case the child refuses future meals. Use flavored syrups where appropriate and always use oral syringes.

Older adults frequently experience polypharmacy and altered drug metabolism alongside age-related dysphagia. Crushing medications, providing ample fluids, and allowing extra time for swallowing ensures safe passage of drugs in geriatric care.

Post-Administration Monitoring and Documentation

The medication process does not end when the patient swallows the pill. Nurses must observe the patient for therapeutic effects and potential adverse drug reactions, returning later to check vital signs or symptom relief depending on the drug given.

Documentation must occur immediately after administration, never before. Record the drug name, dose, exact time, and route on the medication administration record. Document any withheld doses along with the clinical justification in the nursing notes.
