Intravenous Injection (IV)

Introduction and Indications for IV Therapy

Intravenous therapy involves the direct administration of fluids, medications, and blood products into a vein. This route offers the fastest systemic absorption, bypassing the digestive tract and first-pass metabolism in the liver. Nurses utilize IV access in various clinical scenarios, including severe dehydration, emergency resuscitation, administration of vesicant drugs, and total parenteral nutrition. Contraindications include localized infections, severe burns at the insertion site, or damaged peripheral vasculature in the chosen extremity.

Equipment and Supplies Preparation

  • IV Cannula: Selected based on gauge size and patient age or vein caliber.
  • Tourniquet: Applied to distend veins by obstructing venous return.
  • Antiseptic Swab: Chlorhexidine or alcohol used to clean the skin in a friction scrub.
  • Transparent Dressing: Secures the catheter and allows continuous site inspection.
  • Extension Set or Infusion Tubing: Connects the cannula to the fluid source.

Anatomy and Selection of Peripheral Veins

Successful cannulation relies on choosing an appropriate superficial vein on the upper extremities. Common sites include the metacarpal veins on the back of the hand, the cephalic and basilic veins along the forearm, and the median cubital vein in the antecubital fossa. Nurses should always begin with the most distal veins of the upper limb, preserving proximal sites for future access if complications arise. Sclerosed, bruised, or surgically compromised limbs must be avoided.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

  1. Assess and Prepare: Verify the order, explain the procedure to the patient, and gather supplies.
  2. Positioning: Place the patient's arm in a dependent position to encourage venous filling.
  3. Skin Antisepsis: Clean the site with an antiseptic using a friction motion and allow it to dry completely.
  4. Vascular Entry: Insert the needle with the bevel facing up at a 15 to 30-degree angle until blood flash is observed.
  5. Advancement: Lower the angle and advance the catheter slightly into the vein while withdrawing the stylet.
  6. Stabilization: Release the tourniquet, connect the tubing or saline lock, and secure the device with a transparent dressing.

Monitoring and Maintenance of IV Sites

Continuous assessment of the infusion site prevents severe local tissue damage. Nurses inspect the insertion site during every shift change or medication administration, checking for pain, redness, swelling, or coolness. Infusion tubing and intermittent locks must be changed according to institutional infection control guidelines. Patency is verified by flushing with normal saline before and after medication administration.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Management

  • Infiltration: Fluid enters surrounding subcutaneous tissue due to catheter displacement; characterized by swelling and coolness.
  • Extravasation: Infiltration involving a vesicant drug, leading to severe tissue necrosis and blistering.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by mechanical, chemical, or bacterial irritation, marked by a streak of redness and tenderness.
  • Air Embolism: Air enters the circulatory system through loose tubing connections, causing respiratory distress and cardiovascular collapse.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Access

Discontinuation occurs when therapy is complete, complications arise, or the site is no longer required. The nurse closes the roller clamp, gently removes the tape and transparent dressing, and withdraws the cannula parallel to the skin. Pressure is applied immediately using sterile gauze until bleeding stops. The nurse inspects the removed catheter to ensure the tip is intact, preventing retained catheter fragments in circulation.

Documentation and Legal Responsibilities

Accurate charting protects both the patient and the healthcare provider. Nurses must record the date and time of insertion, catheter gauge, anatomical site, type of fluid or medication, number of attempts, and the patient's tolerance. Any complications and subsequent nursing interventions require detailed logging in the patient record. Failure to document accurately can lead to legal liability for negligence.
