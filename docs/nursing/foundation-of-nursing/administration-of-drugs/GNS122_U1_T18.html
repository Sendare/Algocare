Administration of Blood

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a life-saving intervention that involves transferring whole blood or blood components from a donor to a recipient. The primary goal is to restore intravascular volume, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, and correct coagulation defects. Strict adherence to safety protocols is mandatory because human blood is a biological drug carrying risks of severe immunological and infectious complications. Every unit must be treated with absolute care from collection to administration.

Blood Types, Crossmatching, and Compatibility

The ABO and Rhesus (Rh) blood group systems dictate compatibility between donor and recipient. Antigens on red blood cells and antibodies in the plasma determine whether blood mixes safely or triggers agglutination and destruction.

  • Type O negative: Considered the universal donor for red blood cells because it lacks A, B, and Rh antigens.
  • Type AB positive: Considered the universal recipient because plasma lacks antibodies against A, B, and Rh antigens.
  • Crossmatching: Laboratory testing that mixes donor red blood cells with recipient serum to detect potential adverse immune responses before transfusion.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Before any blood product is released from the bank, baseline observations must be recorded. Nurses check baseline vital signs including temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure to compare against any changes during the procedure. Consent must be obtained and documented, ensuring the patient understands the risks and benefits. Two qualified healthcare professionals must independently verify patient identity identifiers against the blood bag label at the bedside.

Equipment, IV Access, and Blood Administration Sets

Administering blood requires specialized equipment to prevent hemolysis and infection. Nurses must use a dedicated, Y-type blood administration set containing an integral mesh filter to trap clots and cellular debris. The intravenous cannula must be large enough, typically an 18-gauge or 20-gauge needle, to prevent mechanical damage to red blood cells. Normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) is the only intravenous fluid compatible with blood products; dextrose or Ringer's lactate causes hemolysis or clotting.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Blood

The transfusion process demands meticulous procedural control. Begin by establishing secure IV access and priming the Y-tubing with normal saline. Spike the blood bag and keep the saline side of the tubing closed while blood flows through the filter. Start the infusion slowly for the first fifteen minutes at a rate around 50 milliliters per hour, remaining at the bedside to watch for acute reactions. Increase the flow rate to the prescribed level once the patient tolerates the initial phase, ensuring the entire unit finishes within a maximum of four hours to prevent bacterial proliferation.

Patient Monitoring and Observation during Transfusion

Continuous vigilance safeguards the recipient throughout the procedure. Nurses must monitor vital signs at regular intervals: before starting, fifteen minutes after initiation, hourly until completion, and once more after the transfusion finishes. The patient should be instructed to immediately report any new symptoms such as chills, chest tightness, lower back pain, itching, or shortness of breath. Quick identification of subtle changes prevents minor reactions from escalating into life-threatening emergencies.

Recognition and Management of Transfusion Reactions

Transfusion reactions range from mild allergic responses to catastrophic hemolytic events.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by ABO incompatibility, presenting with fever, chest pain, and hypotension.
  • Allergic Reaction: Manifests as urticaria, itching, and flushing, often managed with antihistamines.
  • Circulatory Overload (TACO): Results from infusing blood too rapidly, leading to dyspnea, hypertension, and crackles in the lungs.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: Characterized by isolated temperature spikes without hemolysis.

Management protocol dictates stopping the transfusion instantly, disconnecting the blood tubing, infusing fresh normal saline through a new line, notifying the physician and blood bank, and returning the blood bag for investigation.

Post-Transfusion Care and Documentation

Once the blood unit finishes, post-procedure management ensures patient stability and accurate record-keeping. Flush the administration line with normal saline, clamp it, and dispose of the blood bag according to hospital infection control policies. Record the final set of vital signs and compare them against baseline readings. Document the volume infused, start and stop times, product serial numbers, and any adverse reactions observed in the patient chart.
