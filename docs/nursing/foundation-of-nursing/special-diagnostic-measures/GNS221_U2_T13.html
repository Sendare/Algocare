Barium Swallow

Introduction and Purpose of Barium Swallow

A barium swallow, also known as an esophagogram, is a specialized fluoroscopic imaging procedure used to examine the pharynx, esophagus, and proximal stomach. Patients ingest a suspension containing barium sulfate, a radiopaque contrast medium that coats the mucosal lining of the upper gastrointestinal tract. Because soft tissues absorb X-rays similarly, standard radiography fails to visualize the lumen clearly. Barium provides high-density contrast, highlighting anatomical contours, strictures, motility disorders, and mucosal irregularities during real-time fluoroscopy.

Indications and Contraindications

Clinicians order a barium swallow to investigate symptoms like dysphagia, odynophagia, unexplained weight loss, persistent heartburn, and suspected structural abnormalities. Common diagnostic targets include hiatal hernias, esophageal strictures, diverticula, achalasia, and malignancy.

Contraindications demand careful pre-procedure screening:

  • Suspected perforation: Barium leaking into the mediastinum or peritoneal cavity causes severe inflammation and granuloma formation; water-soluble contrast like gastrografin is used instead.
  • High risk of aspiration: If a patient cannot protect their airway, barium entering the lungs can cause severe pneumonitis or pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Complete bowel obstruction: Barium can solidify and worsen an impaction downstream.

Patient Preparation and Pre-Procedure Care

Adequate preparation ensures a diagnostic study free from residual food or secretions that mimic pathology. Nurses must enforce strict fasting instructions, typically requiring the patient to remain nil per os (NPO) for six to eight hours before the examination. This clears the upper gastrointestinal tract and prevents aspiration during fluoroscopy.

Review the patient's medical history for previous contrast reactions, current medications, and pregnancy status in women of childbearing age. Explain the procedure thoroughly to alleviate anxiety, ensuring the patient understands they will need to drink chalky liquid and change positions frequently on the X-ray table.

Procedure Technique and Execution

During the examination, the patient stands or lies on the fluoroscopy table while consuming barium preparations of varying consistencies, sometimes paired with effervescent granules to create a double-contrast view. The radiologist observes the movement of the barium column in real-time.

  1. Initial swallows: Thin barium evaluates mucosal detail and primary peristalsis.
  2. Thick barium or paste: Evaluates motility disorders and structural narrowing.
  3. Position changes: The table tilts, and the patient turns into various oblique angles to capture profiles of the esophagus and stomach from multiple perspectives.

Common Pathological Findings

Fluoroscopic analysis reveals various structural and functional gastrointestinal disorders. An esophageal stricture appears as a localized narrowing of the lumen, often linked to chronic reflux. Achalasia shows as a dilated esophagus tapering into a characteristic 'bird-beak' appearance at the lower esophageal sphincter due to failure of relaxation. Hiatal hernias demonstrate a portion of the stomach sliding above the diaphragmatic hiatus. Other findings include diverticula, esophageal varices appearing as worm-like filling defects, and irregular mucosal destruction indicative of malignancy.

Post-Procedure Nursing Care and Complication Management

After the procedure, nursing interventions focus on eliminating retained barium and monitoring for adverse effects. Barium sulfate has a strong tendency to cause constipation as it absorbs water in the colon.

  • Encourage high fluid intake unless clinically contraindicated.
  • Administer prescribed mild laxatives or stool softeners to assist evacuation.
  • Warn the patient that stools will appear chalky white or light gray for 24 to 72 hours.
  • Monitor for signs of bowel obstruction, severe abdominal pain, or failure to pass flatus.
