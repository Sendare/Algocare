Laparoscopy

Introduction to Laparoscopy and Minimally Invasive Surgery

Laparoscopy, frequently called keyhole surgery, represents a major shift from traditional open procedures. Surgeons access the abdominal and pelvic cavities through tiny incisions, typically measuring between 0.5 to 1.5 centimeters. A specialized tube called a trocar creates an entry point, allowing the insertion of a laparoscope—a fiber-optic camera connected to a high-definition light source and video monitor.

To visualize internal organs clearly, the peritoneal cavity must be distended. Medical-grade carbon dioxide gas is insufflated into the abdomen to create a working space between the abdominal wall and the viscera. Minimally invasive techniques offer distinct advantages over laparotomy, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, quicker return to normal daily activities, and significantly smaller scars. In resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, these benefits help reduce bed occupancy rates and lower the risk of superficial surgical site infections.

Indications and Diagnostic Uses

Laparoscopy serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in modern surgical practice. Clinicians utilize diagnostic laparoscopy to investigate chronic pelvic pain, unexplained infertility, undescended testes, and acute abdominal pain of uncertain origin when non-invasive imaging remains inconclusive.

  • Therapeutic interventions: Appendectomy, cholecystectomy, hernia repair, ectopic pregnancy management, and adhesiolysis.
  • Biopsy collection: Direct visualization allows targeted tissue sampling from the liver, lymph nodes, or peritoneal masses.
  • Staging procedures: Evaluation of gastrointestinal and gynecological malignancies to determine the extent of disease before major resection.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

While laparoscopy offers numerous benefits, certain patient conditions make the approach unsafe. Absolute contraindications include uncorrectable coagulopathies, severe hemodynamic instability, and inability to tolerate general anesthesia. Relative contraindications involve generalized peritonitis, massive bowel distension, multiple previous open abdominal surgeries with suspected severe adhesions, and late-stage pregnancy.

A thorough preoperative risk assessment helps identify patients who require conversion from laparoscopy to an open laparotomy. Nurses must review baseline laboratory results, particularly clotting profiles and hemoglobin levels, to flag potential bleeding risks before the patient enters the operating theatre.

Pre-Operative Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive pre-operative preparation reduces surgical risks and eases patient anxiety. Nurses conduct a thorough baseline assessment, verifying informed consent, checking allergy status, and confirming baseline vital signs.

Bowel preparation may be ordered depending on the specific procedure to reduce the risk of fecal contamination if bowel perforation occurs. Skin preparation involves cleaning the abdomen and groin, avoiding harsh shaving that creates micro-abrasions. Nurses also ensure the patient voids before transfer to the theatre, reducing bladder injury risks during trocar placement. Preoperative education focuses on explaining expected postoperative sensations, such as shoulder tip pain caused by residual carbon dioxide irritating the diaphragm.

Intra-Operative Nursing Care and Equipment Management

Intra-operative nursing in laparoscopic surgery requires meticulous management of specialized equipment and patient positioning. Scrub and circulating nurses must verify the functional integrity of insufflators, light sources, camera systems, and electrosurgical units before the procedure begins.

Patient positioning is critical. Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg positions are frequently used to displace abdominal viscera away from the surgical field using gravity. Nurses must secure the patient properly with straps and padding to prevent nerve damage and slippage during these extreme tilts. Strict maintenance of the sterile field and accurate instrument and sponge counts remain essential nursing responsibilities throughout the procedure.

Post-Operative Nursing Care and Recovery Monitoring

Postoperative monitoring focuses on detecting early complications and managing predictable discomforts. Nurses monitor vital signs regularly, watching for hypotension or tachycardia that might signal internal hemorrhage. Assessment of the surgical incisions includes checking for excessive bleeding, subcutaneous emphysema, or spreading erythema.

Pain management is a priority. While laparoscopic incisions cause less pain than open surgery, visceral pain from gas distension and incisional discomfort require careful analgesia administration. Early ambulation is encouraged as soon as the patient is fully recovered from anesthesia to promote bowel motility, prevent venous stasis, and assist in expelling residual carbon dioxide gas.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Although laparoscopy is generally safe, serious complications can occur unexpectedly.

  • Vascular injury: Major blood vessels like the aorta or vena cava can be punctured during initial Veress needle or trocar insertion, causing rapid hemorrhagic shock.
  • Visceral perforation: Thermal or mechanical injury to the bowel or bladder may lead to peritonitis, presenting days later with fever and severe abdominal rigidity.
  • Gas embolism: Inadvertent entry of carbon dioxide into a blood vessel causes sudden cardiovascular collapse.
  • Port-site hernias: Bowel can protrude through larger trocar sites if fascial closure is inadequate.

Patient Discharge Education and Home Care

Effective discharge education ensures safe recovery at home and reduces unnecessary hospital readmissions. Nurses instruct patients on proper wound care, advising them to keep incision sites clean and dry, and when dressings can be removed.

Patients receive guidance on activity restrictions, including avoiding heavy lifting and strenuous exercise for a specified period. Dietary progression starts with light foods as tolerated, advancing to normal diets as bowel function normalizes. Crucially, nurses provide clear red-flag instructions. Patients must contact the clinic or return immediately if they experience persistent fever, worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, pus draining from incisions, or inability to pass urine.
