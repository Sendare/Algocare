Underwater Seal Drainage

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pleural Space

The pleural space is a thin, fluid-filled cavity located between the visceral pleura, which covers the lungs, and the parietal pleura, which lines the chest wall. Under normal conditions, this space contains only a few milliliters of serous fluid that acts as a lubricant during respiration.

Crucially, the pressure within the pleural space is negative compared to atmospheric pressure. This negative intrapleural pressure acts like a vacuum, keeping the lungs expanded against the chest wall. When air, blood, or excess fluid enters this space, the delicate pressure balance is disrupted. The accumulating substance takes up room, causing the lung to collapse. Chest tube insertion and underwater seal drainage aim to remove these unwanted substances and restore negative pressure.

Indications for Chest Tube Insertion

Chest tubes are inserted into the pleural cavity to drain air, blood, or fluid, thereby re-establishing negative intrapleural pressure. Common clinical conditions requiring this intervention include:

  • Pneumothorax: Accumulation of air in the pleural space, either spontaneous or traumatic.
  • Hemothorax: Presence of blood in the pleural space, commonly following chest trauma or cardiothoracic surgery.
  • Pleural Effusion: Abnormal collection of fluid, which can be serous, purulent (empyema), or chylous.
  • Tension Pneumothorax: Life-threatening buildup of air shifting mediastinal structures, requiring immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube insertion.

Components and Mechanism of the Drainage System

Traditional underwater seal systems utilize a three-chamber setup to manage pleural drainage safely. Modern disposable units replicate this physics in a compact plastic box.

ChamberPrimary FunctionExpected Behavior
Collection ChamberCollects fluid and air from the patientFluid level rises and is measured over time
Water Seal ChamberActs as a one-way valve allowing air out but not back inTidaling occurs with respiration; bubbling indicates air leak
Suction Control ChamberRegulates the amount of negative suction appliedContinuous gentle bubbling indicates proper suction regulation

The water seal chamber is the safety heart of the system. The tip of the drainage straw sits submerged under 2 centimeters of sterile water. As the patient breathes, water levels fluctuate, a phenomenon known as tidaling.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous evaluation of both the patient and the drainage equipment prevents catastrophic complications. The nurse must perform routine checks at the bedside every shift.

  • Assess respiratory rate, depth, effort, and oxygen saturation regularly.
  • Inspect the insertion site for subcutaneous emphysema, swelling, redness, or excessive drainage.
  • Ensure all tubing connections are securely taped or banded to prevent accidental disconnection.
  • Monitor drainage output volume, color, and consistency, marking the fluid level on the collection chamber column at the end of every shift.
  • Verify that the drainage system remains upright and positioned well below the level of the patient's chest at all times.

Patient Management and Care Maintenance

Encouraging mobility and pulmonary hygiene accelerates recovery for a patient with a chest tube. Pain management is a priority, as discomfort prevents deep breathing and coughing.

  • Administer prescribed analgesics before turning, repositioning, or engaging in physiotherapy.
  • Assist the patient to ambulate early if stable, keeping the drainage unit upright and safely carried.
  • Encourage frequent incentive spirometry and deep breathing exercises to promote lung expansion.
  • Never routinely clamp a chest tube during transport or ambulation, as clamping can rapidly cause a tension pneumothorax by trapping accumulating air.

Complications and Emergency Management

Despite careful monitoring, emergencies can occur with underwater seal systems. Quick action preserves patient safety and prevents life-threatening deterioration.

  • Tube Disconnection: If the tube pulls out from the system, submerge the distal end immediately in a bottle of sterile water to maintain a water seal while preparing a new system.
  • Accidental Removal from Patient: If the tube slips out of the chest, cover the wound immediately with a sterile petroleum jelly gauze taped on three sides to allow air to escape while preventing further air entry.
  • Sudden Respiratory Distress: Check for kinks, check for accidental clamping, assess for tension pneumothorax signs, and notify the physician immediately.

Chest Tube Removal Procedure

Chest tubes are removed when the lung has fully re-expanded, air leaks have ceased, and drainage has diminished to minimal amounts, usually less than 100 to 200 milliliters per day.

The removal procedure requires preparation and patient education. The nurse gathers suture removal kits, petroleum gauze, and occlusive dressings. Analgesics are administered roughly thirty minutes prior.

During removal, the clinician instructs the patient to take a deep breath, hold it, and bear down, a maneuver known as the Valsalva maneuver. This increases intrathoracic pressure and prevents air from entering the pleural space as the tube is pulled out. The site is immediately covered with an airtight dressing to seal the wound.
