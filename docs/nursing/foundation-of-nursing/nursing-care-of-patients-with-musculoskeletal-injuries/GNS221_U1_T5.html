Plaster of Paris (POP)

Introduction and Properties of Plaster of Paris

Plaster of Paris (POP) is a calcined gypsum product, specifically calcium sulfate hemihydrate, manufactured by heating gypsum to about 150 degrees Celsius. When mixed with water, it undergoes an exothermic hydration reaction, reverting back to calcium sulfate dihydrate and hardening into a rigid immobilizing structure.

  • Exothermic reaction: Generates heat during setting, which nurses must monitor to prevent skin thermal injury.
  • Setting time: Typically ranges from 10 to 18 minutes, allowing adequate time for molding.
  • Curing time: Complete drying and maximum strength take up to 24 to 72 hours.

Purposes and Clinical Indications

Healthcare providers apply Plaster of Paris casts primarily to immobilize fractured bones, maintain joint alignment, and support healing tissues. Beyond acute fractures, POP finds use in correcting congenital deformities like clubfoot in infants, managing severe soft tissue sprains, and resting inflamed joints or tendon repairs.

Rigid immobilization ensures that bone ends remain in close apposition, promoting callus formation and preventing malunion. Because POP conforms closely to body contours, it provides superior stabilization compared to many prefabricated splints, making it invaluable in busy orthopedic wards across Nigerian hospitals.

Equipment and Materials Setup

Successful cast application requires organized preparation of specialized materials to avoid delays while the plaster sets.

  • Stockinette: Tubular cotton material placed directly against the skin to protect it from abrasive plaster.
  • Cast padding (wool): Layers wrapped over the stockinette, paying special attention to bony prominences.
  • POP bandages: Plaster-impregnated rolls kept dry until the moment of application.
  • Water basin: Filled with tepid water; hot water accelerates setting dangerously, while cold water delays it.
  • Rubber gloves and apron: Protect personnel from the messy wet plaster.

Step-by-Step Application Procedure

Applying a POP cast is a coordinated task requiring an orthopedic surgeon or skilled officer to position the limb while the nurse assists with materials.

  1. Skin preparation: Clean and dry the affected limb, covering any minor wounds with sterile dressings.
  2. Padding application: Roll the stockinette and apply cast padding smoothly without wrinkles, overlapping by half.
  3. Soaking the bandage: Submerge the POP roll horizontally in water until bubbling stops, gently squeeze excess water from the ends, and hand it to the applier.
  4. Wrapping and molding: Apply the bandages in a spiral motion with 50% overlap. Rub and mold the layers together to form a cohesive shell without creating indentations with fingertips.

Post-Application Nursing Care

Immediately after cast application, the priority shifts to drying the cast safely and monitoring distal neurovascular status.

  • Drying technique: Leave the cast uncovered and exposed to circulating air. Support damp casts on palm pillows rather than hard surfaces to prevent flat spots.
  • Neurovascular assessment: Check the five Ps—pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis—every 30 minutes for the first two hours, then regularly.
  • Capillary refill: Press the nail beds of the affected digits; color should return within two seconds.

Recognizing Complications

Complications from POP casts can arise rapidly and threaten limb viability if staff fail to detect them early.

  • Compartment syndrome: Increased pressure within a fascial compartment restricts blood flow. Severe, unremitting pain that worsens with passive movement is the hallmark warning sign.
  • Pressure sores: Caused by rough cast edges or internal plaster ridges pressing against bony prominences.
  • Skin maceration: Occurs if moisture gets trapped inside the padding or if the limb is casted while wet underneath.
  • Joint stiffness and muscle atrophy: Long-term immobilization consequences managed later with physiotherapy.

Patient Education and Home Care

Educating patients and their families ensures safe home recovery and prevents avoidable cast damage. Instruct patients to keep the cast dry at all times, as water weakens plaster and causes skin breakdown beneath. Warn them never to insert foreign objects like sticks or hangers down the cast to scratch itchy skin, as this easily tears the skin and introduces bacteria leading to infection.

Advise elevating the limb above heart level during the first 48 hours to minimize swelling. Emphasize returning to the clinic immediately if they notice foul odors, numbness, discoloration, or sudden severe tightness.

Cast Removal Procedure

When bone healing is confirmed radiologically, the healthcare team removes the Plaster of Paris cast using an electric cast cutter or manual shears. The electric cast cutter features an oscillating blade that vibrates back and forth rather than spinning in a circle; this cuts the hard, brittle plaster safely without slicing soft skin that yields to the vibration.

Nurses should reassure the patient that the loud buzzing noise is normal and harmless. After removal, the limb often appears dry, scaly, and atrophied. The nurse washes the skin gently with warm water and mild soap, advises against forceful scrubbing of dead skin flakes, and encourages gradual joint mobilization.
