Skull Traction

Introduction and Definition of Skull Traction

Skull traction is a specialized orthopedic method used to align, stabilize, and reduce fractures or dislocations of the cervical spine. By applying a direct pulling force to the skull using metal pins or tongs, this technique immobilizes the neck while allowing the weight of the body or external weights to counteract muscle spasms and restore proper spinal alignment. It serves as a crucial bridge between acute injury and definitive surgical stabilization, protecting the delicate spinal cord from further mechanical damage during the healing process.

Indications and Clinical Purposes

  • Cervical Fractures: Stabilizing broken vertebrae caused by motor vehicle accidents or falls.
  • Spinal Dislocations: Reducing displaced neck bones back into their normal anatomical position.
  • Pre-operative Realignment: Straightening the spine prior to definitive surgical fusion.
  • Severe Muscle Spasms: Overcoming powerful cervical muscle contractions that resist standard immobilization collars.

Types of Skull Traction Devices

Several devices apply skeletal traction, each utilizing distinct mechanisms to secure the skull.

  • Gardner-Wells Tongs: Features spring-loaded pins that are tightened to a specific pressure, making it the most commonly used device.
  • Crutchfield Tongs: Requires holes to be drilled slightly into the outer skull table and locked with a threaded bolt.
  • Halo Vest Systems: Consists of a circular metal ring fixed to the skull with four pins, connected to a jacket worn around the torso for early ambulation.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Preparation and Patient Education

Before device placement, the nurse must prepare both the patient and the environment. Explain the procedure simply, noting that local anesthesia will numb the pin insertion sites. Obtain written informed consent, baseline neurological assessments, and vital signs. Shave the scalp area if ordered to reduce infection risks. Ensure suction equipment and bag-valve-mask ventilation are readily available at the bedside in case respiratory distress occurs during positioning.

The Application Procedure and Nursing Assistance

During application by the medical doctor, the nurse supports the patient's head in neutral alignment to prevent sudden neck movement. The physician injects a local anesthetic into the scalp areas where the pins will rest. The device is secured to the skull, and weights are gently attached to a cord running through a pulley. The nurse must ensure weights hang freely at all times and never rest on the floor or the bed frame, which would instantly remove the traction force.

Ongoing Nursing Assessment and Traction Maintenance

Continuous monitoring prevents secondary complications. Perform neurovascular checks on all extremities every two to four hours, assessing movement, sensation, and capillary refill. Inspect the ropes, pulleys, and weights frequently to ensure they remain aligned and unobstructed. Check the patient's skin for pressure breakdown, particularly around the occiput and ears. Never alter, remove, or lift the traction weights without a direct medical order.

Pin Site Care and Prevention of Infection

Skull pins create a direct pathway for bacteria from the skin into the bone, making hygiene critical. Perform pin site care according to hospital protocol, typically using normal saline or chlorhexidine solutions to remove crusting. Inspect the sites daily for signs of infection such as purulent drainage, redness, warmth, or loosening pins. Never remove crusts that adhere tightly around the pin entry point, as they may act as a natural protective seal.

Complications and Emergency Interventions

Patients with skull traction face risks ranging from local skin breakdown to life-threatening respiratory failure. Pin loosening or detachment can cause a sudden loss of spinal alignment. If a pin slips or the patient experiences severe respiratory distress, summon help immediately, manually stabilize the head and neck in neutral alignment, and prepare for emergency airway management. Document all interventions and unusual symptoms thoroughly.
