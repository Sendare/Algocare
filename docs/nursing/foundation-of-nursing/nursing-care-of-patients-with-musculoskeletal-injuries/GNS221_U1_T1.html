Maintaining Muscular Function

Introduction to Muscular Function and Musculoskeletal Health

Skeletal muscle tissue makes up a large portion of body weight and depends on constant neural stimulation, adequate blood supply, and proper nutrition to maintain structure and strength. Healthy muscles allow patients to move independently, maintain posture, and perform activities of daily living without undue fatigue.

Normal muscle function relies on the sliding filament theory, where actin and myosin filaments slide past each other during contraction. This process requires calcium ions, adenosine triphosphate, and intact nerve impulses from motor neurons. When any part of this neuromuscular pathway fails, muscle weakness or paralysis quickly follows.

Pathophysiology of Muscle Atrophy and Immobility

When a muscle remains unused due to bed rest, casting, or paralysis, protein synthesis decreases while protein breakdown accelerates. This imbalance leads directly to muscle atrophy, characterized by a visible reduction in muscle fiber size and overall mass.

Prolonged immobility also alters muscle metabolism, shifting fiber types and reducing oxidative capacity. Over time, connective tissue and fat replace functional muscle fibers, leading to permanent shortening of periarticular tissues known as a contracture.

Assessment of Muscle Strength, Tone, and Joint Mobility

Nursing evaluation of the musculoskeletal system involves inspecting muscle symmetry, palpating resting tone, and testing active and passive range of motion. Nurses grade muscle strength using the standardized Medical Research Council scale ranging from zero to five.

  • Grade 0: No visible or palpable muscle contraction.
  • Grade 1: Trace contraction with visible flicker but no joint movement.
  • Grade 2: Movement possible only with gravity eliminated.
  • Grade 3: Movement possible against gravity alone, but not against resistance.
  • Grade 4: Movement possible against gravity and some resistance.
  • Grade 5: Normal strength against full resistance.

Range of Motion (ROM) Exercises

Range of motion exercises maintain joint flexibility and prevent the shortening of ligaments and tendons.

  • Active ROM: The patient independently moves each joint through its full available plane.
  • Active-Assistive ROM: The patient moves the joint with partial physical or mechanical help from the nurse.
  • Passive ROM: The nurse gently moves each joint through its complete motion while the patient remains completely relaxed.

During passive exercises, the nurse supports the limb above and below the joint, moving it slowly and stopping immediately if the patient reports pain or encounters firm resistance.

Nutritional Support for Muscle Maintenance

Muscle tissue requires adequate building blocks and energy to repair microscopic damage and prevent catabolic wasting. High-quality protein intake provides essential amino acids needed for muscle protein synthesis.

Micronutrients such as vitamin D and calcium are equally vital for neuromuscular function and bone integrity. Nurses collaborate with dietitians to ensure patients on prolonged bed rest receive balanced caloric intake, adequate hydration, and sufficient protein to support ongoing tissue preservation.

Preventing Complications of Immobility

Immobility affects multiple body systems beyond the musculoskeletal system, increasing risks for pressure injuries, deep vein thrombosis, and respiratory atelectasis.

Turning immobile patients every two hours, encouraging deep breathing exercises, and applying compression stockings are standard nursing interventions. Maintaining proper body alignment with pillows and footboards prevents foot drop and abnormal joint positioning during extended bed rest.
