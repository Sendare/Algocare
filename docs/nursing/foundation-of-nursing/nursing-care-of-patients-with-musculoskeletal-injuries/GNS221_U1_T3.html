Passive Range of Motion Exercise

Introduction and Definition of Passive Range of Motion

Passive Range of Motion, commonly abbreviated as PROM, refers to the movement of a joint through its full available range by an external force, without any active exertion from the patient. The nurse or physical therapist provides all the energy to move the limb. The patient remains completely relaxed, allowing the muscles surrounding the joint to rest entirely. This intervention is distinct from active or active-assistive exercises where the patient performs part or all of the movement independently. Understanding this clear boundary helps student nurses prevent accidental exertion by weak or heavily sedated patients.

Purposes and Clinical Indications for PROM Exercises

PROM is prescribed when a patient cannot or should not move a body part independently. The primary goals center on maintaining joint mobility and preventing secondary complications of immobility.

  • Contracture prevention: Regular movement stops soft tissues from shortening and tightening.
  • Circulation enhancement: Moving the limbs helps stimulate local blood flow in bedridden patients.
  • Cartilage nutrition: Synovial fluid circulates within the joint capsule during movement, nourishing the articular cartilage.
  • Sensory awareness: Movement helps maintain the patient's mental awareness of joint position.

Contraindications and Precautions in Nigerian Clinical Practice

While PROM is beneficial, performing it incorrectly can cause severe harm, especially in busy hospital settings with high patient-to-nurse ratios. Nurses must screen patients carefully before starting.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Do not perform PROM when a joint dislocation, an unstable fracture, or an acute surgical tear of a tendon or ligament is present.
  • Precautions: Exercise extreme caution in patients with severe osteoporosis, recent myocardial infarction, or deep vein thrombosis.
  • Pain rule: Movement should never be forced past the point of resistance or acute pain.

General Principles and Guidelines for Safe Execution

Safe execution of PROM protects both the patient from injury and the nurse from ergonomic strain. Proper body mechanics are paramount in Nigerian wards where adjustable beds may not always be available.

  • Support the limb proximally and distally to stabilize the joint being exercised.
  • Expose only the specific limb being worked on to maintain patient privacy and dignity.
  • Move each joint smoothly, slowly, and rhythmically through its normal anatomical plane.
  • Perform each movement gently, typically repeating it five to ten times per session.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Upper and Lower Extremity PROM

Executing PROM requires methodical progression through major anatomical joints, starting from the head or upper extremities and moving downward, or vice versa. For upper limbs, cradle the elbow and wrist to perform flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and circular rotations of the shoulder, followed by elbow bending and wrist movements. For lower limbs, cradle the heel and knee to perform hip and knee flexion and extension, ankle dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, and toe rotations. Always respect the anatomical limits of every joint, stopping well before any sign of strain.

Patient Assessment, Comfort, and Monitoring During the Exercise

Continuous observation of the patient is critical throughout the exercise session. The nurse must monitor facial expressions, verbal feedback, and physiological vital signs for signs of distress. If a patient grimaces, breathes heavily, or complains of discomfort, the exercise must stop immediately. In settings where language barriers exist between nurses and patients from diverse ethnic backgrounds in Nigeria, non-verbal cues such as sudden sweating, muscle guarding, or restlessness serve as vital warning indicators.

Documentation, Reporting, and Complications Management

Accurate record-keeping ensures continuity of care among multidisciplinary healthcare teams. After completing PROM, the nurse records the specific joints exercised, the degree of movement tolerance, and any patient complaints. Any abnormal findings, such as sudden swelling, redness, joint clicking, or persistent pain, must be reported immediately to the attending physician or physiotherapist. Proper documentation also protects nursing staff by proving that standard safety measures were maintained during care.
