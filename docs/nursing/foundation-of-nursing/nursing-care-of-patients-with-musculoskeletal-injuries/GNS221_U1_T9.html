Skeletal Traction

Introduction to Skeletal Traction and Its Indications

Skeletal traction is an orthopedic method that applies a pulling force directly to bone using pins, wires, or tongs. This direct application allows for heavier weights and longer treatment durations compared to skin traction. Orthopedic surgeons primarily use it to reduce fractures, correct deformities, and relieve muscle spasms before definitive surgical fixation or healing occurs.

Common clinical indications include:

  • Femoral shaft fractures: Stabilizing long bone breaks in adults before internal fixation.
  • Cervical spine injuries: Using cranial tongs like Gardner-Wells to realign vertebrae and decompress the spinal cord.
  • Pelvic ring disruptions: Restoring anatomical alignment in complex trauma cases.

A common misconception in the ward is that skeletal traction provides absolute immobilization. In reality, it maintains a dynamic pull that permits limited bed mobility while keeping the bone ends aligned.

Equipment Setup and Mechanics

The mechanical setup of skeletal traction relies on physics principles to maintain a constant, balanced pull. The system typically consists of Steinmann pins or Kirschner wires drilled through the bone, attached to a metal bow or caliper, which then connects to ropes, pulleys, and hanging weights.

To keep the mechanics functioning properly, nurses must verify that:

  • Weights hang freely: Weights must never touch the floor or rest on the bed frame, as this removes the traction force.
  • Ropes move smoothly: Ropes must sit securely within the center of the pulley grooves without fraying.
  • Line of pull is straight: The pulling force must align with the long axis of the bone to prevent angular deformity.

Nursing Assessment and Neurovascular Monitoring

Continuous assessment is vital because skeletal traction alters normal limb positioning and places stress on surrounding tissues. Nurses perform neurovascular checks frequently to catch compromised circulation or nerve damage early.

The neurovascular assessment mnemonic P-QRST or standard checks evaluate:

  • Pulses: Check distal pulses like dorsalis pedis or radial pulses, comparing sides.
  • Pallor and Temperature: Feel the skin for coldness and observe for pale or cyanotic color.
  • Pain: Assess for deep, throbbing pain that resists prescribed analgesics, which may signal compartment syndrome.
  • Paralysis and Paresthesia: Test the patient's ability to wiggle toes or fingers and ask about numbness or tingling.

Pin Site Care and Infection Prevention

Skeletal pins create an open pathway from the external environment directly to the bone, establishing a clear route for pathogens to cause osteomyelitis. Rigorous aseptic technique during pin site care is therefore non-negotiable.

Institutional protocols vary, but standard care usually follows these steps:

  • Perform hand hygiene and wear sterile gloves.
  • Clean around the pin using sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine swabs, moving in a circular motion from the pin outward.
  • Remove dried crusts only if hospital policy dictates, as some crusting acts as a natural biological barrier.
  • Observe daily for signs of infection such as purulent drainage, increasing redness, warmth, or loosening of the pin.

Patient Mobility and Complication Prevention

Prolonged bed rest in skeletal traction exposes patients to systemic complications like deep vein thrombosis, pressure injuries, and respiratory infections. Nurses must implement preventive care measures while keeping the traction aligned.

  • Encourage hourly incentive spirometry and deep breathing exercises to prevent atelectasis.
  • Use pressure-relieving mattresses and turn the patient slightly if permitted by the orthopedic protocol.
  • Administer prescribed anticoagulants and apply anti-embolism stockings or sequential compression devices.
  • Provide a high-protein diet and adequate fluids to support tissue healing and bowel regularity.

Transition and Discontinuation of Traction

Skeletal traction is temporary. Once sufficient callus formation or bone stability occurs, the surgeon discontinues the traction and transitions the patient to a cast, brace, or internal fixation.

During removal, nurses prepare the patient by explaining the procedure and providing analgesia if necessary. The surgeon cuts or removes the pins, and the nurse dresses the remaining skin sites with sterile dressings. Following removal, the nurse assists with gentle range-of-motion exercises, monitors for rebound swelling, and collaborates with physical therapy to begin safe mobilization.
