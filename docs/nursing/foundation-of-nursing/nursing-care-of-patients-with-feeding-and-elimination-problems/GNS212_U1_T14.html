Colostomy Care

Introduction and Indications for Colostomy

A colostomy is a surgical procedure that brings a portion of the colon out through the abdominal wall to create a functional stoma. This diversion reroutes fecal matter, bypassing a diseased, injured, or obstructed segment of the large intestine. Nurses encounter colostomies in various healthcare settings across Nigeria, ranging from emergency surgical wards managing severe abdominal trauma to oncology units caring for colorectal cancer patients.

Common indications for creating a colostomy include:

  • Colorectal cancer: Malignant tumors causing bowel obstruction or requiring resection of the rectum and anus.
  • Severe abdominal trauma: Gunshot wounds, stab wounds, or blunt trauma resulting in extensive colon perforation.
  • Diverticular disease: Complicated diverticulitis leading to bowel perforation, peritonitis, or severe hemorrhage.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease: Severe cases of Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis unresponsive to medical therapy.
  • Congenital anomalies: Birth defects such as imperforate anus in neonates requiring immediate surgical diversion.

Types of Colostomies and Anatomical Considerations

The anatomical location of the colostomy along the large intestine dictates the consistency of the stool and the management approach. Proximal colostomies produce more liquid output, while distal ones yield formed stool.

  • Ascending colostomy: Located on the right side of the abdomen; output is liquid to semi-liquid and highly irritating to the surrounding skin due to digestive enzymes.
  • Transverse colostomy: Located across the upper abdomen; usually temporary, producing mushy to semi-formed output. It often utilizes a loop configuration supported by a bridging rod.
  • Descending colostomy: Located on the left lower quadrant; output is generally semi-formed to solid because more water has been absorbed by the colon.
  • Sigmoid colostomy: The most common permanent colostomy, located in the left lower quadrant; produces solid, well-formed stool that may allow for predictable evacuation routines.

Stoma and Peristomal Skin Assessment

Regular evaluation of the stoma and the surrounding peristomal skin prevents minor irritation from escalating into painful complications. A healthy stoma should appear pink to red, moist, and slightly raised above the skin level.

A pale, dusky, or cyanotic stoma indicates compromised blood supply and requires immediate surgical notification. Postoperative stomas are normally swollen, but this edema gradually subsides over six to eight weeks. Assess the peristomal skin for erythema, maceration, denudation, or fungal rashes, which often result from effluent leakage beneath the adhesive barrier. Ensure the skin is clean and completely dry before applying any new pouching system.

Colostomy Appliance Selection and Pouching Technique

Selecting the correct appliance and mastering the pouching technique protects the skin and promotes patient confidence. Pouching systems can be one-piece or two-piece, and either drainable or closed.

Proper measurement is critical: the opening of the skin barrier should be cut to fit snugly around the stoma, leaving only about one to two millimeters of exposed skin to prevent effluent contact while avoiding stomal trauma.

  1. Preparation: Gather supplies, explain the procedure, and gently remove the old appliance from top to bottom while supporting the skin.
  2. Cleaning: Wash the peristomal skin with warm water and mild soap, then pat completely dry.
  3. Measuring: Trace and cut the barrier opening to match the exact size and shape of the stoma.
  4. Application: Center the barrier over the stoma, press firmly for thirty seconds to ensure a secure seal, and close the pouch tail.

Nutritional Considerations and Dietary Guidelines

Dietary management helps regulate stoma output and prevents embarrassing gas, odor, or mechanical blockages. Immediately after surgery, patients typically progress from clear liquids to a low-residue diet as bowel motility returns.

Long-term nutritional counseling focuses on identifying foods that cause specific reactions:

  • Gas-producing foods: Beans, onions, cabbage, carbonated beverages, and eggs.
  • Odor-producing foods: Fish, garlic, onions, and certain spices.
  • Blockage risks: Corn, celery, coconut, popcorn, nuts, and raw fruit peels, which require thorough chewing and adequate fluid intake.
  • Odor-controlling foods: Yogurt, buttermilk, and cranberry juice can help minimize fecal odor.

Identifying and Managing Colostomy Complications

Early recognition of colostomy complications prevents serious morbidity and readmissions. Nurses must monitor both acute postoperative issues and long-term mechanical challenges.

  • Stomal Necrosis: Caused by interrupted blood supply; the stoma appears dark, dry, and dusky. Requires immediate surgical intervention.
  • Parastomal Hernia: Protrusion of bowel through the abdominal muscle weakness around the stoma site; managed with abdominal binders or surgical repair if severe.
  • Stomal Prolapse: Excessive telescoping or protrusion of the bowel outward through the stoma site, often linked to increased intra-abdominal pressure.
  • Stomal Retraction: The stoma sinks below the skin level, creating deep skin folds that frequently leak effluent and cause severe peristomal skin breakdown.

Patient Education and Psychological Support

Living with a colostomy involves significant body image adjustments, lifestyle changes, and mastering new physical skills. Effective rehabilitation requires a compassionate, step-by-step educational approach starting early in the hospital stay.

Encourage patients to look at their stoma and participate in appliance changes gradually, moving from observation to independent execution. Address common psychological hurdles such as fear of odor, leakage in public, or loss of sexual intimacy. Connecting patients with established stoma support groups or peer visitors can drastically improve coping mechanisms and long-term emotional adjustment.
