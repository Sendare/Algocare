Bladder Irrigation

Introduction and Purpose of Bladder Irrigation

Bladder irrigation is a specialized nursing procedure involving the instillation of sterile fluid into the bladder. The primary goals are to maintain catheter patency, remove blood clots or mucus, and administer medication directly to the bladder wall. In Nigerian clinical settings, this technique is frequently utilized post-urological surgeries, such as transurethral resection of the prostate, where hematuria and clot formation can rapidly obstruct urinary drainage. By keeping the catheter clear, the nurse prevents painful bladder spasms and dangerous urine retention.

Indications and Contraindications

Recognizing when to use bladder irrigation and when to avoid it prevents patient harm.

  • Indications: Gross hematuria with clot risk, obstructed indwelling urinary catheters, and local instillation of chemotherapeutic agents or antiseptics.
  • Contraindications: Recent urinary tract trauma, unverified urethral disruption, and active lower urinary tract infections without obstruction, as irrigation can push pathogens further into the upper urinary tract.

Open versus Closed Irrigation Systems

Bladder irrigation can be delivered through two main approaches, each carrying distinct infection risks. The open system requires disconnecting the catheter from the drainage tubing to instill fluid, which exposes the sterile urinary tract to environmental pathogens and increases the likelihood of catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Conversely, the closed system utilizes a triple-lumen catheter and a dedicated irrigation port, allowing continuous or intermittent flushing without breaking the sterile junction. Modern nursing practice strongly favors the closed system to protect vulnerable hospitalized patients from ascending infections.

Equipment and Preparation

Proper preparation ensures a smooth and sterile procedure. The nurse must gather the following items before approaching the bedside:

  • Irrigation Solution: Usually sterile normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) warmed to room temperature.
  • Administration Set: IV pole and specialized tubing with a flow clamp.
  • Catheter: A functional triple-lumen catheter if continuous irrigation is ordered.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Clean gloves, apron, and eye protection due to splash risks.
  • Antiseptic Swabs: Alcohol pads for cleaning ports.

Continuous Bladder Irrigation Procedure

Continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) requires constant vigilance to maintain balance. Hang the irrigation bag on the IV pole and prime the tubing to eliminate air. Clean the irrigation port of the triple-lumen catheter with an antiseptic swab and connect the tubing. Open the roller clamp to allow the prescribed drip rate to flow. The key nursing duty during CBI is calculating net fluid output: monitor and record the total volume infused versus the total volume emptied from the drainage bag every shift. If output is significantly less than input, the catheter is likely blocked.

Intermittent Bladder Irrigation Procedure

Intermittent irrigation is performed when manual flushes are needed to clear occasional clots or instill specific medications. Using an open or closed system, the nurse draws a measured volume of sterile saline into a syringe. After cleaning the access port, the nurse gently instills the fluid into the bladder. The syringe is then removed, and the catheter is lowered to allow gravity drainage of the fluid and debris into a collection container. Forceful injection must be avoided at all costs to prevent trauma to the bladder wall.

Monitoring, Complications, and Troubleshooting

Complications during bladder irrigation demand prompt clinical action. Fluid retention leading to water intoxication can occur if irrigation fluid is absorbed through open surgical beds. Bladder spasms frequently happen, often managed with prescribed antispasmodics or by ensuring proper drainage flow. If the drainage stops entirely, the nurse must first check for external kinks in the tubing. If none are found, a doctor's order may be required to perform a manual flush to dislodge stubborn clots.

Documentation and Nursing Responsibilities

Accurate record-keeping completes the irrigation procedure. The nurse must document the type and volume of solution used, the appearance of the drainage (color, presence of clots, sediment), the patient's tolerance, and the exact calculation of net fluid balance. Any complications like severe spasms or bright red bleeding must be reported to the medical team immediately. Maintaining clear records ensures continuity of care across different nursing shifts.
