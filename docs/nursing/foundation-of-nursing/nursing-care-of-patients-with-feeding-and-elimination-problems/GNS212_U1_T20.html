Catheters and Catheterisation

Anatomy and Physiology of the Urinary Tract

Understanding the lower urinary tract is essential before attempting any catheterisation procedure. Urine flows from the kidneys through the ureters into the bladder, where it is stored until micturition occurs. The bladder acts as a muscular reservoir, lined with transitional epithelium and controlled by the detrusor muscle.

  • Male urethra: Approximately 18 to 20 cm long, featuring prostatic, membranous, and penile sections with two natural curves that must be navigated during insertion.
  • Female urethra: Much shorter, measuring about 3.8 to 5 cm, making insertion more direct but also carrying a higher risk of accidental urethral trauma if misplaced into the vagina.
  • Internal sphincter: Involuntary smooth muscle preventing urine leakage from the bladder.
  • External sphincter: Voluntary skeletal muscle allowing conscious control over urination.

Indications and Types of Urinary Catheters

Urinary catheterisation involves the introduction of a hollow tube through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine. Clinical indications include acute urinary retention, monitoring precise urine output in critically ill patients, managing urological surgeries, and delivering local medication.

Catheters vary by material, lumen count, and design:

  • Intermittent catheters: Single-lumen devices inserted to drain the bladder and removed immediately afterward.
  • Indwelling retention catheters: Also known as Foley catheters, these feature a secondary lumen for inflating a retention balloon with sterile water.
  • Three-way catheters: Used specifically for continuous bladder irrigation, featuring an extra port to infuse flushing solution.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Preparation minimizes patient anxiety and reduces the risk of introduction of pathogens into the urinary tract. Nurses must first verify the clinical order, assess the patient's allergy history—particularly regarding latex and chlorhexidine—and check for previous urological surgeries or urethral strictures.

Explain the procedure clearly to secure informed consent and cooperation. Gather all required sterile equipment at the bedside, ensuring adequate lighting and proper positioning. For female patients, the dorsal recumbent or Sims position is standard, while male patients lie supine with legs extended slightly apart.

Step-by-Step Insertion Procedure

Strict aseptic technique prevents catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Perform hand hygiene, set up the sterile field, and don sterile gloves. Cleanse the meatus using antiseptic swabs, wiping from the center outward for females or in a circular motion from the meatus down the shaft for males.

Lubricate the catheter tip generously—using a local anesthetic gel for male patients to ease discomfort and relax the sphincter. Gently advance the catheter until urine flow is observed, then advance it an additional few centimeters further before inflating the retention balloon. Pull back gently until resistance is felt to ensure secure placement in the bladder neck.

Ongoing Catheter Care and Maintenance

Maintaining a closed drainage system is the single most effective intervention for preventing catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Secure the catheter tubing to the patient's thigh or lower abdomen to prevent urethral traction and micro-tearing of the mucosal lining.

Empty the collection bag regularly using a clean measuring container for each patient, ensuring the spigot never touches the contaminated container surface. Keep the drainage bag positioned below the level of bladder height at all times to prevent the backflow of urine into the bladder.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Catheterisation carries inherent risks that require vigilant monitoring by nursing staff. The most common complication is catheter-associated urinary tract infection, presenting with fever, cloudy urine, or suprapubic tenderness.

  • Urethral trauma: Results from forced insertion or inadequate lubrication, leading to bleeding and false passages.
  • Bladder spasms: Caused by bladder irritation from the balloon; managed with antispasmodic medications rather than flushing blocked lines.
  • Crust formation: Mineral buildup that blocks drainage, requiring catheter replacement if patency cannot be restored.

Catheter Removal Protocol

Removing an indwelling catheter requires a medical order and should occur as soon as clinical indications resolve to minimize infection risks. Gather a syringe, perform hand hygiene, and explain the procedure to the patient. Insert the syringe into the inflation port and allow the sterile water to withdraw completely by passive recoil or gentle aspiration.

Instruct the patient to take a deep breath and exhale slowly, then smoothly withdraw the catheter. Monitor the patient for the first spontaneous voiding post-removal, noting volume and time. Report urinary retention if the patient is unable to void within six to eight hours.
