Insertion of Flatus Tube

Introduction and Purpose of Flatus Tube Insertion

A flatus tube, sometimes referred to as a rectal tube, is a flexible medical device inserted through the anal sphincter into the rectum. The primary purpose of this intervention is to relieve severe abdominal distension caused by the accumulation of intestinal gas that the patient cannot expel naturally. When gas becomes trapped in the large intestine, it creates uncomfortable pressure, cramping, and distension, which can negatively impact respiratory excursion and post-operative recovery. By creating a temporary, open pathway through the anal canal, the flatus tube allows trapped air to escape freely into a collection bag or bedpan. This procedure is typically reserved for situations where simpler nursing interventions, such as early ambulation, repositioning, or administering carminative enemas, have failed to resolve the flatulence.

Indications and Contraindications for Flatus Tube Insertion

Flatus tube insertion is indicated for patients experiencing painful abdominal distension due to gas accumulation, frequently seen following abdominal surgeries, during prolonged bed rest, or in paralytic ileus settings.

However, the procedure is strictly contraindicated in specific clinical scenarios to prevent severe harm:

  • Recent rectal or anal surgery: Introducing a tube can disrupt fresh suture lines and cause hemorrhage.
  • Rectal pathology: Conditions such as severe hemorrhoids, rectal tumors, or strictures increase trauma risks.
  • Neutropenia or bleeding disorders: Risk of mucosal tearing and subsequent infection or uncontrolled bleeding is very high.
  • Inflammatory bowel conditions: Severe colitis or active ulceration contraindicates mechanical irritation of the bowel wall.

Required Equipment and Materials

Gathering all necessary supplies before approaching the bedside ensures an efficient procedure and minimizes patient discomfort and exposure.

  • Flatus tube: Usually a soft rubber or plastic tube, sized appropriately for the patient (typically French size 22 to 30 for adults).
  • Water-soluble lubricant: Essential to reduce friction and prevent trauma to the delicate rectal mucosa.
  • Clean gloves: Worn throughout the procedure to maintain standard precautions.
  • Incontinence pad or waterproof sheet: Placed under the patient's hips to protect the bed linens from accidental stool leakage.
  • Tissue paper or wipes: For cleaning the perianal area after the tube is removed.
  • Collection device: A basin, odor bag, or bedpan to catch any liquid stool or gas discharge.
  • Tape: Optional, to secure the external end of the tube if left in place temporarily.

Patient Preparation and Communication

Clear communication reduces anxiety and secures patient cooperation during an intimate and potentially embarrassing procedure. The nurse must explain the steps, the expected sensations of pressure, and the reason for the tube in simple, reassuring language. Provide absolute privacy by drawing curtains and screening the bed. Assist the patient into a Left lateral (Sims') position, with the upper knee flexed toward the chest. This posture naturally aligns with the anatomical downward curve of the sigmoid colon and rectum, making insertion smoother and less painful. Assess the patient's baseline vital signs and abdominal girth to establish a comparison point for post-procedure relief.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Flatus Tube Insertion

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on clean non-sterile gloves.
  2. Lubricate the tip of the flatus tube generously, approximately 5 to 10 centimeters for an adult.
  3. Expose the buttocks while maintaining privacy and locate the anal opening.
  4. Instruct the patient to take slow, deep breaths through the mouth to promote relaxation of the anal sphincter.
  5. Insert the tube gently following the anatomical direction of the rectum toward the umbilicus, advancing it about 7 to 10 centimeters in adults.
  6. Never force the tube against resistance, as this can perforate the bowel wall.
  7. Secure or monitor the outer end; place it in a collection container or let it drain for a short period, usually no longer than 20 to 30 minutes to prevent mucosal ischemia.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Patient Comfort

Once the flatus tube is removed, the nurse must assess the patient's response to the intervention. Re-examine the abdomen to check if distension has decreased and ask the patient if the cramping or pain has subsided. Inspect the perianal area and clean away any leaked stool or lubricant, applying barrier cream if skin irritation is present. Reposition the patient for comfort and check their vital signs. Flatus tubes should rarely be left in place indefinitely; leaving a tube in place for hours can cause continuous pressure necrosis of the rectal mucosa. Therefore, standard practice limits indwelling time to short intervals, typically 20 to 30 minutes, unless specifically ordered otherwise by the physician.

Documentation and Reporting

Accurate charting provides a legal record and communicates the patient's progress to the healthcare team.

Document the following details promptly:

  • Date and time of the procedure and who performed it.
  • Reason for insertion, such as severe abdominal distension and lack of flatus.
  • Size of the tube used and the depth of insertion.
  • Patient tolerance and level of cooperation during the process.
  • Results: Approximate amount of gas expelled, presence of stool, and changes in abdominal girth.
  • Time of tube removal and condition of the perianal skin.

Report any abnormal findings, such as bright red bleeding, severe unyielding pain, or failure to expel gas, to the attending physician immediately.
