Gastrostomy Feeding

Introduction and Indications for Gastrostomy

Gastrostomy feeding is a method of providing nutrition, fluids, and medications directly into the stomach through a surgical or endoscopic opening in the abdominal wall. This route bypasses the oral cavity and esophagus entirely, making it ideal for patients who have a functioning gastrointestinal tract but cannot swallow safely or efficiently.

Common clinical indications include:

  • Neurological impairments: Stroke, advanced dementia, Parkinson's disease, or cerebral palsy leading to severe dysphagia and aspiration risk.
  • Structural abnormalities: Head and neck cancers, esophageal strictures, or severe maxillofacial trauma that obstruct the upper GI tract.
  • Increased metabolic demands: Severe burns, critical illness, or failure to thrive where oral intake is chronically inadequate.

Types of Gastrostomy Tubes

Various tubes are used for gastrostomy feeding depending on how they were placed, their material, and their profile.

  • Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) tube: Placed via endoscopy with an external retention bolster and an internal bumper, widely used for initial placement.
  • Skin-level button device (Low-profile device): A discrete, flush-fitting device with a valve mechanism, popular for active patients and children after the stoma tract has fully matured.
  • Surgically placed tubes: Tubes placed via open or laparoscopic surgery when endoscopy is not feasible, sometimes utilizing a Foley or red rubber catheter in emergency settings.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Before initiating a gastrostomy feeding, nurses must perform a thorough baseline assessment to ensure patient safety and detect any potential complications early.

Check the placement documentation and verify that the stoma site is fully healed, especially for newly placed tubes. Inspect the skin surrounding the insertion site for redness, edema, excoriation, or purulent drainage. Assess bowel sounds in all four quadrants and check for abdominal distension, nausea, or residual discomfort. Confirm the patient's positioning, ensuring the head of the bed can be elevated to at least 30 to 45 degrees during and after the feeding to prevent aspiration.

Administration Procedure and Residual Checks

Administering a gastrostomy feed requires strict aseptic technique and careful adherence to volume and rate guidelines.

  1. Verify tube placement: Check external length markings against the initial insertion record and assess gastric residual volume if ordered.
  2. Flush the tube: Flush with 30 mL of warm water using a catheter-tip syringe to ensure patent lumen flow before feeding.
  3. Deliver the feed: Pour formula into the feeding bag or syringe barrel, allowing it to flow by gravity or regulating it via an enteral nutrition pump.
  4. Post-feed flush: Flush the tube again with 30 to 50 mL of water when the feed is finished to clear formula remnants and prevent clogging.

Stoma Site Care and Maintenance

Daily maintenance of the gastrostomy stoma site prevents skin breakdown, localized infection, and device migration. Clean the peristomal skin gently using mild soap and warm water, then pat the area completely dry. Avoid using harsh hydrogen peroxide or alcohol-based solutions unless specifically ordered, as they can delay tissue healing. Rotate the external bolster or tube slightly daily—only if the tract is mature and the physician permits—to prevent the internal bumper from embedding into the gastric mucosa. Inspect the retention disc to ensure it rests comfortably against the skin without pressing too tightly, which can cause pressure necrosis.

Medication Administration via Tube

Administering medications through a gastrostomy tube requires careful preparation to avoid tube occlusion and ensure proper drug absorption. Whenever possible, request liquid formulations from the pharmacy. If solid pills must be used, crush them into a fine powder and dissolve them thoroughly in warm water. Never crush enteric-coated or sustained-release medications, as doing so alters their pharmacokinetic profile and can cause toxicity or tube blockage. Administer each medication separately, flushing the tube with 15 to 30 mL of water between each drug to prevent adverse chemical interactions within the lumen.

Complications and Nursing Interventions

Gastrostomy tubes can present several mechanical and clinical complications that demand prompt nursing intervention.

  • Tube Clogging: Prevented by frequent flushing; treated by gentle warm water instillation using a dedicated enteral syringe.
  • Tube Dislodgement: A medical emergency in new stomas because the tract can close rapidly. Never force a tube back in; notify the physician immediately.
  • Peristomal Leakage: Often caused by excessive gastric volume, infection, or a deteriorating bumper size; requires skin barrier protection and clinician evaluation.
  • Aspiration: Manifests as coughing, dyspnea, or decreased oxygen saturation; requires immediate cessation of feeding and suctioning of the airway.

Home Care and Caregiver Education

Preparing patients and caregivers for home management of gastrostomy feeding reduces hospital readmissions and fosters independence. Education must cover proper hand hygiene before handling equipment, daily skin care routines, and recognizing signs of infection such as spreading redness, foul-smelling drainage, or fever. Caregivers should practice assembling the feeding set, operating the pump, and flushing the tubing under nurse supervision before discharge. Emphasize the importance of keeping emergency supplies at home, including a replacement tube of the same size or a Foley catheter to maintain tract patency if accidental dislodgement occurs.
