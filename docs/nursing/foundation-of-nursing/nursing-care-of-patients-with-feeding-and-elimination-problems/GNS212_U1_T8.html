Faecal Incontinence

Introduction and Definition of Faecal Incontinence

Faecal incontinence is the involuntary loss of solid or liquid faeces, or gas, that represents a social and hygienic problem for the individual. It ranges from occasional leakage of stool during flatulence to complete loss of bowel control. In Nigerian clinical settings, patients often hide this condition due to cultural stigma, leading to delayed presentation at healthcare facilities. Nurses must create a safe and non-judgmental environment to encourage open communication about bowel habits.

Anatomy and Physiology of Normal Bowel Control

Normal continence relies on a complex coordination of rectal storage, pelvic floor support, and sphincter function.

  • Internal Anal Sphincter: An involuntary smooth muscle that maintains resting tone.
  • External Anal Sphincter: A striated voluntary muscle that provides extra closure during sudden increases in intra-abdominal pressure.
  • Puborectalis Muscle: Forms a sling around the anorectal junction, maintaining the acute anorectal angle that prevents leakage.
  • Rectal Compliance: The ability of the rectum to expand and store stool without high pressure.

Etiology and Common Causes in Nigerian Clinical Practice

The causes of faecal incontinence vary widely, but certain factors are particularly prevalent in local Nigerian practice. Obstetric trauma remains a leading cause among women, stemming from prolonged obstructed labour and poorly repaired episiotomies or perineal tears. Other contributing factors include severe chronic diarrhoea from infectious enteritis, neurological deficits from stroke or spinal cord injury, and complications from rectal surgeries or advanced age.

Pathophysiology and Types of Faecal Incontinence

Faecal incontinence generally manifests as either urge incontinence or passive incontinence.

  • Urge Incontinence: The patient feels the normal urge to defecate but cannot reach the toilet in time due to sphincter weakness or neuropathic failure.
  • Passive Incontinence: Stool leaks without the patient being aware of the urge, often seen in neuropathy or complete sphincter destruction.
  • Overflow Incontinence: Leakage occurs around a hard fecal impaction trapped in the rectum.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Thorough assessment identifies the underlying cause and guides effective management. Nurses must take a detailed bowel history, including frequency, consistency, and pad usage. A digital rectal examination assesses resting sphincter tone and voluntary squeeze pressure. Diagnostic tools such as anorectal manometry, endoanal ultrasound, and sigmoidoscopy may be ordered by the medical team to evaluate sphincter integrity and rectal compliance.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Bowel Training Management

Managing faecal incontinence requires a structured, patient-centred approach focused on restoring predictable bowel habits. Bowel training programs establish a regular schedule, often utilizing the gastrocolic reflex after meals. Dietary modification, including adequate hydration and soluble fibre intake, helps bulk up watery stools. Nurses also coordinate scheduled toileting times and administer prescribed anti-diarrhoeal medications when appropriate.

Skin Care and Prevention of Perineal Dermatitis

Frequent contact with liquid stool places patients with faecal incontinence at high risk for moisture-associated skin damage and perineal dermatitis. Nurses must cleanse the perianal area gently using mild soap and warm water after every incontinence episode, patting the skin dry rather than rubbing. Barrier creams or ointments containing zinc oxide should be applied to protect the epidermis from the corrosive enzymes present in faeces.

Psychosocial Support and Complications Management

Faecal incontinence deeply affects a patient's mental health, often causing severe anxiety, social withdrawal, and depression. Patients may stop attending social gatherings or religious services due to fear of odour or leakage. Nursing care must address these emotional burdens by offering empathetic listening, providing discreet containment products, and connecting patients with counseling services or support groups.
