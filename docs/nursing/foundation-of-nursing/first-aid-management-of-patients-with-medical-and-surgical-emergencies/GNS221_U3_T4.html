Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is the escape of blood from the vascular system due to vessel damage. It can occur following trauma, surgical procedures, or underlying medical pathologies such as clotting disorders or ruptured aneurysms. The severity of a haemorrhage depends heavily on the volume of blood lost, the rate of the bleeding, and the specific site of the vascular injury. Rapid loss of even a small percentage of total blood volume can trigger circulatory collapse, whereas a slower bleed over a longer duration allows physiological compensatory mechanisms to adjust, masking early signs of clinical deterioration.

Classification of Haemorrhage: Types and Sources

  • Arterial haemorrhage: Blood spurts out in bright red pulses corresponding to the heartbeat, representing a high-pressure injury that demands immediate intervention.
  • Venous haemorrhage: Blood flows in a steady, dark red stream, reflecting a lower pressure system that is generally easier to control.
  • Capillary haemorrhage: Blood oozes slowly from minor tissue abrasions and frequently clots spontaneously without advanced medical intervention.
  • External haemorrhage: Bleeding is visible on the body surface, originating from breaks in the skin or mucous membranes.
  • Internal haemorrhage: Blood collects inside body cavities or organs, remaining hidden from direct view and presenting significant diagnostic challenges.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

Hypovolemic shock develops when the circulating blood volume drops below the level required to adequately perfuse vital organs.

  1. Compensated stage: Baroreceptors detect falling blood pressure, prompting the sympathetic nervous system to release catecholamines. This increases heart rate and causes peripheral vasoconstriction to redirect blood flow to the brain and heart.
  2. Decompensated stage: Compensatory mechanisms fail as fluid loss exceeds thirty percent of total volume. Blood pressure plummets, peripheral pulses weaken, and inadequate cellular perfusion initiates anaerobic metabolism.
  3. Irreversible stage: Prolonged cellular hypoxia leads to widespread cellular death, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome, and death despite fluid resuscitation efforts.

Clinical Assessment and Recognition of Bleeding

A systematic nursing assessment is vital for detecting early deterioration in patients at risk for haemorrhage. Tachycardia is often the earliest vital sign abnormality, appearing before a drop in systemic blood pressure manifests. Nurses must monitor for subtle behavioral shifts such as restlessness, confusion, or anxiety, which indicate cerebral hypoperfusion. Skin changes, delayed capillary refill, and decreasing urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h serve as reliable indicators of failing peripheral and renal perfusion.

First Aid and Emergency Management of External Haemorrhage

Immediate control of external bleeding prevents rapid exsanguination. Direct pressure applied firmly over the wound using a clean dressing remains the primary intervention. If bleeding persists through the first dressing, additional layers are applied without removing the original pad to avoid disrupting fragile clot formation. When direct pressure fails on a limb, a tourniquet is placed proximal to the bleeding site. Hemostatic dressings containing specialized clotting agents can also be packed directly into deep junctional wounds where tourniquets cannot be applied.

Identification and Management of Internal Haemorrhage

Internal bleeding occurs within closed body cavities such as the abdomen, thorax, or retroperitoneal space. Common signs include abdominal rigidity, distension, bruising over the flanks, and absent bowel sounds. Patients may cough up bright red blood from the lungs or pass black, tarry stools from the gastrointestinal tract. Management requires rapid surgical intervention or interventional radiology to stop the source of bleeding, alongside aggressive resuscitation to stabilize hemodynamics.

Nursing Interventions and Fluid Resuscitation

Managing a bleeding patient requires coordinated nursing actions to restore tissue perfusion. Nurses must secure large-bore peripheral intravenous lines to facilitate rapid fluid administration. Isotonic crystalloids are infused initially, but modern protocols emphasize early administration of blood products to prevent the lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, oxygen saturation, and urine output helps evaluate the effectiveness of the resuscitation strategy.

Complications of Severe Haemorrhage

Uncontrolled blood loss frequently leads to cascade complications that threaten patient survival. The lethal triad of trauma—hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy—creates a destructive feedback loop where clotting fails entirely. Cold intravenous fluids and exposure lower body temperature, which impairs enzyme reactions required for clot formation. Tissue hypoperfusion generates lactic acid, lowering blood pH and further inhibiting coagulation enzymes, ultimately driving the patient toward multi-organ failure.
