Fracture

Definition and Types of Fractures

A fracture is a break or disruption in the structural continuity of a bone. When the mechanical force applied exceeds the tensile strength of the bone tissue, cracking or complete separation occurs. Fractures are broadly categorized based on how the bone breaks and the condition of the surrounding skin.

  • Closed (Simple) Fracture: The bone breaks, but the overlying skin remains intact, minimizing the risk of external contamination.
  • Open (Compound) Fracture: The broken bone ends pierce through the skin or a deep wound exposes the bone to the outside environment, carrying a high risk of osteomyelitis.
  • Complete Fracture: The break goes completely through the bone, dividing it into two or more distinct pieces.
  • Incomplete Fracture: The bone is cracked or bent but not fully separated into parts, commonly seen in young children whose bones are more flexible (greenstick fractures).
  • Displaced Fracture: The bone pieces are out of normal alignment at the fracture site.
  • Nondisplaced Fracture: The bone cracks either part or all of the way through, but maintains proper anatomical alignment.

Pathophysiology and Bone Healing

When a bone breaks, blood vessels rupture, causing bleeding into the fracture site and forming a fracture hematoma. This initial clotting phase lasts about 48 to 72 hours. Following this, the body initiates a complex repair sequence divided into distinct stages:

  1. Inflammatory Phase: Granulation tissue replaces the hematoma, bringing phagocytes to clean up necrotic debris.
  2. Soft Callus Formation: Fibroblasts and osteoblasts migrate to the area, depositing collagen and cartilage to form a soft bridge between the bone ends.
  3. Hard Callus Formation: Ossification occurs as mineral salts are deposited into the soft callus, turning it into a bony bridge known as a hard callus within 3 to 4 weeks.
  4. Remodeling: Osteoclasts resorb excess bone while osteoblasts lay down new compact bone along lines of stress, restoring the bone to its original shape and strength.

Causes and Risk Factors

Most fractures result from sudden trauma such as motor vehicle accidents, falls, or sports injuries where the force applied exceeds bone resistance. However, a significant portion of fractures occur due to underlying pathologies that weaken the structural integrity of the bone.

  • Direct Trauma: A direct blow or impact strikes the bone, causing a fracture at the site of impact.
  • Indirect Trauma: Force travels along the bone shaft from another point of impact, resulting in a break at a distance.
  • Pathological Fractures: Normal daily stress causes a break in a bone weakened by disease, such as osteoporosis, bone tumors, or osteogenesis imperfecta.
  • Stress (Fatigue) Fractures: Repeated, repetitive micro-trauma exceeds the bone's ability to repair itself over time, commonly seen in athletes or military recruits.

Key risk factors include advanced age, postmenopausal estrogen loss, nutritional deficiencies in calcium and Vitamin D, and sedentary lifestyles.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Recognizing a fracture requires a systematic physical assessment. Classic signs and symptoms often present immediately after the injury, though some may develop gradually as swelling increases.

Sign / SymptomClinical Description
Pain and TendernessResulting from muscle spasm, tissue trauma, and direct nerve irritation at the break.
Loss of FunctionInability to move the extremity normally due to pain or a disrupted mechanical lever system.
DeformityAbnormal positioning or angulation of the limb caused by muscle pull or bone displacement.

Swelling and EcchymosisAccumulation of fluid and extravasated blood in the soft tissues around the injury.

Emergency First Aid Management

Immediate pre-hospital care aims to prevent further injury, reduce pain, and minimize complications like neurovascular compromise or wound contamination. The golden rule of fracture first aid is to immobilize the bone in the position it was found.

  • Assess Airway, Breathing, and Circulation: Prioritize life-threatening injuries before focusing on the extremity.
  • Control Bleeding: Apply direct pressure to any bleeding wounds, using sterile dressings if available, avoiding direct pressure over exposed bone ends in open fractures.
  • Immobilize: Splint the joint above and the joint below the fracture site to prevent movement of the broken bone ends.
  • Check Neurovascular Status: Evaluate distal pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, and motor-sensory function before and after applying any splint.
  • Apply Cold Packs: Use ice packs wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling and numb local pain, avoiding direct skin contact.

Do not attempt to push protruding bone ends back into the body under any circumstances.

Medical and Surgical Treatment Interventions

Treatment focuses on achieving reduction (setting the bone) and retention (holding it in place until healing occurs). Reduction can be closed through manual manipulation or open through surgical exposure.

  • Closed Reduction and Immobilization: Manual traction aligns the bone fragments without a surgical incision, followed by the application of a plaster or fiberglass cast.
  • Traction: Pulling force applied to a body part to align a fracture and reduce muscle spasms; can be skin traction (Buck's) or skeletal traction using pins inserted into the bone.
  • Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF): Surgery exposes the fracture to realign the bone, using metal plates, screws, rods, or pins to secure the fragments internally.
  • External Fixation: Pins are inserted through the skin into the bone above and below the fracture and attached to an external metal frame, often used for open fractures with extensive soft tissue damage.

Potential Complications

Fractures can lead to local and systemic complications that threaten limb viability or life. Early identification allows for prompt medical intervention.

  • Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment restricts blood flow, causing severe, unremitting pain that is unrelieved by opioids and loss of distal pulses.
  • Fat Embolism Syndrome: Fat globules from the yellow bone marrow are released into the bloodstream, traveling to the lungs and brain, causing respiratory distress and petechiae.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism: Immobility promotes blood stasis in lower extremities, which can dislodge and travel to the pulmonary vasculature.
  • Delayed Union or Nonunion: Healing takes significantly longer than normal or fails to occur entirely due to poor blood supply or inadequate immobilization.
  • Infection (Osteomyelitis): Bacterial invasion of the bone, particularly common in open fractures or following surgical fixation.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on monitoring healing, preventing complications of immobility, and restoring functional independence.

  • Neurovascular Assessment: Regularly check the 5 Ps (Pain, Pallal, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis) distal to the injury.
  • Cast Care: Keep casts dry, handle wet plaster with palms rather than fingertips to avoid indentations, and inspect skin edges for irritation.
  • Pin Site Care: Clean skeletal pins or external fixators using aseptic technique to prevent pin-tract infections.
  • Mobility Support: Assist with safe transfers, encourage deep breathing exercises to prevent pneumonia, and teach proper crutch or walker walking mechanics.
  • Nutrition: Promote a high-protein, high-calcium diet rich in Vitamin C and D to support tissue repair and bone mineralization.
