Asphyxia

Definition and Causes of Asphyxia

Asphyxia is a condition where the body is deprived of an adequate supply of oxygen, leading to hypoxia and hypercapnia. If left uncorrected, cellular damage and death follow swiftly.

  • Mechanical Obstruction: Foreign bodies, choking, strangulation, or hanging blocking the airway.
  • Environmental Factors: Confinement in spaces with low oxygen or replacement of oxygen by toxic gases like carbon monoxide.
  • Pathological Conditions: Severe asthma attacks, epiglottitis, drowning, or massive pulmonary embolism.
  • Neurological Impairment: Brainstem injuries or central nervous system depression from drug overdoses that stop the respiratory drive.

Pathophysiology and Stages

The body responds to oxygen deprivation in distinct physiological phases. Recognizing these stages helps nurses gauge how long a patient has been deprived of oxygen.

  1. Stage of Dyspnoea: Rapid breathing and heart rate occur as the body tries to compensate for falling oxygen levels. Cyanosis begins to appear.
  2. Stage of Convulsions: Oxygen starvation in the brain triggers involuntary muscle spasms, twitching, and loss of consciousness.
  3. Stage of Apnoea: Breathing slows down and eventually stops altogether. Blood pressure drops, pupils dilate, and reflexes disappear.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

A patient suffering from asphyxia presents with distinct physical signs that demand immediate intervention. Nurses must perform a rapid primary survey focusing on the airway, breathing, and circulation.

  • Skin Changes: Cyanosis around the lips, nail beds, and mucous membranes due to unoxygenated hemoglobin.
  • Respiratory Distress: Use of accessory muscles, nasal flaring, and gasping or absent breath sounds.
  • Neurological Status: Restlessness, confusion, lethargy, or deep coma.
  • Cardiovascular Signs: Tachycardia shifting to bradycardia as hypoxia worsens, accompanied by falling blood pressure.

Emergency Management and Resuscitation

Immediate action is critical to restore ventilation and tissue oxygenation. Every second counts when the brain is deprived of oxygen.

  • Remove the patient from the dangerous environment or eliminate the source of obstruction.
  • Open the airway using the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver, unless a cervical spine injury is suspected.
  • Clear visible foreign bodies or secretions from the mouth using suction or a finger sweep if the object is solid and seen.
  • Initiate rescue breaths or bag-valve-mask ventilation with high-flow oxygen.
  • Begin chest compressions immediately if no pulse is felt or if the heart rate remains critically low.

Post-Resuscitation Nursing Care

Once spontaneous circulation and breathing return, comprehensive monitoring is essential to prevent secondary organ damage, particularly to the brain and kidneys.

  • Continuously monitor oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry and keep the airway patent.
  • Perform frequent neurological checks to assess recovery of consciousness and detect cerebral edema early.
  • Monitor vital signs closely, watching for hypotension or arrhythmias.
  • Administer prescribed medications and maintain accurate fluid balance charts.
  • Provide a calm, reassuring environment as the patient awakens from the hypoxic episode.
