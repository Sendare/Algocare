Stings

Introduction to Stings and Common Culprits in Nigeria

Stings are puncture wounds inflicted by venomous creatures using specialized organs like stingers or fangs. In the Nigerian environment, nursing encounters with stings frequently involve insects and arachnids adapted to tropical climates. Identifying the specific culprit helps determine the expected severity and immediate management trajectory.

  • Hymenoptera species: Bees, wasps, and hornets possess a venom apparatus linked to the abdominal region. Honeybees leave behind a barbed stinger with a venom sac that continues to pump toxins, while wasps and hornets can sting multiple times.
  • Scorpions: Common in rural and semi-arid regions of Nigeria, scorpion stings deliver potent neurotoxins through a curved tail telson. Species like Leiurus or Parabuthus inflict intense localized pain accompanied by systemic autonomic symptoms.
  • Ants: Fire ants and certain tropical black ants use powerful mandibles to anchor themselves while stinging with the abdomen, delivering irritating alkaloid venoms.

Pathophysiology and Local vs. Systemic Reactions

Venom is a complex mix of proteins, peptides, and enzymes that trigger localized tissue destruction or widespread immune responses. When a sting occurs, mast cells degranulate and release histamine, serotonin, and bradykinin.

Local reactions stay confined to the puncture site. Patients experience sharp pain, transient erythema, and localized edema measuring less than ten centimeters across. This is a direct pharmacological effect of the venom components combined with normal inflammatory signaling.

Systemic reactions go far beyond the site of injury. These occur due to IgE-mediated hypersensitivity or massive toxic loads from multiple stings. Generalized urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, and hemodynamic collapse characterize this dangerous response, requiring urgent resuscitation in the emergency ward.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment of Sting Victims

A systematic nursing assessment ensures prompt identification of life threats. Begin with the primary survey checking airway, breathing, and circulation. Inspect the skin thoroughly to count sting sites and locate embedded stingers.

  • Mild presentations: Localized burning pain, pruritus, wheal-and-flare reaction, and minor swelling.
  • Moderate presentations: Extensive edema spreading across a whole limb, generalized itching, and mild gastrointestinal upset.
  • Severe presentations: Stridor, wheezing, hypotension, cyanosis, confusion, and sense of impending doom.

Ask the patient or relatives about previous sting history and known allergies. Document the exact time elapsed since the sting, as rapid symptom onset often predicts severe clinical deterioration.

First Aid Management and Immediate Interventions

Immediate pre-hospital or bedside actions can limit venom absorption and reduce symptom severity. Time is critical when dealing with venomous stings.

  1. Move to safety: Quickly escort the patient away from the stinging insects to prevent further attacks.
  2. Remove the stinger: Scrape the stinger off gently using a rigid card or fingernail. Never squeeze with tweezers or fingers, as this forces the remaining venom sac to empty into the wound.
  3. Clean the area: Wash the site thoroughly with clean soap and water to prevent secondary bacterial infection.
  4. Apply cold therapy: Place a cold pack or ice wrapped in a clean cloth over the site for ten minutes at a time to vasoconstrict vessels and numb pain.

Pharmacological Treatment and Emergency Care

Medical management relies on dampening inflammatory pathways and treating specific symptoms. For routine stings, oral antihistamines like chlorpheniramine or cetirizine relieve itching and localized swelling.

Topical or oral analgesics manage pain. When moderate to severe local swelling develops, short courses of oral corticosteroids help curtail delayed inflammatory reactions. Secondary bacterial infections resulting from scratching require appropriate antibiotic therapy.

Emergency interventions must be deployed instantly for severe reactions. Intravenous access is secured immediately, and continuous cardiac monitoring is initiated while preparing emergency resuscitation medications.

Recognizing and Managing Anaphylactic Shock

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening, multi-system allergic emergency. Nurses must recognize the early signs before airway closure occurs. Symptoms include throat tightness, hoarseness, widespread hives, wheezing, tachycardia, and a rapid drop in blood pressure.

Epinephrine is the absolute first-line drug of choice and must be administered intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh without delay. Delaying epinephrine administration significantly increases mortality.

Supportive care follows immediately: position the patient flat with elevated legs unless breathing is difficult, administer high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask, and set up intravenous fluid resuscitation with normal saline to combat distributive shock.

Complications Associated with Severe Stings

Severe envenomation or prolonged anaphylaxis can cause profound secondary complications requiring intensive nursing monitoring.

  • Acute Kidney Injury (AKI): Massive hemolysis, rhabdomyolysis from multiple wasp or scorpion stings, and sustained hypotension can lead to acute tubular necrosis and renal failure. Monitor strict input and output.
  • Serum Sickness: A delayed immune-complex reaction that may appear days or weeks after receiving animal serum products or severe stings, marked by fever, arthralgia, and rash.
  • Secondary Cellulitis: Bacterial invasion through broken skin caused by scratching can develop into spreading cellulitis, requiring wound care and systemic antibiotics.

Prevention and Health Education for the Community

Community health nurses play a vital role in reducing sting incidents through targeted education. Preventive measures can protect families, farmers, and outdoor workers across Nigeria.

Advise individuals to avoid disturbing nests of bees, wasps, and ants. When working outdoors in fields or clearing bushes, wearing long-sleeved clothing, boots, and trousers provides a physical barrier. Discourage the use of strongly scented perfumes or brightly colored clothing that attract stinging insects.

Teach community members basic first aid steps and emphasize the importance of seeking immediate medical attention rather than relying solely on harmful traditional concoctions applied to broken skin.
