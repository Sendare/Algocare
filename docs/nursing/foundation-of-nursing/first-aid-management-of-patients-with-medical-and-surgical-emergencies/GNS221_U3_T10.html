Poultices

Introduction and Physiological Effects of Poultices

A poultice is a soft, moist mass of herbs, flour, or other medicinal substances, usually heated and spread on a cloth for application to inflamed or painful parts of the body. In Nigerian clinical practice, traditional poultices often utilize local medicinal leaves like cassava or pawpaw, while institutional care relies on standardized clay, kaolin, or mustard preparations. The primary physiological mechanism involves local vasodilation. When warmth is applied via the moist mass, superficial blood vessels expand, increasing blood flow to the affected area. This hyperemia brings extra white blood cells to fight infection and speeds up the removal of cellular waste. Simultaneously, the moisture softens hardened tissues, relieves localized pain, and eases muscular tension.

Common Types of Poultices and Their Medicinal Uses

Different formulations serve distinct therapeutic needs in ward settings:

  • Kaolin Poultice (Cataplasma Kaolin): A heavy, clay-based preparation that retains heat for long periods, frequently used to relieve deep-seated chest congestion or painful boils.
  • Herbal Poultice: Prepared from crushed fresh leaves or roots, traditionally utilized in community health settings for localized swellings and insect bites.
  • Linseed Poultice: Made from crushed flaxseed flour, valued for its emollient properties that soften hard abscesses and ease joint stiffness.

Indications and Contraindications for Application

Poultices are indicated for localized inflammatory conditions where drawing action or sustained heat is beneficial. Common targets include early-stage boils, carbuncles, deep muscle aches, and mild respiratory congestion. However, applying a poultice is dangerous in certain situations.

Never apply a hot poultice over broken skin, fresh wounds, or active hemorrhages, as heat increases bleeding and can introduce toxins into open tissue. Patients with sensory deficits, such as those with diabetic neuropathy or spinal cord injuries, must not receive hot poultices because they cannot accurately gauge temperature, risking severe thermal burns. Furthermore, avoid applying irritating herbal poultices over known allergic skin sites.

Equipment, Materials, and Preparation Steps

Preparing a safe and effective treatment requires gathering specific supplies before touching the patient. The nurse needs the poultice medium (such as kaolin powder or crushed herbs), boiling water, a mixing bowl, a spatula, a thermometer to check temperature, clean dressings or a lint cloth, a waterproof binder, and a protective lubricant like petroleum jelly.

Mix the powder or herbs with boiling water to form a smooth paste, stirring continuously to prevent lumps. Allow the mixture to cool to a safe handling temperature, usually tested on the inner wrist. Apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly to the patient's skin to protect it from irritation and make later removal easier. Spread the warm paste evenly onto the clean cloth, leaving a margin of fabric around the edges, then fold the edges over to contain the mass securely.

Application, Patient Monitoring, and Safety

Once prepared, gently place the poultice over the target area, securing it with a bandage or binder to hold it firmly in place without cutting off circulation. Monitor the patient continuously during the first five minutes, asking them to report any sharp burning sensations. Inspect the underlying skin every ten to fifteen minutes for excessive redness, blistering, or signs of maceration. Keep the application in place only for the prescribed duration—usually twenty to thirty minutes—because prolonged heat can cause rebound congestion where blood vessels swell excessively and worsen the condition.
