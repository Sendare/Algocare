Burden of Unsafe Injection Practices

Introduction to Injection Safety in Nursing

An injection is one of the most common healthcare procedures worldwide, used for therapeutic or preventive purposes. However, when administered without adherence to strict safety standards, injections pose severe health risks to both patients and healthcare providers. Injection safety refers to a set of measures taken to perform injections in an optimal manner, ensuring no harm comes to the recipient, the healthcare worker, or the community.

Nurses form the frontline of healthcare delivery and administer the vast majority of parenteral medications. Consequently, understanding injection safety goes beyond knowing the correct anatomical site or push rate; it encompasses the entire lifecycle of an injection, from preparation to waste disposal. Unsafe practices often stem from systemic issues such as supply shortages, poor staffing, or a lack of continuous professional education.

Global and National Burden of Unsafe Injections

The World Health Organization estimates that billions of injections are administered annually in developing and transitional countries. A significant percentage of these injections are unsafe, primarily driven by the reuse of disposable syringes and needles without proper sterilization.

In Nigeria and many sub-Saharan African settings, systemic resource constraints exacerbate this burden. Patients frequently request injections, believing they act faster than oral medications, which inadvertently drives over-prescribing and unsafe practices in under-resourced private and public facilities. The consequences manifest as massive outbreaks of bloodborne viral infections and bacterial abscesses.

Common Unsafe Injection Practices

  • Syringe reuse: Using the same syringe or needle for multiple patients, sometimes changing only the needle while reusing the plastic barrel.
  • Medication contamination: Accessing multi-dose vials with contaminated needles or leaving a needle inserted in a vial septum for future use.
  • Unsafe medication preparation: Preparing medications in a contaminated workspace or mixing drugs without aseptic technique.
  • Improper sharps disposal: Leaving used needles uncapped, on bedsides, or overflowing in makeshift containers.

Pathophysiology and Disease Transmission Risks

Unsafe injections facilitate the direct parenteral transmission of infectious pathogens. The most prominent bloodborne pathogens transmitted through contaminated sharps include Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus.

The infectious risk varies by pathogen. Hepatitis B carries the highest risk of transmission after a single needle-stick injury due to its high viral load in blood, followed by Hepatitis C and HIV. Beyond viral pathogens, unhygienic practices introduce bacterial agents such as Staphylococcus aureus, leading to severe local abscesses, sepsis, and toxic shock syndrome.

Health and Economic Consequences

The fallout from unsafe injection practices extends far beyond immediate clinical complications. Chronic infections like Hepatitis B and C lead to long-term liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, placing a massive burden on families and healthcare systems.

Economically, managing chronic viral infections or severe injection-site abscesses requires costly medications, prolonged hospital stays, and surgical debridement. For low-income households in developing regions, these unexpected medical expenses frequently plunge families into deep poverty, compounding the public health crisis with social devastation.

Occupational Hazards for Healthcare Workers

Nurses and midwives experience the highest frequency of occupational needle-stick and sharps injuries among all healthcare professions. These incidents commonly occur during recapping of needles, improper disposal into overfilled bins, or when passing sharp instruments during emergency procedures.

Living with the constant threat of seroconversion to HIV or hepatitis causes profound psychological distress, anxiety, and depression among nursing personnel. Preventive institutional policies, availability of safety-engineered devices, and immediate access to post-exposure prophylaxis are critical to protecting the workforce.

Best Practices for Safe Injection Administration

  • Aseptic technique: Perform hand hygiene before preparation, and disinfect the injection site and vial rubber septums with alcohol swabs.
  • Single-use devices: Use a sterile, single-use syringe and needle for every injection, ensuring packaging is intact.
  • No recapping: Never recap used needles using two hands. If recapping is unavoidable, use a one-handed scoop technique.
  • Single-dose preference: Utilize single-dose medication vials whenever possible to prevent cross-contamination between patients.

Sharps Injury Prevention and Waste Management

Proper waste management is the final defense against community exposure to contaminated sharps. Puncture-resistant safety boxes must be placed within arm's reach of every area where injections are prepared or administered.

Containers must never be overfilled; they should be securely closed and sealed when they reach the three-quarters full mark. Disposing of needles in regular household or municipal waste poses grave danger to waste pickers and the general public, perpetuating the cycle of accidental transmission.

The Role of the Nurse in Advocacy and Quality Improvement

Nurses play a pivotal leadership role in driving systemic change to eliminate unsafe injection practices. Beyond clinical competence, nurses must advocate for institutional policies that prioritize the procurement of safety-engineered devices and adequate personal protective equipment.

Quality improvement initiatives, such as conducting routine audits of injection practices and organizing peer education workshops, empower healthcare teams to identify hazards and correct dangerous habits. By fostering a blame-free reporting culture, facilities can encourage transparent reporting of needle-stick incidents and near-misses.
