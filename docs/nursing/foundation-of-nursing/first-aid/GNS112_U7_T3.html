Shock

Definition and Pathophysiology of Shock

Shock is a life-threatening clinical syndrome characterized by systemic cellular hypoxia due to a critical imbalance between oxygen delivery and oxygen consumption. When tissue perfusion drops, cells cannot maintain aerobic metabolism. They switch to anaerobic pathways, producing excess lactic acid and leading to metabolic acidosis. Without intervention, this cellular distress causes membrane pump failure, intracellular edema, and ultimately cell death and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).

Stages of Shock

Shock progresses through distinct clinical phases if underlying causes remain uncorrected:

  1. Compensated Stage: Homeostatic mechanisms maintain adequate blood pressure and perfusion to vital organs through baroreceptor reflex and hormone release, though subtle tachycardia may appear.
  2. Progressive Stage: Compensatory mechanisms begin failing. Hypoperfusion worsens, causing vasoconstriction to divert blood away from non-essential organs, leading to declining organ function and falling blood pressure.
  3. Irreversible Stage: Severe cellular damage, tissue necrosis, and organ failure become so profound that medical treatment can no longer save the patient, culminating in death.

Types and Classifications of Shock

Medical classification divides shock into four major categories based on the underlying hemodynamic disturbance:

  • Hypovolemic Shock: Caused by a severe loss of intravascular volume from hemorrhage, severe dehydration, or burns.
  • Cardiogenic Shock: Results from primary cardiac pump failure, commonly seen in acute myocardial infarction or severe heart failure.
  • Distributive Shock: Characterized by severe peripheral vasodilation leading to relative hypovolemia. Subtypes include septic, anaphylactic, and neurogenic shock.
  • Obstructive Shock: Caused by a physical obstruction to blood flow within or directly outside the heart, such as cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, or pulmonary embolism.

Clinical Assessment and Manifestations

Initial signs of shock are often subtle, requiring vigilant nursing assessment. Common indicators include:

  • Tachycardia as the heart tries to maintain cardiac output.
  • Tachypnea driven by metabolic acidosis and hypoxia.
  • Cool, pale, or clammy skin in hypovolemic and cardiogenic states, contrasting with warm, flushed skin early in septic shock.
  • Altered mental status, ranging from restlessness and anxiety to lethargy and unresponsiveness, reflecting reduced cerebral perfusion.
  • Oliguria, defined as urine output dropping below 0.5 mL/kg/hour, signaling poor renal blood flow.

Emergency Nursing Management and Interventions

Immediate nursing care focuses on stabilizing the airway, breathing, and circulation while identifying the underlying cause. Nurses must secure large-bore intravenous lines for rapid fluid resuscitation or medication administration. Supplemental oxygen should be delivered at high flow rates to maximize oxygen saturation in remaining red blood cells. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, electrocardiograms, and accurate hourly urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter guides ongoing therapy.

Pharmacological Therapy

When fluid resuscitation alone fails to restore adequate blood pressure and tissue perfusion, vasoactive medications are initiated. Vasopressors such as norepinephrine constrict peripheral blood vessels to boost systemic vascular resistance and raise blood pressure. Inotropic agents like dobutamine strengthen myocardial contractility to improve cardiac output in cardiogenic shock. For anaphylaxis or septic shock, specific agents like epinephrine, antibiotics, or corticosteroids target the root pathophysiological trigger.
