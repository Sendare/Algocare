Priorities of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing requires rapid decision-making in high-pressure environments where seconds can mean the difference between life and death. The core of this specialty is triage, the systematic sorting of patients based on the severity of their illness or injury rather than their order of arrival. In Nigerian healthcare facilities, nurses often face overwhelming patient loads, making effective triage essential for resource allocation.

  • Emergency Severity Index (ESI): A five-level tool that categorizes patients from level 1 (resuscitation required immediately) to level 5 (non-urgent).
  • Primary vs Secondary Assessment: A structured approach ensuring immediate threats are handled before detailed examinations begin.
  • Golden Hour: The critical first sixty minutes after traumatic injury when medical and surgical intervention gives the highest chance of survival.

The Primary Survey and Resuscitation (ABCDE Approach)

The ABCDE approach provides a standardized framework for identifying and managing life-threatening conditions in any emergency patient. Every healthcare provider must follow this exact sequence because an issue in a preceding step will cause failure in subsequent interventions. Resuscitation occurs simultaneously with assessment whenever an immediate danger is found.

A patient talking in full sentences generally has a patent airway, adequate breathing, and sufficient brain perfusion, giving a quick initial snapshot of their stability. Skipping steps or failing to re-evaluate after an intervention leads to missed deterioration.

Airway Management and Cervical Spine Protection

Opening and maintaining the airway takes absolute priority over all other resuscitative efforts. In any trauma case, assume a cervical spine injury is present until clinical assessment or imaging proves otherwise.

  1. Head-tilt/chin-lift: Used for medical patients without suspected trauma to lift the tongue away from the posterior pharynx.
  2. Jaw-thrust maneuver: The safest method for trauma patients, displacing the mandible forward without extending the neck.
  3. Oropharyngeal airway (OPA): Inserted in unconscious patients to prevent the tongue from blocking the throat.
  4. Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA): Tolerated better by semi-conscious patients, though contraindicated in severe facial fractures.

Breathing and Ventilation Assessment

Once the airway is secure, the nurse evaluates whether the patient is breathing effectively. Look for chest symmetry, use of accessory muscles, and respiratory rate. Auscultate lung fields bilaterally to ensure air is entering both lungs equally.

Life-threatening conditions such as tension pneumothorax or flail chest can develop rapidly and compromise ventilation. A tension pneumothorax causes air to enter the pleural space without escaping, shifting the mediastinum and compressing the heart. This requires immediate needle decompression before waiting for chest X-ray confirmation.

Circulation, Hemorrhage Control, and Shock Management

Circulatory assessment focuses on evaluating tissue perfusion by checking pulse rate, blood pressure, capillary refill time, and skin color. Hemorrhage remains the leading cause of preventable death after trauma, making bleeding control a top priority.

  • External Bleeding: Apply direct firm pressure using sterile gauze. If bleeding persists from a limb, apply a tourniquet proximal to the wound.
  • Internal Bleeding: Suspect intra-abdominal or pelvic bleeding in patients with signs of shock and an unexplained drop in blood pressure.
  • Hypovolemic Shock: Occurs when profound fluid or blood loss reduces perfusion, requiring rapid intravenous access with large-bore cannulas and warm isotonic crystalloids or blood products.

Disability and Neurological Evaluation

The disability assessment provides a quick baseline of the patient's neurological status during the primary survey. Clinicians commonly use the AVPU scale for rapid screening or the more detailed Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) to evaluate eye opening, verbal response, and motor response.

  • Alert: The patient is fully awake and responds to normal stimuli.
  • Verbal: The patient responds only when spoken to.
  • Pain: The patient responds exclusively to painful stimuli like a sternal rub.
  • Unresponsive: The patient shows no response to any stimulus.

A sudden drop in GCS score indicates rising intracranial pressure, hypoxia, or cerebral hypoperfusion, requiring immediate re-evaluation of airway and circulation.

Exposure, Environmental Control, and Secondary Survey

Exposure involves removing the patient's clothing to perform a thorough head-to-toe physical examination while preventing hypothermia. Environmental heat loss occurs rapidly in emergency rooms, especially when cold intravenous fluids are infused or wet clothes remain on the patient. Warm blankets and thermal management must accompany exposure.

Once immediate life threats are managed and the patient is stable, the secondary survey begins. This involves taking a detailed medical history using the SAMPLE mnemonic (Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading to injury) and performing a comprehensive head-to-toe physical assessment.

Documentation, Communication, and Handover in Emergencies

Clear communication and accurate documentation are vital components of emergency care. Critical details must be relayed swiftly to incoming team members or receiving units without omitting essential clinical data. The ISBAR framework (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provides a structured format for verbal handovers.

Contemporaneous documentation ensures that every intervention, medication dose, and vital sign change is recorded with exact timestamps. In busy Nigerian emergency departments where shifts change frequently, structured handovers prevent critical errors and maintain continuity of care for unstable patients.
