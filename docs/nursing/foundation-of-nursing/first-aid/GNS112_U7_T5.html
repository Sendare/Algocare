Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning and Toxicological Principles

Poisoning occurs when any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—interferes with normal body chemistry, causing structural damage or functional disturbance. Paracelsus's classic toxicological principle states that the dose makes the poison, meaning virtually any substance, including water and oxygen, becomes toxic if consumed in a sufficiently large quantity. Nurses must evaluate not just the chemical agent involved, but the route of exposure, the amount absorbed, and the patient's individual metabolic rate. Understanding these foundational principles allows clinicians to anticipate the speed and severity of symptom onset.

Classification and Common Routes of Poisoning

Poisons are typically classified by their source, chemical structure, or intended use, such as household cleaning agents, agricultural pesticides, pharmaceuticals, or industrial chemicals. The primary routes of exposure dictate how rapidly a substance enters systemic circulation:

  • Ingestion: Swallowing toxic agents, common in accidental pediatric exposures or intentional overdoses.
  • Inhalation: Breathing in toxic gases, fumes, or vapors, leading to rapid alveolar absorption.
  • Absorption: Skin or mucous membrane contact with lipid-soluble chemicals, pesticides, or certain plants.
  • Injection: Introduction of toxins directly into tissue or blood vessels via insect stings, snakebites, or contaminated needles.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria, poisoning presentations often reflect unique socioeconomic and environmental realities. Accidental poisonings frequently involve young children swallowing kerosene, caustic soda, or leftover medications stored in unlabelled beverage bottles within homes. Conversely, intentional poisonings and deliberate self-harm are observed among adolescents and adults facing severe economic pressures or psychological distress. Agricultural exposures involving organophosphate pesticides are widespread in rural farming communities, while traditional remedies and herbal concoctions of unverified dosages occasionally lead to severe heavy metal or botanical toxicity.

Pathophysiology and Systemic Effects of Toxins

Toxins disrupt normal physiology through various cellular mechanisms, such as binding to vital receptors, inhibiting crucial enzymes, or destroying cellular membranes. For example, carbon monoxide binds firmly to hemoglobin with an affinity much higher than oxygen, starving tissues of cellular respiration. Organophosphates inactivate acetylcholinesterase, causing a dangerous buildup of acetylcholine at nerve synapses and neuromuscular junctions. The clinical picture depends entirely on which organ systems—neurological, cardiovascular, respiratory, or renal—suffer the greatest functional disruption.

Clinical Assessment and Toxidromes

A toxidrome is a constellation of signs and symptoms that strongly suggests a specific class of poisoning. Recognizing these patterns allows nurses and doctors to initiate presumptive treatment before laboratory results return.

  • Cholinergic toxidrome: Characterized by salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, and emesis (SLUDGE), typically caused by organophosphates.
  • Anticholinergic toxidrome: Presents with dry skin, flushed appearance, dilated pupils, fever, and mental confusion (blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, dry as a bone, hot as a hare).
  • Opioid toxidrome: Marked by central nervous system depression, respiratory depression, and pinpoint pupils (miosis).
  • Sympathomimetic toxidrome: Features agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, and dilated pupils, seen with amphetamines or cocaine.

Emergency Management and Decontamination

Emergency care for poisoning always begins with the primary survey: airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs). If the patient is unstable, resuscitation takes precedence over identifying the toxin or performing decontamination. Once stabilized, nurses assist with external and internal decontamination. External decontamination involves removing contaminated clothing and thoroughly washing skin or eyes with copious amounts of water. Gastric lavage and induced emesis are rarely used today due to high aspiration risks; instead, single-dose activated charcoal is often administered orally or via nasogastric tube to adsorb toxins in the gastrointestinal tract if the patient presents early and has a protected airway.

Pharmacological Antidotes and Supportive Care

Specific antidotes exist for only a fraction of known poisons, but when available, they can be life-saving. Antidotes work by neutralizing the toxin, blocking receptor sites, or enhancing elimination. For example, naloxone reverses opioid overdose by competitively binding to opioid receptors, while atropine blocks excessive acetylcholine at receptor sites in organophosphate poisoning. Supportive nursing care remains the cornerstone of recovery for most poisoned patients, focusing on continuous cardiac monitoring, mechanical ventilation support, fluid resuscitation, and managing electrolyte imbalances or seizures.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care for the poisoned patient requires meticulous monitoring and rapid response to sudden clinical deterioration. Nurses must maintain strict intake and output records, monitor neurological status using the Glasgow Coma Scale, and regularly check vital signs, especially oxygen saturation and cardiac rhythm. For patients with altered mental status or ongoing decontamination measures, preventing aspiration and pressure injuries is vital. In cases of intentional self-harm, mental health evaluation, continuous observation for suicide risk, and safeguarding the hospital environment are essential components of the recovery process.

Prevention, Education, and Public Health Strategies

Nurses play a vital role beyond the acute care setting by engaging in community education and public health advocacy to prevent poisoning incidents. Public campaigns should emphasize safe chemical storage practices, such as keeping household cleaners, pesticides, and medications in their original, labeled containers and out of the reach of children. Healthcare personnel can partner with local community leaders and schools to raise awareness about the dangers of storing hazardous substances in beverage bottles. Promoting proper protective equipment among agricultural workers further reduces occupational exposure to dangerous pesticides.
