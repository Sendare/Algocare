Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS)

Introduction to Paediatric Advanced Life Support and Physiological Differences

Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) provides a structured framework for managing critically ill or injured infants and children. Children are not simply small adults; their unique anatomical and physiological profile demands a distinct resuscitation approach.

  • Upper Airway: Large tongue, narrow trachea, and a prominent occiput make airway obstruction a primary risk during manipulation.
  • Respiratory Mechanics: Compliant chest walls and fatigue-prone intercostal muscles lead to rapid respiratory failure.
  • Cardiovascular Reserve: Cardiac output in children is heavily dependent on heart rate rather than stroke volume, meaning bradycardia is an ominous sign of impending arrest.
  • Metabolic Rate: Higher basal oxygen consumption causes rapid desaturation and metabolic acidosis during periods of stress.

The Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT)

The Pediatric Assessment Triangle is a rapid, visual, and auditory tool used within seconds to form a general impression of a child's condition without touching them. It dictates the urgency of intervention.

An abnormality in any one side of the triangle indicates physiological compromise, while abnormalities in all three suggest imminent cardiopulmonary arrest.

High-Quality Basic Life Support and CPR

Initiating high-quality basic life support promptly improves survival outcomes in paediatric arrest. Rescuers must deliver effective chest compressions coupled with safe rescue breaths.

  • Compression-to-Ventilation Ratio: 15:2 for two-rescuer infant and child CPR; 30:2 for a single rescuer.
  • Compression Depth: At least one-third the anteroposterior diameter of the chest (about 4 cm in infants and 5 cm in children).
  • Compression Rate: 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
  • Technique: Use two fingers for a lone rescuer on an infant, or the two-thumb encircling hands technique when two rescuers are present. Use the heel of one or two hands for children.

Airway Management and Ventilation Strategies

Airway patency takes priority in paediatric emergencies. Simple maneuvers like the jaw-thrust or gentle head-tilt chin-lift clear most upper airway blockages.

Bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation requires an appropriately sized mask that covers the mouth and nose without resting on the eyes or extending past the chin. Excessive tidal volumes or high pressure can cause gastric inflation, restricting diaphragmatic movement and increasing aspiration risk. Advanced airways, such as endotracheal tubes, require careful sizing based on length-based tape formulas or age calculations. Always confirm endotracheal tube placement using continuous waveform capnography alongside physical auscultation.

Recognition and Management of Respiratory Emergencies

Respiratory compromise precedes cardiac arrest in the vast majority of paediatric cases. Differentiating between upper airway obstruction, lower airway disease, lung tissue disease, and disordered control of breathing guides targeted therapy.

  • Upper Airway Obstruction: Features stridor, barking cough, and retractions. Managed with humidified oxygen, nebulized epinephrine, or corticosteroids.
  • Lower Airway Obstruction: Presents with wheezing and prolonged expiration, typically seen in asthma or bronchiolitis. Treated with bronchodilators.
  • Lung Tissue Disease: Exhibits crackles, grunting, and tachypnea, managed with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and antimicrobials if infectious.
  • Disordered Control: Characterized by irregular breathing patterns and hypoventilation, requiring bag-valve-mask support.

Recognition and Management of Shock States

Shock is a critical condition where systemic tissue perfusion fails to meet metabolic demands. In children, maintaining blood pressure is a late compensatory mechanism; a normal blood pressure does not rule out shock.

  • Hypovolemic Shock: Caused by fluid loss from diarrhea, vomiting, or hemorrhage. Characterized by delayed capillary refill and cool extremities.
  • Distributive Shock: Caused by sepsis, anaphylaxis, or neurogenic injury, leading to inappropriate vasodilation. Early stages may present with bounding pulses and warm skin ('warm shock').
  • Cardiogenic Shock: Results from congenital heart defects or myocarditis, marked by pulmonary congestion and hepatomegaly.
  • Obstructive Shock: Triggered by cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, or ductal-dependent lesions.

Initial treatment centers on rapid vascular access and fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloids (20 mL/kg), except in cardiogenic shock where fluid overload worsens the condition.

Pediatric Cardiac Arrest Algorithms and Rhythm Disturbances

Paediatric cardiac arrest management splits into shockable and non-shockable pathways. Unlike adults, non-shockable rhythms like Asystole and Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA) are far more common than ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

  • Non-Shockable Rhythms: Immediately begin CPR and administer epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg of 1:10,000 IV/IO) every 3 to 5 minutes, focusing on reversible causes (the Hs and Ts).
  • Shockable Rhythms: Deliver an initial defibrillation energy dose of 2 J/kg, escalating to 4 J/kg or higher for subsequent shocks, alongside epinephrine and antiarrhythmics like amiodarone or lidocaine.

Post-Resuscitation Care

Restoring spontaneous circulation marks only the first milestone; post-resuscitation care focuses on preventing secondary multi-organ injury. Stabilization involves optimizing oxygenation and ventilation to avoid normocapnia or hypocarbia, which can cause cerebral vasoconstriction.

Cardiovascular support requires maintaining age-appropriate blood pressure using inotropes or vasopressors. Neurological monitoring must watch for seizures, and providers should actively prevent fever, as hyperthermia worsens ischemic brain damage. Managing blood glucose levels prevents both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, ensuring the recovering brain and heart have stable metabolic support.
