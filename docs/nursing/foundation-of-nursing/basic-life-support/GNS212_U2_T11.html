Face Mask Oxygen Administration

Introduction and Indications

Oxygen therapy via a face mask is a vital intervention in Nigerian clinical practice, utilized whenever a patient cannot maintain adequate oxygen saturation through room air alone. Medical-surgical wards and emergency units routinely manage cases such as severe malaria with respiratory distress, lobar pneumonia, acute asthma exacerbations, and post-operative recovery where hypoxemia threatens organ function. The primary goal is to reverse tissue hypoxia by delivering a higher fraction of inspired oxygen than is found in ambient air. Nurses must recognize that hypoxia manifests differently across age groups, often presenting as restlessness or confusion in elderly patients before cyanosis appears.

Equipment Needed for Oxygen Delivery

Before initiating therapy, assemble all necessary materials at the bedside to ensure a smooth procedure.

  • Oxygen source: A piped wall outlet or a portable compressed gas cylinder equipped with a working regulator.
  • Flowmeter: A device attached to the oxygen source to control and measure the flow rate in liters per minute.
  • Humidifier bottle: Filled with sterile water to prevent mucosal drying when delivering oxygen at higher flow rates.
  • Connecting tubing: Kink-resistant plastic tubing linking the flowmeter to the delivery mask.
  • Appropriate face mask: Selected based on the prescribed concentration and patient's clinical state.
  • Pulse oximeter: Essential for baseline and continuous monitoring of oxygen saturation.

Types of Oxygen Face Masks and Flow Rates

Different masks deliver varying concentrations of oxygen depending on their design and the flow rate applied.

  • Simple Face Mask: Delivers moderate oxygen concentrations at flow rates between 5 to 10 liters per minute.
  • Venturi Mask: Utilizes color-coded adapters to deliver precise, fixed oxygen concentrations, ideal for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Non-Rebreather Mask: Features a reservoir bag and one-way valves, capable of delivering high concentrations of oxygen approaching 90 to 100 percent at flow rates of 10 to 15 liters per minute.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Thorough preparation ensures patient safety and cooperation. Begin by assessing the patient's baseline respiratory rate, depth, pattern, and oxygen saturation level via pulse oximetry. Inspect the nares, mouth, and facial skin for any existing breakdown, irritation, or secretions. Explain the procedure clearly to the patient and relatives to alleviate anxiety, as panic increases oxygen consumption. Ensure the patient is sitting in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position to maximize lung expansion, and remove any makeup, oil, or food debris from the face before applying the mask.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Oxygen

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on clean gloves to maintain infection control.
  2. Connect the flowmeter to the oxygen source and attach the humidifier bottle if using higher flow rates.
  3. Attach the mask tubing to the humidifier outlet and turn on the oxygen to the prescribed flow rate, ensuring gas is flowing freely.
  4. Place the mask gently over the patient's nose, mouth, and chin, shaping the flexible metal strip over the bridge of the nose for a snug fit.
  5. Secure the elastic strap comfortably around the back of the patient's head, ensuring it is not too tight to cause skin pressure.
  6. Re-evaluate the patient's comfort and check oxygen saturation levels after fifteen minutes.

Ongoing Nursing Monitoring and Patient Safety

Oxygen is a drug and must be monitored continuously. Check the flow rate and humidifier water level every shift. Inspect the skin over the ears and nasal bridge every four hours for signs of pressure injury caused by elastic straps or mask edges. Monitor vital signs and pulse oximetry readings regularly to evaluate the effectiveness of therapy. Enforce strict fire safety rules: oxygen supports combustion, so smoking, naked flames, and electrical sparks must be strictly prohibited near the patient's bed.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

While oxygen is lifesaving, improper administration can lead to complications. Oxygen toxicity can occur when high concentrations of oxygen are administered for extended periods, leading to pulmonary damage. In patients with chronic respiratory conditions who rely on hypoxic drive, high flow rates can depress breathing and cause carbon dioxide narcosis. Drying of the nasal and oral mucosa is common and can be mitigated by ensuring adequate humidification and offering frequent mouth care. Skin breakdown under the mask requires regular repositioning of straps and barrier creams where appropriate.
