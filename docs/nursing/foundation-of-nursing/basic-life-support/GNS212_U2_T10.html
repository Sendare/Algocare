Oxygen Tent

Introduction and Definition of Oxygen Tent

An oxygen tent is a canopy device used in clinical settings to deliver higher concentrations of oxygen to a patient. It consists of a clear plastic canopy that encloses the upper body or the entire bed of the patient, secured tightly under the mattress to prevent leakage. This equipment is most frequently utilized in pediatric wards because young children often tolerate face masks or nasal prongs poorly. The enclosed environment allows healthcare providers to control both the oxygen concentration and the ambient temperature around the child.

Indications for Oxygen Tent Therapy

Oxygen tent therapy is indicated for patients who require controlled, humidified oxygen administration without invasive interfaces. Common clinical scenarios include:

  • Acute respiratory infections: Conditions like severe bronchiolitis or pneumonia causing hypoxemia.
  • Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis): The high humidity combined with oxygen helps soothe inflamed upper airways.
  • Post-tonsillectomy monitoring: Ensuring stable oxygen saturations while managing mild airway swelling.
  • Pediatric burn care: Delivering oxygen while protecting vulnerable skin surfaces from direct pressure.

Components and Setup

Setting up an oxygen tent requires coordinating several mechanical parts to ensure safe and effective delivery.

  1. Canopy: The transparent vinyl cover fitted over the bed or crib.
  2. Oxygen source: Connected to a flowmeter and humidifier, often driven by compressed air or oxygen tanks.
  3. Cooling mechanism: Built-in refrigeration units to prevent the tent interior from becoming uncomfortably warm.
  4. Sealing flaps: Edges tucked firmly beneath the mattress to prevent gas escaping into the room.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Managing a patient inside an oxygen tent demands vigilant nursing surveillance. Nurses must regularly check pulse oximetry readings to evaluate oxygenation status. Because opening the canopy causes a sudden drop in oxygen concentration, nurses must perform quick, organized assessments. Respiratory rate, work of breathing, skin color, and level of consciousness must be documented frequently. Additionally, monitor the child's clothing and bed linen for dampness resulting from the high humidity inside the tent.

Safety Precautions and Fire Hazards

Oxygen supports combustion, meaning it makes fires start easier and burn much faster. Strict safety protocols are mandatory around any oxygen delivery system. Post clear 'Oxygen in Use' warning signs outside the patient unit. Prohibit smoking, open flames, and electrical spark-producing toys or devices within the immediate vicinity. Nurses must also inspect all electrical equipment, such as monitors or nebulizers, to ensure cords are intact and free of fraying before bringing them near the tent.

Complications and Troubleshooting

Several complications can arise during oxygen tent therapy. Hypothermia may develop if the cooling system is set too low, especially in infants. Conversely, stuffiness and overheating occur if the cooling unit fails. Condensation buildup can wet the patient's skin, leading to skin breakdown or chilling. If the oxygen concentration inside the tent reads lower than prescribed, the nurse should inspect the canopy seals, check the mattress tucks, and verify that the flowmeter is set to the correct liter flow.
