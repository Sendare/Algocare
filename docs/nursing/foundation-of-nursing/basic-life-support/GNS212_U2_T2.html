Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a portable electronic device that automatically diagnoses life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. When the heart enters these chaotic rhythms, it cannot pump blood effectively. The AED delivers a calculated electric shock to reset the heart's electrical system, allowing a normal sinus rhythm to resume. Time is critical during sudden cardiac arrest; every minute without defibrillation reduces survival rates significantly. In Nigerian clinical and community settings, the availability of public-access AEDs is growing, making it vital for nurses to understand their function and teach basic operators how to use them effectively.

Indications for AED Use in Basic Life Support

During basic life support, an AED is indicated when a patient presents with three specific clinical criteria: unconsciousness, absence of normal breathing, and lack of a palpable pulse.

  • Ventricular Fibrillation (VF): The most common shockable rhythm where the ventricles quiver uselessly.
  • Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (VT): A rapid, ineffective heart rhythm that fails to produce a cardiac output.

Never apply an AED to a patient who is conscious, breathing normally, or has a palpable pulse, as delivering an electric shock to a normal heart can induce dangerous arrhythmias.

Anatomy and Components of a Standard AED

While different manufacturers produce various models, every standard AED shares core components designed for rapid deployment under stress.

  • Power Source: A long-life lithium battery that supplies energy for analysis and shocks, requiring regular self-test checks.
  • Electrodes (Pads): Adhesive pads equipped with metal plates and conductive gel that attach to the bare chest to sense rhythm and deliver energy.
  • Connecting Cables: Wires linking the pads to the main processor unit.
  • Indicator Lights and Speaker: Visual prompts and voice instructions that guide the rescuer through each step of the rescue protocol.
  • Shock Button: A manual push-button that the user presses when prompted by the device, though fully automated models deliver shocks without user input.

Step-by-Step Operation and Application Protocol

  1. Power On: Turn on the AED immediately by pressing the power button or opening the lid.
  2. Expose the Chest: Quickly cut or tear away clothing to bare the chest completely.
  3. Prepare the Skin: Wipe the chest dry if it is wet, and clip excessive chest hair if it prevents the pads from sticking.
  4. Apply Pads: Place the upper-right pad below the right collarbone and the lower-left pad on the lower left rib cage.
  5. Analyze Rhythm: Clear the patient and let the machine analyze the heart rhythm. Ensure no one touches the patient.
  6. Deliver Shock: If advised, ensure everyone is clear of the patient, shout a clear warning, and press the shock button.
  7. Resume CPR: Immediately start chest compressions and ventilations following the shock delivery.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Rescuers must adapt AED use for specific environmental and patient conditions to ensure safety and effectiveness.

ConditionAction Required
Implanted Pacemaker/ICDPlace pads at least one inch away from the device bulge to avoid damaging the unit.
Medication PatchesRemove the patch with gloved hands and wipe the skin clean before placing the pad.
Excessive Chest HairShave the area rapidly if pads fail to stick well and create an air pocket.
Metal Surfaces / WaterMove the patient off conductive metal sheets and out of puddles to prevent shock hazard to bystanders.

Troubleshooting Common AED Errors and Obstacles

Equipment failures and environmental challenges can interrupt an emergency response. If the AED prompts 'Check Pads' or 'Poor Connection,' the rescuer should firmly press down on the adhesive edges to eliminate air pockets. If a low battery warning occurs, replace the unit or battery immediately if a backup is available. When pediatric patients under eight years old or under 25 kilograms require defibrillation, pediatric pads and an attenuator should be used if available; if not, standard adult pads are acceptable as a last resort, ensuring they do not overlap on a small chest.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Handover

Once the patient achieves a return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), the nurse shifts focus to stabilization, monitoring, and detailed handover. Maintain airway patency, administer supplemental oxygen as ordered, and monitor vital signs continuously. Do not remove the AED pads immediately, as the patient may re-enter a shockable rhythm; leave them attached and connected to the device in monitoring mode. During clinical handover to the advanced medical team, provide a concise SBAR report detailing total downtime, number of shocks delivered, medications administered, and the patient's current hemodynamic status.
