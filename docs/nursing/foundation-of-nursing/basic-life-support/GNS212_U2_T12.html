Intermittent Suction of the Tracheobronchial Tree

Introduction and Purpose of Tracheobronchial Suctioning

Tracheobronchial suctioning is an invasive procedure designed to clear accumulated mucus, blood, and other secretions from the lower airways when a patient cannot effectively cough them up independently. This intervention is commonly performed on individuals with an artificial airway, such as an endotracheal tube or a tracheostomy, or those with neuromuscular weakness and suppressed cough reflexes. The primary goal is maintaining airway patency, improving gas exchange, and preventing lower respiratory tract infections caused by retained secretions. Applying suction intermittently rather than continuously protects the delicate mucosal lining of the trachea and bronchi from excessive trauma and sudden pressure drops.

Indications and When to Avoid Suctioning

A nurse must carefully determine whether a patient actually needs airway suctioning. Routine suctioning without clinical signs causes unnecessary trauma and hypoxemia.

  • Visible secretions: Mucus is seen in the artificial airway.
  • Coarse breath sounds: Rhonchi or crackles are heard over the trachea or bronchi.
  • Respiratory distress: Sudden drop in oxygen saturation or increased work of breathing.
  • Ineffective cough: The patient is weak and cannot expel mucus independently.
  • Copious secretions: Suspected aspiration of gastric contents or saliva.

Suctioning is NOT a harmless procedure and has critical boundaries. Severe bronchospasm, bleeding disorders, laryngeal spasm, and recent tracheal surgery serve as strong contraindications where the risks far outweigh any potential benefit.

Required Equipment and Preparation

Gathering proper supplies beforehand ensures the procedure is efficient and reduces the duration of airway disruption.

Adjust the negative pressure regulator to safe limits. For adults, the pressure generally ranges from 100 to 120 mmHg, while pediatric settings require lower pressures between 80 and 100 mmHg to protect fragile tissues.

Pre-Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before touching the airway, the nurse assesses the patient's baseline respiratory status, including oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, breath sounds, and heart rate. Explaining the procedure to the conscious patient helps reduce anxiety and promotes cooperation, even if they cannot speak due to an artificial airway. Hyperoxygenation is a mandatory preparatory step. The nurse delivers 100% oxygen using a manual resuscitation bag or ventilator settings for 30 to 60 seconds to build up oxygen reserves and prevent severe hypoxia during the temporary removal of airflow.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Intermittent Suction

  1. Don PPE: Put on clean gloves, mask, and goggles, then use sterile gloves for the dominant hand handling the catheter.
  2. Insert gently: Introduce the catheter into the artificial airway without applying suction until reaching the desired depth or resistance.
  3. Apply intermittent suction: Withdraw the catheter while rotating it between the thumb and forefinger, applying suction for no longer than 10 to 15 seconds.
  4. Rinse and repeat: Clear the tubing with sterile normal saline and allow the patient to rest and reoxygenate between passes.

A common misconception is that pushing the catheter as far as possible yields better results. In reality, deep insertion beyond the tip of the artificial airway risks severe mucosal damage. Advance the catheter only until resistance is met or until the carina is reached, then pull back slightly before applying suction.

Potential Complications and Nursing Actions

Tracheobronchial suctioning places significant physiological stress on the patient. The nurse must monitor for adverse events during and immediately after the intervention.

  • Hypoxemia: Caused by removing oxygen along with secretions; fix by stopping suction and providing manual breaths with 100% oxygen.
  • Cardiac dysrhythmias: Triggered by vagal nerve stimulation or low oxygen; pause suctioning immediately and give oxygen.
  • Mucosal trauma and bleeding: Caused by rough handling or excessive vacuum pressure; check pressure settings and use gentle technique.
  • Bronchospasm: Airway tightening in response to irritation; administer prescribed bronchodilators if ordered.

Post-Procedure Evaluation and Documentation

After completing the suctioning procedure, evaluate its effectiveness by reassessing the patient's breath sounds, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. The lungs should sound clearer, and the work of breathing should decrease. Document the procedure accurately in the patient chart, noting the date, time, amount, color, consistency, and odor of the secretions removed, along with how well the patient tolerated the intervention and any complications encountered.
