Introduction to General Examination of the Patient (Head-to-Toe)

Introduction and Purpose of General Examination

A general physical examination is a structured head-to-toe evaluation designed to gather baseline health data and detect objective signs of disease. The primary purpose is to identify clinical abnormalities early, evaluate the patient's overall functional capacity, and monitor changes in their clinical condition over time. Nurses perform this assessment upon admission, during routine shift changes, and whenever a patient reports new symptoms. It establishes a clinical baseline that guides subsequent nursing interventions and medical treatments.

Preparation of the Environment and Patient

Creating a suitable environment ensures patient comfort, privacy, and accuracy of findings. The examination room or bedside area must be warm, well-lit, and quiet to allow proper inspection and auscultation.

  • Privacy: Use bedside curtains or a separate room to maintain dignity and reduce anxiety.
  • Lighting: Adequate natural or bright artificial light is essential for observing skin color and lesions.
  • Patient positioning: Comfortably position the patient, explaining each step of the procedure to secure cooperation and reduce apprehension.

Essential Equipment and Infection Control Practices

Conducting a safe physical examination requires specific tools and strict adherence to infection prevention protocols. Standard precautions protect both the nurse and the patient from cross-contamination.

EquipmentPrimary Clinical Use
StethoscopeAuscultating heart, lung, and bowel sounds
SphygmomanometerMeasuring blood pressure
PenlightInspecting the oral cavity and pupillary light reflex
ThermometerAssessing core body temperature

Hand hygiene must be performed before and after every patient contact, and equipment like stethoscopes must be disinfected between uses.

General Survey and Initial Observations

The general survey begins the exact moment the nurse meets the patient, forming an overall impression of health status. This phase involves observing physical appearance, body structure, mobility, and behavior without using specialized instruments.

  • Apparent state of health: Note if the patient appears acutely ill, frail, or comfortable.
  • Level of consciousness: Assess orientation to person, place, and time.
  • Nutritional status: Observe for signs of cachexia, obesity, or fluid retention.
  • Posture and gait: Evaluate how the patient stands and walks for balance abnormalities.

Measurement of Vital Signs

Vital signs are critical quantitative indicators of physiological function. They include temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. These measurements must be taken accurately because sudden deviations often serve as the earliest warning signs of clinical deterioration. Pain is frequently evaluated alongside vital signs as the fifth vital sign, providing a more comprehensive view of patient comfort and stress response.

Systematic Head-to-Toe Physical Assessment Technique

A systematic head-to-toe assessment ensures no body system is overlooked. The standard physical examination sequence follows four core techniques: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. (Note: For the abdomen, auscultation is performed before palpation and percussion to avoid altering bowel sounds.)

The assessment starts at the head, checking the scalp, eyes, ears, nose, and mouth, then moves downward to the neck, chest, abdomen, extremities, and skin.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

Accurate documentation creates a permanent legal record of the patient's health status and ensures continuity of care among healthcare team members. Findings must be recorded objectively, avoiding vague terms like 'normal' or 'fine.' Instead, describe specific observations such as 'clear breath sounds bilaterally' or 'skin warm and dry.' Any abnormal findings must be reported immediately to the attending physician or senior nurse in charge.
