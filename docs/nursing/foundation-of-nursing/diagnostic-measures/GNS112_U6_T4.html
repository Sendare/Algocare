Collection and Observation of Specimens

Introduction and Infection Control in Specimen Collection

Specimen collection is a core nursing responsibility that aids in diagnosing diseases, tracking treatment efficacy, and monitoring patient health. Nurses collect various bodily samples, including urine, stool, sputum, and vomitus, and inspect them for physical abnormalities. Standard precautions are non-negotiable during these procedures to prevent cross-contamination between patients, healthcare workers, and the hospital environment. Hand hygiene must be performed before and after every procedure, and clean disposable gloves worn whenever handling body fluids. Personal protective equipment like aprons and face shields protect against unexpected splashes, a common risk during specimen handling.

Collection and Observation of Urine Specimens

Urine collection varies depending on the targeted test, ranging from random urinalysis to sterile midstream collection for culture and sensitivity. A midstream clean-catch specimen requires the patient to cleanse the urethral meatus, void a small amount into the toilet, and then capture the middle portion of the urine stream in a sterile container.

  • Random Specimen: Collected in a clean container for routine physical and chemical screening.
  • Timed Specimen: Requires collecting all urine produced over a set duration, such as 12 or 24 hours, often kept on ice or with a chemical preservative.
  • Catheter Specimen: Aspirated directly from the indwelling catheter port using a sterile syringe, never from the drainage bag.

Nurses observe urine for color, clarity, odor, and the presence of unusual particles or blood.

Collection and Observation of Stool Specimens

Stool specimens are collected to investigate gastrointestinal bleeding, parasitic infections, bacterial pathogens, and malabsorption syndromes. Instruct the patient to pass faeces into a clean, dry bedpan or a specialized commode hat without mixing it with urine or toilet paper, as both can contaminate the sample and alter laboratory results. Using a tongue depressor or collection spoon, the nurse transfers about one inch of formed stool or 15 to 30 milliliters of liquid stool into a clean specimen container. Observation points include color, consistency, odor, presence of mucus, pus, visible worms, or frank blood.

Collection and Observation of Sputum Specimens

Sputum specimens help diagnose respiratory infections such as tuberculosis, pneumonia, and bronchitis. The best time to collect sputum is early in the morning before the patient has eaten or brushed their teeth, as secretions accumulate overnight. Instruct the patient to take three deep breaths, cough deeply from the chest, and expectorate the thick mucus directly into a sterile sputum cup, avoiding ordinary saliva. Nurses note the volume, color, consistency, and presence of blood streaks or foul odors in the collected sputum.

Collection and Observation of Vomitus

Vomitus observation and collection assist in identifying gastrointestinal bleeding, poisoning, infections, or bowel obstructions. The nurse measures the total volume, notes the color, consistency, odor, and presence of partially digested food or blood. Coffee-ground vomitus indicates old, digested bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract, while bright red blood signals active hemorrhage. When laboratory analysis or toxicological screening is requested, collect a representative sample into a clean container immediately and send it securely to the laboratory.

Documentation, Labelling, and Transport of Specimens

A perfectly collected specimen becomes useless if it is mislabelled or delayed in transport. The specimen container must be labelled directly in the presence of the patient with their full name, hospital number, ward, date, time of collection, and the specific test required. Complete the laboratory request form accurately, noting any relevant clinical details or antibiotic therapy. Transport the specimen to the laboratory promptly to prevent bacterial overgrowth or cell degradation that can falsify test outcomes.
