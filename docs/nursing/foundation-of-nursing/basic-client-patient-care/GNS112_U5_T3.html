Oral Hygiene

Introduction and Definition of Oral Hygiene

Oral hygiene is the practice of keeping the mouth, teeth, and gums clean and free of disease. It involves daily habits like brushing and flossing, alongside professional dental checkups. In nursing care, oral hygiene is a fundamental component of basic nursing care that directly impacts a patient's comfort, nutrition, and overall systemic health. Neglecting the oral cavity allows microbial biofilms to accumulate, which can lead to localized decay and severe systemic infections.

Assessment of the Oral Cavity

A systematic oral assessment requires a penlight, tongue depressor, and gloves. Nurses inspect the lips, tongue, mucosa, gingiva, teeth, and palate for abnormalities.

  • Gums: Check for color, swelling, bleeding, or recession.
  • Teeth: Note missing, cracked, carious, or mobile teeth.
  • Mucosa: Look for lesions, ulcers, white patches, or unusual dryness.
  • Saliva: Assess for adequate moisture or thick, ropy secretions.

Common Oral Pathologies in Hospitalized Patients

Hospitalized patients face a high risk of oral complications due to immobility, nil per os (NPO) status, oxygen therapy, and medications that cause dry mouth. Recognizing these pathologies early prevents severe discomfort and systemic complications.

PathologyClinical Description
Dental CariesBacterial destruction of tooth enamel resulting from acid production.
GingivitisEarly, reversible inflammation of the gums characterized by redness and bleeding.
StomatitisPainful inflammation of the oral mucosa, often triggered by chemotherapy.
Oral CandidiasisFungal infection presenting as adherent white plaques on the mucosa (thrush).

Oral Hygiene Procedures for Conscious and Unconscious Patients

The approach to oral care changes entirely based on the patient's level of consciousness. For conscious patients, encourage independence by providing a soft-bristled brush, toothpaste, and an emesis basin at the bedside.

For unconscious patients, the primary hazard is aspiration. Position the patient in a side-lying recovery position with the head turned toward you. Use a padded tongue blade and sponge swabs moistened with water or diluted antiseptic mouthwash. Never use excessive fluid or put your fingers directly between the patient's teeth, as an involuntary bite reflex can cause severe injury. Suction equipment must remain ready at the bedside to clear pooled secretions immediately.

Specialized Care for Dentures

Dentures require careful handling because they break easily and are expensive to replace. Line the sink with a washcloth or fill it with water before cleaning to cushion a drop. Use a soft denture brush and specialized denture cleanser or mild soap—regular toothpaste is too abrasive and creates microscopic scratches that trap bacteria.

Store clean dentures in a labeled container filled with water or denture solution overnight to prevent warping. Never place dentures in boiling water or use harsh household bleach. Assess the underlying gum tissues for pressure sores or irritation caused by ill-fitting appliances.

Patient Education and Lifelong Oral Health

Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on maintaining oral health beyond the clinical setting. Effective teaching empowers individuals to prevent dental decay and periodontal disease through consistent daily routines.

  • Brushing Technique: Use a soft-bristled brush at a 45-degree angle to the gum line, using gentle circular motions for at least two minutes twice daily.
  • Interdental Cleaning: Floss daily to remove plaque from areas a toothbrush cannot reach.
  • Dietary Guidance: Limit sugary snacks and sticky foods that promote acid production by oral bacteria.
  • Regular Dental Visits: Schedule professional examinations and cleanings at least once or twice a year.
