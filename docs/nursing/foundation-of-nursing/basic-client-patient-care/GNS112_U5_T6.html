Pressure Sore

Introduction to Pressure Sores

A pressure sore, also known as a decubitus ulcer or bedsore, is localized damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue. It usually develops over a bony prominence as a result of sustained pressure, or pressure combined with shear and friction. In Nigerian clinical settings, patients with impaired mobility, such as those recovering from severe strokes or trauma in orthopaedic wards, are at high risk. These lesions range from mild skin redness to deep craters exposing muscle and bone. Early detection is vital because untreated sores can quickly progress to severe infections, sepsis, and prolonged hospital stays.

Common Pressure Points

Pressure points shift depending on the patient's primary position. When a patient lies on their back in the supine position, vulnerable areas include the occiput, scapulae, elbows, sacrum, and heels. In the side-lying position, pressure concentrates over the ears, acromion process, ribs, greater trochanter, knees, and ankles. Patients sitting in wheelchairs frequently develop sores at the ischial tuberosities, sacrum, and coccyx. Nurses must inspect these specific anatomical zones routinely during every shift change.

Causes and Risk Factors

The development of a pressure sore relies on mechanical forces and physiological vulnerabilities.

  • Pressure: Vertical force that compresses blood vessels, causing local ischemia.
  • Friction: Surface damage that occurs when skin rubs against bed sheets or rough materials.
  • Shear: Internal mechanical force created when skin stays stationary while underlying bone and muscle slide downward.
  • Moisture: Incontinence or heavy perspiration softens the epidermis, increasing vulnerability to friction.
  • Malnutrition: Protein-energy malnutrition impairs tissue healing and reduces skin resilience.

Stages and Assessment of Pressure Sores

Pressure injuries are categorized into stages based on the depth of tissue destruction observed during physical assessment.

  • Stage 1: Intact skin with non-blanchable redness over a localized area, usually over a bony prominence.
  • Stage 2: Partial-thickness loss of skin with an exposed dermis; the wound bed is pink or red and moist.
  • Stage 3: Full-thickness loss of skin, where adipose tissue is visible and granulation tissue is present.
  • Stage 4: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss with exposed or directly palpable bone, tendon, or muscle.
  • Unstageable: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss where the extent cannot be confirmed because it is obscured by slough or eschar.

Nurses use tools like the Braden Scale during admission to evaluate sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear.

Treatment and Wound Management

Managing an established pressure sore requires a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach. The primary step involves pressure relief by repositioning the patient and using specialized support surfaces. Wound care protocols mandate thorough cleansing with normal saline, avoiding harsh antiseptics like hydrogen peroxide that damage fragile new tissue. Debridement removes necrotic tissue to promote healing, which can be autolytic, enzymatic, or sharp. Dressings are chosen based on exudate levels, keeping the wound bed moist while protecting the surrounding skin from maceration. Nutritional support rich in proteins and vitamin C is essential for cellular repair.

Prevention Strategies in Nursing Care

Prevention remains the gold standard in nursing practice, saving patients from pain and hospitals from prolonged care costs. Nurses must turn bedbound patients every two hours and encourage wheelchair-bound patients to shift weight every fifteen minutes. Keeping skin clean and dry, especially after episodes of incontinence, prevents moisture-associated skin damage. Barrier creams protect the epidermis from moisture. Using pillows or foam wedges to keep bony prominences from direct contact with each other is equally effective. Finally, documenting risk assessments and turning schedules ensures consistent care across all nursing shifts.
