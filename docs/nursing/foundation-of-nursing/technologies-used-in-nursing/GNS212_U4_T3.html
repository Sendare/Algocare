Infusion Pumps

Introduction to Infusion Pumps and Their Purpose in Nursing

An infusion pump is a medical device used to deliver fluids into a patient's body in a controlled manner. Nurses utilize these devices primarily in hospital wards, intensive care units, and emergency settings to administer medications, total parenteral nutrition, blood products, and large volumes of intravenous fluids. Unlike gravity drips, which rely on height and can be affected by patient movement or vein position, infusion pumps use positive pressure to push fluids through tubing at an exact rate. This precision prevents dangerous fluctuations in drug delivery, especially for high-alert medications like vasopressors or insulin where a small error can cause severe clinical complications.

Types of Infusion Pumps and Clinical Applications

Various clinical scenarios demand specific types of infusion pumps.

  • Large Volume Pumps (LVPs): Used for routine intravenous fluids, blood products, and continuous medications where high volumes are delivered.
  • Syringe Pumps: Designed for precise, slow delivery of small volumes, frequently utilized in neonatal care or for titrating potent drips.
  • Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA) Pumps: Allow patients to self-administer small, pre-programmed doses of pain medication within strict safety lock-out intervals.
  • Elastomeric Pumps: Disposable, non-electronic devices that use a balloon-like reservoir to deliver continuous regional anesthesia or chemotherapy at home.

Programming and Calculating Infusion Rates

Setting up an infusion pump requires accurate mathematical calculations to convert ordered doses into pump settings. Nurses must frequently calculate milliliters per hour (mL/hr) based on drug concentrations and patient weight. Before starting any infusion, standard safety practices require independent double-checking of calculations and pump settings with a colleague, particularly for weight-based pediatric or critical care drips. Modern pumps often feature drug libraries with built-in dose error reduction systems that alert the nurse if a programmed rate exceeds safe clinical parameters.

Safety Protocols and Patient Monitoring

Using infusion pumps safely extends beyond initial programming. Nurses must regularly inspect the access site for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or extravasation, because positive-pressure pumps can continue pushing fluid into subcutaneous tissue even after a vein is blown. Power cords must be managed properly to prevent tripping hazards while ensuring the pump remains plugged into emergency power outlets during transport or power outages. Tubing connections must be visibly secure, and primary lines should be clearly labeled with medication name, concentration, date, and time of preparation.

Troubleshooting Common Pump Alarms and Errors

Infusion pumps sound various alarms when interruptions occur. An occlusion alarm indicates a blockage in the line, which could stem from a kinked tube, a closed roller clamp, or a clotted IV catheter. An air-in-line alarm triggers when gas bubbles are detected inside the cassette, requiring the nurse to pause the infusion and clear the tubing. An infusion complete alarm notifies staff that the targeted volume has finished delivering. Nurses should never silence an alarm without first identifying and resolving the root cause of the warning.

Infection Control and Maintenance of Infusion Lines

Maintaining sterility during infusion therapy prevents catheter-related bloodstream infections. Nurses must scrub all needless injection ports and hub connectors with an antiseptic wipe for the recommended duration and allow them to dry completely before access. Administration sets have specific manufacturer-recommended replacement intervals—typically every 96 hours for continuous crystalloid infusions, but much more frequently for blood products or lipid emulsions which support rapid bacterial growth. Regular inspection of equipment for cracks, leaks, or particulate matter ensures both mechanical integrity and patient safety.
