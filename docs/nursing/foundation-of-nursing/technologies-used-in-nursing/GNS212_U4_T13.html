Automated External Defibrillator

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs)

An Automated External Defibrillator is a portable electronic device that automatically diagnoses life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias of ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. The primary goal of early defibrillation is to restore a normal heart rhythm through a measured electric shock, stopping the chaotic electrical activity of the heart. Time to defibrillation is the single most critical factor determining survival in sudden cardiac arrest. For every minute defibrillation is delayed, the chance of survival declines significantly.

Indications and Rhythm Recognition

An AED is indicated when a patient is unresponsive, pulseless, and not breathing normally (exhibiting agonal gasps). The device is specifically programmed to detect two shockable rhythms:

  • Ventricular Fibrillation (VF): The most common initial rhythm in sudden cardiac arrest, characterized by quivering, ineffective ventricles.
  • Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (VT): A rapid, regular or irregular heartbeat that fails to produce a pulse or cardiac output.

Non-shockable rhythms, such as asystole and pulseless electrical activity, will prompt the AED to advise continuing CPR without delivering a shock.

Components and Accessory Kit

Standard AED units contain several essential items designed for immediate deployment during an emergency. Understanding these parts prevents delays in high-stress situations.

  • Power Unit: Usually a long-life lithium battery that supplies energy for analysis and shocks.
  • Defibrillator Pads: Adhesive electrodes equipped with gel and cables that transmit the heart rhythm and deliver the shock.
  • Preparation Kit: Scissors to cut clothing, a razor for chest hair removal, a dry towel for moisture, and non-latex gloves.
  • Indicator Light: A visual cue confirming the device has passed its daily or weekly self-test.

Step-by-Step Operation Protocol

  1. Power On: Open the lid or press the power button to activate voice prompts.
  2. Expose the Chest: Remove clothing and ensure the skin is clean and dry.
  3. Apply Pads: Place one pad on the upper right chest and the other on the lower left ribs.
  4. Clear the Patient: Order everyone to step back and avoid touching the patient during analysis.
  5. Deliver Shock: Press the flashing shock button if instructed, then immediately resume CPR.

Integration with Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

The AED and CPR work hand in hand. High-quality chest compressions circulate oxygenated blood to the heart muscle, making the heart more responsive to the defibrillation shock. Modern AED protocols prioritize minimal interruptions to chest compressions. When the device is analyzing rhythm or charging, compressions must pause briefly. As soon as the shock is delivered, or if a non-shockable rhythm is identified, chest compressions must resume without delay.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Certain environmental and physical conditions require modifications during AED use to ensure safety and effectiveness.

  • Water and Metal: Do not use an AED in standing water. Move the patient to a dry surface. Avoid placing pads directly over metallic medication patches or implanted pacemakers.
  • Paediatric Patients: Use pediatric pads or select the pediatric setting for children under eight years old or weighing less than 25 kg.
  • Implanted Devices: If a pacemaker is visible, place the AED pad at least one inch away to prevent equipment damage.

Maintenance, Inspection, and Troubleshooting

Reliability depends on scheduled checks of the unit. Nurses assigned to clinical units must inspect AEDs according to hospital policy, usually daily or weekly. The inspection involves verifying the status indicator light, checking expiration dates on pads and batteries, and ensuring the rescue kit is fully stocked. Common troubleshooting steps involve replacing expired supplies or swapping out batteries when low-power warning chirps occur.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Documentation

After successful resuscitation, nursing responsibilities shift to stabilization and comprehensive record-keeping. The nurse monitors vital signs, maintains airway patency, and prepares for transfer to an intensive care unit. Documentation must be thorough and accurate, capturing the exact time of collapse, number of shocks delivered, rhythm changes, and total duration of resuscitation efforts. Many modern AEDs record event data internally, which can later be downloaded for medical review.
