Cardiac Monitors

Introduction to Cardiac Monitoring in Nursing

Cardiac monitoring is the continuous observation of electrical activity in the heart. This practice allows nurses to detect life-threatening rhythm changes before symptoms become severe. In Nigerian healthcare settings, you will find cardiac monitors in emergency departments, intensive care units, and post-operative recovery rooms.

Continuous telemetry gives the healthcare team immediate insight into heart rate changes and dangerous arrhythmias. The nurse watches the monitor screen at the bedside or at a central nursing station, staying alert to sudden changes in the patient's hemodynamic status.

Basic Principles of Electrocardiography and Waveforms

An electrocardiogram reflects the electrical impulses traveling through the heart muscle. The standard waveform consists of specific deflections representing depolarization and repolarization.

  • P wave: Represents atrial depolarization, the spread of electrical impulse through the right and left atria.
  • QRS complex: Represents ventricular depolarization, showing electrical activation sweeping through the thick ventricular walls.
  • T wave: Represents ventricular repolarization, the recovery phase where heart muscle prepares for the next beat.

Electrical signals moving toward a positive electrode produce an upward deflection on the monitor paper or screen.

Types of Cardiac Monitors and Lead Placement

Cardiac monitors range from simple bedside units to ambulatory telemetry packs worn by mobile patients. Obtaining a clear signal depends entirely on proper electrode placement.

Skin preparation is crucial before sticking electrodes. Oils and dead skin cells block electrical signals, creating a poor tracing on the monitor.

Patient Preparation and Skin Care

Good monitor tracings start with careful preparation of the patient's skin. Oily skin, sweat, and chest hair interfere with electrical conduction between the skin and the electrode gel.

Clip excess chest hair rather than shaving it to prevent skin micro-abrasions that can cause infection or patient discomfort. Wash oily skin with soap and water, then dry it completely before applying disposable electrodes. Press firmly around the adhesive edge to ensure full contact. Change electrodes every twenty-four hours to prevent skin breakdown and maintain clear signal quality.

Interpreting Normal Sinus Rhythm vs. Arrhythmias

The nurse must quickly distinguish between a normal heart rhythm and dangerous electrical disturbances. Normal sinus rhythm originates in the sinoatrial node with a regular rate between 60 and 100 beats per minute, accompanied by normal P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves.

Deviations from this pattern require immediate clinical reasoning. Ventricular fibrillation shows chaotic, irregular waves with no recognizable QRS complexes, indicating cardiac arrest. Asystole presents as a flat line on the monitor, meaning complete absence of electrical activity. Recognizing these patterns dictates whether you call a code blue or administer routine medications.

Troubleshooting Common Monitor Artifacts and Alarms

Monitor alarms can cause alarm fatigue if staff ignore them or fail to set correct parameters. False alarms often stem from patient movement, loose cables, or dried electrode gel.

Artifacts mimic dangerous arrhythmias on the screen. Muscle tremors create jagged baseline interference, while a loose lead wire causes erratic wandering baselines or flat lines. Always check the patient first before checking the machine. Assess whether the patient is talking, breathing comfortably, and has a strong pulse before assuming the monitor screen displays true cardiac arrest.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Safety

Safe cardiac monitoring requires balancing technology with direct human observation. Never rely solely on the monitor screen; always correlate rhythm findings with the patient's physical pulse, blood pressure, and level of consciousness.

Customise alarm limits for each individual patient based on their baseline condition and doctor's orders. Avoid turning off alarms permanently. Educate the patient and their relatives about the telemetry box, explaining that gentle movement will not break the machine, but wandering outside hospital signal range will lose the connection.
