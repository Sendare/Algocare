Pulse Oximeter

Introduction and Physiological Principles of Pulse Oximetry

Pulse oximetry provides a non-invasive method for monitoring a patient's arterial oxygen saturation, commonly referred to as SpO2. Unlike arterial blood gas analysis, which requires a painful blood draw and laboratory processing, this bedside tool offers continuous, real-time data.

The technology relies on two main physiological concepts:

  1. Light absorption: Oxygenated hemoglobin and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb different wavelengths of light. Oxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more infrared light, while deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more red light.
  2. Pulsatile flow: The device isolates the arterial blood pulse by measuring light absorption only during the surge of blood flow with each heartbeat, filtering out tissue, bone, and venous blood absorbance.

Components and Mechanism of the Device

A standard pulse oximetry system consists of a microprocessor unit and a sensor probe attached to the patient. The probe features two light-emitting diodes (LEDs) on one side—one emitting red light (around 660 nanometers) and the other emitting infrared light (around 940 nanometers). A photodetector sits on the opposite side to measure the unabsorbed light passing through the tissue bed.

The internal microprocessor compares the ratio of red-to-infrared light absorbed during the pulsatile cycle against a built-in calibration curve to compute the final percentage value displayed on the screen.

Clinical Indications in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

In resource-constrained environments across Nigeria, pulse oximetry serves as a vital triage and monitoring tool. Early identification of silent hypoxia prevents sudden clinical deterioration in busy emergency wards, maternal units, and rural health clinics.

  • Emergency Triage: Rapidly assessing patients presenting with acute respiratory distress, severe malaria, or trauma.
  • Maternal Care: Monitoring women with severe pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, or sepsis.
  • Pediatric Wards: Assessing infants and children suffering from severe pneumonia, bronchiolitis, or severe acute malnutrition.
  • Perioperative Monitoring: Tracking patients undergoing minor or major surgeries during and after general anesthesia.

Procedure for Obtaining an Accurate Reading

Obtaining a reliable SpO2 reading requires proper technique and preparation of the patient. Interference from movement, cold extremities, or physical barriers can lead to false or missing readings.

  1. Explain the procedure to the patient to reduce anxiety and movement.
  2. Inspect the chosen site—usually a finger, toe, or earlobe—ensuring the skin is clean, dry, and free of dark nail polish.
  3. Warm cold extremities gently if peripheral vasoconstriction is present.
  4. Position the sensor so that the light emitter and photodetector align directly opposite each other.
  5. Wait for the waveform to stabilize and the pulse rate to match the patient's auscultated apical or radial pulse before recording the value.

Interpreting Values and Clinical Correlates

Normal SpO2 levels in healthy adults typically range from 95% to 100% on room air. Values between 90% and 94% indicate mild hypoxemia, while readings below 90% signify significant hypoxia requiring immediate clinical intervention, such as supplemental oxygen therapy.

It is essential to correlate the numerical saturation percentage with the patient's overall clinical presentation. A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) may have a healthy baseline reading between 88% and 92%. Treating them to reach 100% can sometimes suppress their respiratory drive. Always evaluate the pulse wave amplitude alongside the digital readout to confirm signal reliability.

Factors Affecting Accuracy and Common Pitfalls

Several clinical and environmental variables can cause inaccurate pulse oximetry readings, leading to misdiagnosis or mistreatment. Nurses must recognize these common pitfalls:

  • Poor Perfusion: Hypotension, hypothermia, vasoconstriction, or cardiac arrest reduce peripheral pulsatile flow, making it difficult for the device to detect a signal.
  • Motion Artifact: Patient shivering, tremors, or restlessness confuses the microprocessor, resulting in erratic numbers.
  • Abnormal Hemoglobin: Conditions like methemoglobinemia or carboxyhemoglobinemia skew light absorption ratios, often showing falsely normal readings in patients who are actually hypoxic.
  • Ambient Light: Bright overhead surgical lamps or direct sunlight shining on the photodetector can overwhelm the sensor.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care

Monitoring a patient with a pulse oximeter extends beyond recording numbers on a chart. Nurses hold the responsibility of ensuring equipment integrity, verifying data accuracy, and advocating for the patient.

Routine care involves regularly changing the sensor site—every two to four hours for disposable adhesive probes or at least every shift for reusable spring-clip probes—to prevent pressure injuries, skin breakdown, or burns. Nurses must also troubleshoot alarm fatigue, ensuring that limit settings are tailored to each individual patient rather than silencing them indiscriminately.
