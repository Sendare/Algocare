Ambulation and Use of Wheelchair

Physiological and Psychological Benefits of Early Ambulation

Early mobility after illness or surgery speeds up physical recovery and prevents the dangerous complications of prolonged bed rest. When patients move, their cardiovascular, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems maintain tone and function.

  • Cardiovascular: Prevents venous stasis and reduces the risk of deep vein thrombosis by improving lower limb circulation.
  • Respiratory: Enhances lung expansion, promotes effective coughing, and lowers the chance of hypostatic pneumonia.
  • Musculoskeletal: Minimizes muscle atrophy, joint contractures, and bone demineralization.
  • Psychological: Boosts self-esteem, reduces feelings of helplessness, and fosters independence.

Pre-Ambulation Assessment and Patient Readiness

Before helping any patient out of bed, nurses must evaluate their physical and cognitive readiness to prevent falls and injury. Check vital signs, muscle strength, balance, and level of consciousness. A patient experiencing orthostatic hypotension, severe dizziness, or extreme fatigue is not ready to walk.

Always test sitting balance on the edge of the bed first, letting the patient dangle their legs for a few minutes while watching for pale skin, sweating, or complaints of lightheadedness.

Assisted Ambulation Techniques and Use of Assistive Devices

When patients require help to walk, nurses use specific supporting techniques and assistive devices such as gait belts, walkers, canes, and crutches. A gait belt gives the nurse a secure, non-painful handhold around the patient's waist to control balance.

  • Walkers: Provide maximum stability; the patient moves the walker forward, then steps into it.
  • Canes: Used on the strong side of the body to support the weak side; moved forward simultaneously with the affected leg.
  • Crutches: Require significant upper body strength; proper measurement prevents axillary nerve damage.

Wheelchair Selection, Fitting, and Safe Transfers

A well-fitted wheelchair prevents skin breakdown, postural deformities, and falls. The seat width should allow about two inches of clearance on each side of the user's hips, while the footrests must be adjusted so the thighs are parallel to the floor.

Safe transfers require careful setup. Lock both wheelchair wheels, position the chair at a 45-degree angle to the bed, and ensure the patient wears non-skid footwear. Pivot the patient toward their stronger side whenever possible during the transfer.

Propelling the Wheelchair and Navigating Environmental Obstacles

Patients with upper body strength can propel themselves by pushing forward on the upper rear of the wheelchair handrims. When strength is unequal, the stronger arm compensates by taking wider turns.

Caregivers pushing a wheelchair must use proper body mechanics. To cross a high threshold or go up a curb, tilt the chair back slightly using the tipping lever to lift the front casters off the ground before moving forward. When descending a steep ramp or curb, back the wheelchair down slowly to prevent the patient from pitching forward.

Preventing Complications: Falls, Fatigue, and Pressure Injuries

Mobility devices and ambulation programs carry inherent risks that require constant monitoring by the healthcare team. Falls remain the leading safety hazard during walking exercises, often triggered by rushing or environmental clutter.

Skin integrity demands vigilance, particularly over bony prominences like the sacrum and ischial tuberosities in wheelchair users. Patients must perform weight-shifting maneuvers every fifteen to thirty minutes to relieve pressure. Furthermore, monitor for physical exhaustion; pushing through severe fatigue compromises muscle control and drastically increases accident rates.
