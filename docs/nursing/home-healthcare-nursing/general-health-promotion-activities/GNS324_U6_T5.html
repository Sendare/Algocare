Falls Prevention

Introduction to Falls and Home Safety Risks

Falls represent a major public health challenge, particularly among older adults in Nigeria and globally. A fall is defined as an unintentional event resulting in a person coming to rest on the ground, floor, or other lower level. In community settings, the home is where most accidents happen due to familiar surroundings breeding complacency. Common environmental hazards include poor lighting, loose floor rugs, absent handrails in stairwells, and cluttered walkways. Nurses must recognize that a single fall often triggers a cascade of physical, psychological, and social decline, frequently leading to the dreaded post-fall syndrome where fear of another fall restricts daily activities.

Age-Related Risk Factors and Physiological Changes

Biological aging brings inevitable physiological shifts that compromise stability.

  • Proprioception: Decreased joint position sense makes it harder to detect body placement without looking.
  • Vision: Reduced visual acuity, poor contrast sensitivity, and cataracts blur environmental hazards.
  • Vestibular function: Inner ear degeneration impairs balance and spatial orientation.
  • Muscle mass: Sarcopenia selectively depletes fast-twitch muscle fibers needed for rapid corrective stepping.

Comorbidities like osteoarthritis, stroke, and peripheral neuropathy further compound these age-related declines.

Comprehensive Fall Risk Assessment Tools

Clinical assessment requires validated screening tools to quantify individual vulnerability. The Morse Fall Scale and Hendrich II Fall Risk Model are widely utilized in clinical and home health practice. These tools evaluate history of falling, secondary diagnoses, ambulatory aid use, intravenous therapy, gait, and mental status. Nurses must conduct these assessments upon admission, after any change in health status, and periodically during home visits. Identifying high-risk patients early allows for targeted multidisciplinary interventions before an adverse event occurs.

Environmental Modifications and Hazard Reduction

Modifying the living space is a cornerstone of secondary and tertiary prevention. Nurses should guide patients and caregivers to systematically remove or alter household dangers.

HazardRecommended Modification
Loose throw rugsRemove entirely or secure with heavy-duty double-sided tape
Dim lightingInstall brighter, glare-free bulbs and nightlights along pathways
Bathroom slick floorsPlace non-slip mats inside tubs and install grab bars near toilets
Cluttered pathwaysClear electrical cords and furniture out of walking routes

Small, low-cost adjustments frequently prevent catastrophic fractures.

Medication Review and Polypharmacy Management

Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—significantly increases fall risk through drug interactions, sedation, hypotension, and dizziness. Medications frequently implicated are known as FALLS-increasing drugs (FRIDs). These include sedatives, hypnotics, anti-anxiety agents, antihypertensives, and anticholinergics. Nurses must collaborate with physicians and pharmacists to reconcile medications, taper unnecessary prescriptions, and educate patients about side effects like orthostatic dizziness. Emphasizing timing adjustments can also minimize daytime sedation risks.

Mobility, Balance, and Exercise Interventions

Targeted physical activity remains one of the most effective evidence-based strategies for fall prevention. Exercise programs must focus on lower-limb strength, dynamic balance, and gait training. Practices like Tai Chi improve postural control by engaging core muscles and encouraging slow, deliberate weight shifts. Resistance training builds muscle power in the quadriceps and calves, which are essential for catching oneself during a slip. Nurses encourage routine physical activity tailored to individual functional capacity while ensuring a safe, supervised start.

Assistive Devices and Patient Education

Assistive devices such as canes, walkers, and crutches compensate for impaired balance and weakness. However, an improperly fitted device can worsen instability or cause upper-body strain. Canes are held on the unaffected side to support the weaker limb, while walkers require correct sizing where the handgrip aligns with the patient's wrist crease with elbows flexed at about twenty to thirty degrees. Patient education must emphasize proper usage techniques, routine maintenance of rubber tips, and compliance with recommended mobility aids during all ambulation.

Post-Fall Protocol and Emergency Response

Despite rigorous preventive measures, falls will occasionally occur, requiring an immediate, structured response. The primary nursing action after a fall is to assess for injury, vital sign instability, head trauma, and fractures before attempting to move the patient. If the patient is uninjured and conscious, they should be rolled onto their side, brought to a crawling position, and assisted to a sturdy chair using a safe lifting technique. Nurses must document the incident thoroughly, monitor neurological status, and report the event to the primary healthcare provider to prevent future occurrences.
