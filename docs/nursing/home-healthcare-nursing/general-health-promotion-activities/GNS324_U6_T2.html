Grooming: Bathing, Oral Care, Dressing and Toileting

Introduction and Assessment of Functional Status

Personal grooming encompasses daily activities that maintain physical appearance and personal hygiene, including bathing, oral care, dressing, and toileting. In home healthcare and hospital wards alike, nurses must first evaluate the patient's functional capacity before jumping in to help. Determining what a patient can do independently prevents unnecessary loss of functional skills, a common pitfall in routine care.

  • Independent: Patient performs the task safely without assistance.
  • Supervision: Patient requires oversight, prompting, or verbal cues.
  • Minimal Assistance: Patient does 75% or more of the work.
  • Moderate Assistance: Patient contributes about 50% to 74% of the effort.
  • Maximal Assistance: Patient contributes 25% to 49% of the effort.
  • Total Dependence: Patient contributes less than 25% or nothing at all.

Bathing Techniques and Skin Integrity

Bathing removes dead skin cells, sweat, and bacteria while stimulating circulation. The choice of bath—whether bed bath, partial bath, or tub bath—depends on the patient's mobility, medical stability, and energy levels.

When executing a bed bath, wash from the cleanest areas to the dirtiest areas. Start with the eyes using plain water, wiping from the inner to the outer canthus. Proceed to the face, neck, arms, chest, abdomen, legs, and finish with the perineal area. Water temperature must be checked carefully; lukewarm water prevents skin dryness and accidental burns, especially in elderly patients or those with peripheral neuropathy. Thoroughly drying skin folds prevents moisture-associated skin damage and fungal growth.

Oral Hygiene Practices

Oral hygiene prevents tooth decay, gum disease, and systemic infections like aspiration pneumonia. For conscious patients, brushing teeth twice daily using a soft-bristled toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste is standard. Unconscious patients require special precautions: position the patient in a side-lying position with the head turned toward you to prevent aspiration of fluids, and use a padded tongue blade if necessary. Avoid sticking fingers directly into an unconscious patient's mouth to prevent accidental biting.

Denture care is equally important. Remove dentures at night to give the underlying gum tissue a rest, and store them in a labeled container filled with water or denture cleaning solution so they do not warp.

Assisting with Dressing and Adapting to Limitations

Dressing promotes self-esteem and dignity, but physical impairments can make fastening buttons or pulling shirts overhead difficult. When a patient has unilateral weakness from a stroke, use the principle of dressing the affected side first and undressing the affected side last.

  • Affected side first: When putting on a shirt, guide the weak or paralyzed arm through the sleeve before the strong arm.
  • Unaffected side last: When removing clothes, take the strong arm out first, leaving the weak arm supported until the end.
  • Adaptive devices: Utilize button hooks, long-handled shoehorns, and Velcro fasteners to encourage independence.

Toileting Assistance and Incontinence Care

Toileting support requires balancing patient privacy with safety. Depending on mobility, patients may use a standard toilet, a bedside commode, or a bedpan. When placing a bedpan for a non-ambulatory patient, elevate the head of the bed slightly if not contraindicated, or ask the patient to lift their hips by bending their knees and pushing down on their feet.

Perineal hygiene following elimination must be performed from front to back in female patients to prevent urinary tract infections by avoiding the spread of fecal bacteria to the urethra. Skin exposed to urine or feces requires prompt cleansing with mild cleansers and barrier creams to prevent moisture-associated skin breakdown.

Infection Control and Safety During Grooming

Personal care activities frequently involve contact with non-intact skin, mucous membranes, blood, and body fluids. Standard precautions dictate the mandatory use of clean gloves whenever there is a risk of touching bodily secretions, open wounds, or soiled linens. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention to break the chain of infection, performed before and after patient contact.

Environmental safety is equally vital. Lock the wheels of beds, wheelchairs, and bedside commodes before transferring a patient. Ensure call bells are within reach before leaving the room, and keep bathroom floors free of puddles to prevent falls.
