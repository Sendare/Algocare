Fire Safety in Home Care

Introduction and Patient Vulnerability in Home Care

Fire safety in home healthcare presents unique challenges compared to institutional facilities like hospitals. Nurses operating in community settings cannot rely on built-in sprinkler systems, fire doors, or large shift teams to manage emergencies. The home environment belongs to the patient, making safety modifications subject to their consent and lifestyle.

Certain home care clients face significantly higher risks during a fire incident due to physical or cognitive limitations:

  • Mobility impairments: Bedbound patients, wheelchair users, or those requiring walking aids cannot evacuate quickly without physical assistance.
  • Sensory deficits: Hard-of-hearing clients may miss standard smoke alarm audio cues, while visually impaired individuals struggle to navigate in smoke.
  • Cognitive decline: Dementia or confusion can cause patients to hide during emergencies, misunderstand danger, or accidentally start fires.
  • Oxygen therapy use: Patients receiving home oxygen therapy create an enriched combustion environment where fabrics and skin ignite easily.

Common Fire Hazards in Residential Settings

Identifying domestic hazards allows community nurses to guide families proactively before a tragedy occurs. Cooking appliances represent the leading origin of residential fires, often caused by unattended pots or grease accumulation.

Electrical systems in older Nigerian homes frequently suffer from overloaded sockets, frayed appliance cords, and substandard wiring. Makeshift repairs using incorrect fuse ratings create severe short-circuit risks. Portable heating devices and cooking stoves placed too close to curtains, mattresses, or clothing also account for numerous preventable burns and property losses. Additionally, open flame sources such as candles used during power outages pose extreme risks when left burning near flammable bedding or mosquito nets.

Preventative Strategies and Safe Practices

Prevention requires practical lifestyle adjustments that respect the patient's autonomy while enforcing strict safety boundaries. Nurses must educate patients and caregivers on safe cooking habits, such as turning pot handles inward and keeping tea towels away from burners.

When electrical issues arise, advise families to unplug unused heavy appliances and avoid daisy-chaining multiple extension cords. For households using liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), cylinders should always stand upright in well-ventilated outdoor or semi-outdoor spaces, far from heat sources. Smokers receiving home care must be discouraged from smoking in bed, and caregivers should provide deep, non-tip ashtrays alongside water containers for extinguishing butts safely.

Smoke Alarms and Fire Extinguisher Management

Early warning systems buy critical minutes for escape during a residential fire. Smoke alarms must be installed on every level of the home and outside sleeping quarters.

Nurses should teach capable family members the PASS technique for portable extinguishers: Pull the pin, Aim low at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, and Sweep side to side. Emphasize that fire extinguishers are only meant for small, contained fires; if a fire blocks the exit or grows past wastebasket size, evacuation is the only safe option.

Developing a Practical Home Fire Escape Plan

A successful escape plan accounts for every occupant's specific functional capacity. Every home must have a drawn floor plan showing two ways out of every room, typically a door and a window.

Designated outdoor meeting places ensure that all household members are accounted for after evacuation. For immobile patients, the plan must outline who specifically is responsible for pushing the wheelchair or carrying the client. Families should practice this drill regularly, simulating low-visibility conditions by crawling low under smoke where air remains cleaner and cooler. Never use elevators during a structural fire; stairs represent the only safe vertical route.

Emergency Response Actions for Community Nurses

When a nurse encounters a fire while delivering care, instant decision-making is required. The priority sequence follows standard rescue principles: rescue anyone in immediate danger, sound the alarm, confine the fire by closing doors, and extinguish or evacuate.

If clothing catches fire, instruct the patient to stop, drop, and roll immediately, smothering flames with a heavy blanket or rug. Nurses must never endanger their own lives trying to salvage personal belongings or medical charts. Once safely outside, the nurse contacts local emergency services, provides clear address details, and reports any trapped occupants to arriving firefighters. Following an incident, document the event thoroughly and ensure psychological support is arranged for the shaken patient and family.
