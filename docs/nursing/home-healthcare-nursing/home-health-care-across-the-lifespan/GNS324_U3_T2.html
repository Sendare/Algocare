Physical and Psychological Changes in the Elderly

Introduction to Aging and Gerontological Nursing

Gerontological nursing focuses on the care of older adults, promoting optimal health, and managing chronic conditions associated with aging. Aging is a natural, progressive physiological process that affects every organ system over time. Chronological age, often set at 65 years and older, serves as a general benchmark, but functional age provides a more accurate picture of a person's health status. Nurses in this field must differentiate between normal age-related changes and pathological disease processes to provide effective care in Nigerian healthcare settings, where the aging population is steadily increasing.

Normal Physiological Changes Across Body Systems

Aging brings gradual structural and functional declines across all major organ systems.

  • Cardiovascular: Decreased cardiac output, stiffening of blood vessels, and increased systolic blood pressure.
  • Respiratory: Reduced chest wall compliance, weakened respiratory muscles, and lower vital capacity.
  • Integumentary: Thinning of the epidermis, loss of subcutaneous fat, decreased elasticity, and reduced sweat gland activity.
  • Gastrointestinal: Reduced peristalsis, decreased saliva production, and slower liver metabolism.
  • Musculoskeletal: Progressive loss of bone density and muscle mass, leading to joint stiffness.

Psychological and Cognitive Changes in Later Life

Cognitive aging involves varying degrees of decline in processing speed and short-term memory, though long-term memory and wisdom typically remain intact. Normal forgetfulness must be carefully distinguished from pathological conditions like dementia and delirium. Psychological well-being is often tested by major life transitions, such as retirement, loss of a spouse, and changing family roles. Depression is common in older adults but is frequently misdiagnosed as normal aging or overlooked entirely by healthcare providers.

Social and Environmental Factors Impacting the Elderly

Social isolation and loneliness pose major threats to the health and longevity of older adults. Retirement often leads to a loss of social networks, status, and regular financial income, which increases vulnerability. Environmental safety is equally crucial; poor lighting, loose floor rugs, and lack of bathroom grab bars elevate the risk of accidental falls. In Nigeria, changing family structures and the migration of younger relatives to urban areas or abroad mean more older adults live alone or without traditional family caregiving support.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment of Older Adults

Assessing an older adult requires a comprehensive approach that evaluates physical, functional, psychological, and social dimensions. Tools like the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) help screen for cognitive impairment, while functional assessments measure Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) such as bathing and feeding. Nurses must also conduct medication reviews to check for polypharmacy, a common issue where patients take multiple drugs simultaneously, raising the risk of adverse drug interactions and falls.

Promoting Independence and Quality of Life

Nursing care for older adults prioritizes maximizing autonomy and maintaining functional ability rather than encouraging dependency. Encouraging regular, age-appropriate physical activity helps preserve muscle mass and joint mobility. Nutrition education must address altered taste buds, poor dentition, and reduced caloric needs. Nurses collaborate with physical therapists, occupational therapists, and family caregivers to create supportive care plans that empower the older adult to manage their daily routine safely.
