Prevention of Gender-Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Home Healthcare

Gender-based violence (GBV) is a pervasive public health issue that frequently intersects with clinical practice. While hospitals and clinics provide controlled environments, home healthcare places nurses directly inside private dwellings where violence often occurs. Nurses enter domestic spaces to provide wound care, administer medications, or monitor chronic conditions, making them uniquely positioned to observe family dynamics. The privacy of the home can shield perpetrators, leaving victims isolated and vulnerable. Recognizing GBV as a clinical emergency rather than a private domestic affair is the first step toward effective intervention.

Forms and Dynamics of Abuse

Gender-based violence manifests in multiple ways beyond physical assault. Understanding these categories helps nurses identify non-obvious forms of harm during home visits.

  • Physical Abuse: Infliction of bodily pain, injury, or forced physical restraint.
  • Emotional and Psychological Abuse: Constant belittling, intimidation, threats, and isolation from friends or family.
  • Sexual Abuse: Any non-consensual sexual contact or coercion.
  • Economic Abuse: Controlling access to money, withholding funds, or sabotaging employment opportunities.

The cycle of violence often traps victims through fear, financial dependency, and psychological manipulation, challenging the assumption that victims can simply leave.

Identifying Vulnerable Populations and Risk Factors

Certain individuals face heightened exposure to gender-based violence due to societal inequalities and physical limitations. In Nigeria and globally, structural power imbalances disproportionately affect women and girls. However, men, boys, and gender-nonconforming individuals also experience abuse. Additional risk factors in home care include physical disability, cognitive impairment, extreme poverty, and social isolation. A patient dependent on an abusive caregiver for daily activities of living faces severe barriers to reporting abuse or seeking safety.

Recognizing Clinical Indicators and Signs of Abuse

Clinical indicators of abuse can be subtle or overt. Nurses must look beyond the primary medical diagnosis and observe behavioral and physical cues during home assessments.

  • Physical Signs: Unexplained bruises in various stages of healing, patterned injuries, frequent late presentations for care, or recurrent urinary tract infections suggesting sexual trauma.
  • Behavioral Cues: An overly dominant partner who insists on answering all questions, the patient appearing excessively frightened or guarded, or sudden withdrawal during discussions.

A common misconception is that victims will readily disclose abuse if asked directly. In reality, fear of retaliation often prompts concealment.

Screening and Assessment Strategies

Conducting a safe screening requires careful planning. The nurse must interview the patient privately, away from family members or suspected perpetrators, even if it requires creative excuses such as assisting with a bathroom trip or fetching a supply from the car. Direct, non-judgmental questioning builds trust. Asking open-ended questions like, "How things are at home?" or "Do you feel safe with your partner?" opens the door for disclosure. Documenting objective findings with exact quotes and clear photographs, where legally permitted, preserves crucial evidence.

Crisis Intervention and Safety Planning

When a patient discloses abuse or faces imminent danger, immediate crisis intervention takes priority over routine care plans. The nurse must validate the patient's feelings and prioritize physical safety. Collaborating with the patient on a safety plan involves identifying a safe place to go, packing an emergency bag with vital documents, medication, and money, and agreeing on a secret code word to alert neighbors or family members. Safety plans must be realistic and tailored to the patient's specific domestic reality.

Legal Frameworks and Multidisciplinary Referral

Nurses operate within legal and ethical boundaries regarding abuse disclosure. Mandatory reporting laws vary by region, but protecting vulnerable patients remains a professional obligation. In Nigeria, acts like the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act provide legal recourse. However, patient autonomy must be respected unless immediate danger to life exists. Effective management requires a multidisciplinary referral network involving social workers, legal aid organizations, law enforcement, and local shelters to provide comprehensive support.
