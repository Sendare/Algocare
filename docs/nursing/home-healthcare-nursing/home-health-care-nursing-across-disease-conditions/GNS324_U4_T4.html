Cerebral Palsy

Introduction and Definition of Cerebral Palsy

Cerebral palsy refers to a group of permanent disorders affecting the development of movement and posture. These motor impairments stem from non-progressive disturbances occurring in the developing fetal or infant brain. While the underlying brain lesion does not worsen over time, the physical manifestations often change as the child grows and matures. This condition represents the most common cause of physical disability in pediatric practice across Nigeria.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Brain injury leading to cerebral palsy can occur during the prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal periods.

  • Prenatal factors: Maternal infections like rubella or toxoplasmosis, gestational diabetes, and severe placental insufficiency.
  • Perinatal factors: Birth asphyxia, extreme prematurity, low birth weight, and neonatal jaundice (kernicterus).
  • Postnatal factors: Bacterial meningitis, traumatic head injury, and severe viral encephalitis within the first few years of life.

Pathophysiology and Types of Cerebral Palsy

Pathological changes typically involve damage to the motor cortex, basal ganglia, or cerebellum. Clinically, cerebral palsy divides into distinct types based on the nature of the movement disorder:

  1. Spastic: Characterized by hypertonicity, stiff muscles, and exaggerated deep tendon reflexes, representing the most prevalent form.
  2. Dyskinetic: Involves involuntary, writhing, or abrupt movements due to basal ganglia involvement.
  3. Ataxic: Marked by poor balance, lack of coordination, and unsteady gait originating from cerebellar damage.
  4. Mixed: Exhibits a combination of spastic and dyskinetic features.

Clinical Manifestations and Developmental Milestones

Children with cerebral palsy display delayed achievement of major motor milestones such as rolling, sitting, crawling, and walking. Nurses must look for persistent primitive reflexes, abnormal posturing, asymmetrical crawling, or favoring one side of the body early in infancy. Feeding difficulties, delayed speech development, and cognitive impairments may also accompany the primary motor deficits.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Comprehensive nursing assessment involves detailed developmental screening, neurological examination, and observation of muscle tone. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on neuroimaging such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to identify structural brain lesions. Nurses also assess nutritional status, swallowing safety, and the presence of associated sensory deficits like vision or hearing impairments.

Home Healthcare Management and Interprofessional Care

Managing cerebral palsy requires a collaborative team including pediatricians, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech pathologists, and nurses. Physiotherapy focuses on preventing contractures and maintaining joint mobility through stretching and positioning. Nurses assist families with home care routines, administration of medications for spasticity, and guidance on assistive devices like braces and wheelchairs.

Family Education and Psychosocial Support

Caring for a child with chronic physical disabilities places significant emotional and financial strain on families. Nurses must provide empathetic education regarding daily care, feeding techniques, and positioning to prevent pressure injuries. Connecting families with support groups and community resources helps alleviate caregiver burnout and promotes positive coping strategies.

Potential Complications and Long-term Prognosis

Long-term outlook varies depending on the severity and type of cerebral palsy. Common complications include joint contractures, scoliosis, hip dislocation, nutritional deficiencies from dysphagia, and chronic pain. With appropriate multidisciplinary interventions and adaptive equipment, many individuals achieve a degree of independence and participate meaningfully in their communities.
