Fractures

Introduction and Classification of Bone Fractures

A fracture is a break or disruption in the continuity of a bone. In Nigerian clinical settings, nurses encounter fractures resulting from road traffic accidents, falls, and pathological conditions like osteoporosis or osteomyelitis. Bones possess a remarkable capacity for self-repair, but proper initial classification dictates the entire treatment trajectory.

  • Closed (Simple): The bone breaks, but the overlying skin remains intact, minimizing immediate infection risks.
  • Open (Compound): The broken bone protrudes through the skin or a severe wound leads down to the fracture site, demanding urgent surgical debridement.
  • Complete: The break goes entirely through the bone, dividing it into two or more distinct pieces.
  • Incomplete: The bone is fractured only partially, commonly seen in children as greenstick fractures due to their more flexible bones.

Clinical Manifestations and Initial Assessment

When a bone breaks, the body reacts immediately with local tissue trauma, bleeding, and nerve irritation. Nurses must perform a rapid, systematic assessment to identify injury severity and prevent further neurovascular compromise.

  1. Pain and Tenderness: Caused by muscle spasms, direct tissue trauma, and pressure on sensory nerves near the fracture site.
  2. Deformity: Abnormal positioning or unnatural angle of the affected limb resulting from muscle pull and bone displacement.
  3. Loss of Function: Inability to move the extremity normally due to structural instability and severe pain.
  4. Crepitus: A crackling sensation or sound heard when broken bone ends rub against each other.
  5. Swelling and Ecchymosis: Rapid accumulation of blood and serous fluid in the soft tissues surrounding the injury.

Neurovascular Assessment and Complication Monitoring

Neurovascular compromise is a surgical emergency following a fracture. Swelling, tight casts, or displaced bone ends can compress arteries and nerves, cutting off blood supply and sensation to distal tissues. Nurses utilize the 5 Ps framework during every shift assessment:

  • Pain: Unrelieved by prescribed analgesia or pain out of proportion to the injury.
  • Pallor: Pale, cold, or blue skin color in the affected fingers or toes.
  • Pulses: Weak or absent distal pulses compared to the uninjured side.
  • Paresthesia: Numbness, tingling, or pins-and-needles sensation.
  • Paralysis: Inability to move distal digits actively.

Failure to recognize these signs early can lead to permanent muscle and nerve damage, or necessitate amputation.

Medical and Surgical Management

Fracture management aims to achieve reduction (setting the bone), immobilization to maintain alignment, and restoration of function. Medical management relies on closed reduction and external immobilization, whereas severe or unstable fractures require surgical intervention.

Nursing Care for Casts and Traction Devices

Casts and traction devices require meticulous nursing maintenance to prevent skin breakdown, infection, and mechanical failure. Plaster casts take up to 48 hours to dry completely; during this time, nurses handle them with open palms rather than fingertips to avoid indentations that can press against the skin and cause pressure ulcers.

For patients in skeletal traction, the nurse must ensure weights hang freely at all times and never rest on the floor or bed frame. Ropes must remain in the center of pulley grooves. Pin sites require sterile cleaning protocols to prevent osteomyelitis. Any foul odor, purulent drainage, or warmth around a pin site must be reported immediately to the medical team.

Mobility Support and Assistive Devices

Promoting safe mobility while protecting the healing bone is a central nursing duty. Patients learning to use crutches, walkers, or canes require thorough instruction to prevent falls and secondary fractures. When fitting crutches, there should be a two-to-three finger-width space between the top of the crutch and the patient's axilla to prevent pressure on the brachial plexus nerves.

When teaching stairs with crutches, a helpful clinical reminder is that the good leg goes up first, followed by the crutches and the affected leg. Conversely, when going down stairs, the crutches and bad leg go down first, followed by the good leg. Weight-bearing status—ranging from non-weight-bearing to full weight-bearing—must be strictly followed according to surgeon prescriptions.

Nutrition and Healing Promotion

Bone healing is a high-energy metabolic process requiring specific dietary components. Nurses educate patients and their families about the importance of balanced nutrition to accelerate osteogenesis and tissue repair.

  • Calcium and Vitamin D: Essential for bone mineralization and matrix hardening found in dairy, local leafy greens, and fish.
  • Protein: Necessary for collagen matrix formation and cellular repair; low protein delays callus formation.
  • Vitamin C: Required for collagen synthesis and connective tissue development.
  • Fluid and Fiber: Crucial for preventing constipation, a frequent side effect of immobility and opioid pain medications.

Prevention and Early Identification of Complications

Fractures carry systemic risks that can become life-threatening if ignored. Nurses must monitor for major complications throughout the recovery period.

  • Fat Embolism Syndrome (FES): Occurs when fat globules from the yellow bone marrow are released into the bloodstream, blocking small blood vessels in the lungs and brain. It presents with respiratory distress, tachycardia, and petechiae over the chest and neck.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Blood clots forming in deep leg veins due to immobility and vessel trauma, potentially leading to pulmonary embolism. Prophylaxis includes early ambulation, compression stockings, and anticoagulants.
  • Infection (Osteomyelitis): Bacterial invasion of bone tissue, especially common in open fractures or contaminated pin sites, requiring prolonged antibiotic therapy.
