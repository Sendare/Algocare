Tuberculosis

Introduction to Tuberculosis and Transmission Dynamics

Tuberculosis is a chronic, communicable granulomatous disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It primarily attacks the pulmonary system, though extrapulmonary sites like the spine, lymph nodes, and kidneys are also susceptible. Transmission occurs almost exclusively via the airborne route. When an individual with active pulmonary disease coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings, they expel microscopic droplet nuclei containing the bacilli. These infectious droplets remain suspended in the air for hours, waiting to be inhaled by a susceptible host.

Once inhaled, these bacilli navigate past the upper airway defenses and settle deep within the terminal alveoli. The probability of transmission depends heavily on environmental factors, such as the concentration of infectious droplets in the air and the duration of exposure. Poorly ventilated rooms, overcrowded tenement housing, and enclosed hospital wards greatly accelerate the spread of the disease within Nigerian communities.

Pathophysiology: Latent versus Active Infection

Upon inhalation of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the body's immune system mounts a cellular defense. Macrophages engulf the bacilli, initiating an inflammatory response that wall off the bacteria into granulomas, or tubercles.

  • Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI): The bacilli are contained inside the granuloma in a dormant state. The infected person feels completely well, exhibits no clinical symptoms, has a normal chest radiograph, and cannot transmit the bacteria to others.
  • Active Tuberculosis Disease: The immune system fails to contain the bacteria. The granulomas rupture, causing tissue necrosis, cavitation, and liquefaction within the lung parenchyma. The patient becomes symptomatic and infectious.

A common clinical misconception is that anyone with a positive tuberculin skin test is automatically infectious. In reality, individuals with latent infection pose zero transmission risk.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Assessment

Pulmonary tuberculosis presents with a constellation of insidious, progressive systemic and respiratory symptoms. Nurses must systematically assess for these clinical indicators during home visits and clinic consultations:

  • Persistent Cough: Lasting longer than two weeks, initially dry but eventually producing purulent or blood-tinged sputum.
  • Hemoptysis: Coughing up blood, resulting from vascular erosion caused by expanding lung cavities.
  • Constitutional Symptoms: Low-grade afternoon fevers, drenching nocturnal diaphoresis, unexplained weight loss, and profound fatigue.
  • Chest Pain: Dull, aching thoracic discomfort exacerbated by deep breathing or coughing.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Accurate diagnosis relies on a combination of history, physical examination, microbiological assays, and imaging.

  1. Sputum Smear Microscopy: Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) staining reveals the presence of mycobacteria under a microscope, offering rapid initial screening.
  2. GeneXpert MTB/RIF Assay: A rapid molecular test that detects tuberculosis DNA and identifies resistance to rifampicin within hours.
  3. Chest Radiography: Identifies infiltrates, cavitary lesions, and lymphadenopathy, predominantly in the upper lung lobes.
  4. Tuberculin Skin Test (TST): Involves intradermal injection of purified protein derivative to assess delayed-type hypersensitivity, though it cannot differentiate active from latent disease.

Pharmacological Management and Directly Observed Therapy

Treating active tuberculosis requires a prolonged multidrug regimen to eradicate actively dividing bacilli while preventing the emergence of drug-resistant strains. The standard intensive phase relies on a combination of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol taken daily for two months, followed by a continuation phase of isoniazid and rifampicin for four months.

Because non-adherence leads to treatment failure and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), the World Health Organization advocates for Directly Observed Therapy (DOT). Under DOT, a trained healthcare worker or designated community treatment supporter watches the patient swallow every single dose of medication. This direct supervision bridges the gap between prescription and actual ingestion, safeguarding both the individual and the wider community.

Infection Control and Environmental Safety in the Home

Managing tuberculosis at home necessitates strict environmental controls to protect household contacts. The nurse must educate families on maximizing natural ventilation by keeping doors and windows wide open during daylight hours. Sunlight and fresh air rapidly dilute and destroy airborne droplet nuclei.

Patients should cover their mouths and noses with a tissue or handkerchief when coughing or sneezing, disposing of used materials properly. During the infectious intensive phase, patients should sleep alone in a well-ventilated room and limit visitors, especially young children and immunocompromised individuals, until sputum smears convert to negative.

Nutritional Support and Patient Education for Recovery

Tuberculosis is a severe wasting disease that places high metabolic demands on the body. Malnutrition worsens clinical outcomes and prolongs recovery. Nurses must collaborate with dietitians and families to encourage high-protein, calorie-dense local foods that support tissue repair and immune function.

Patient education forms the cornerstone of adherence. Individuals must understand that feeling better after a few weeks of treatment does not mean the disease is cured. Stopping medication early allows dormant bacilli to multiply, leading to a relapse that is much harder to treat.

Monitoring for Medication Adherence and Adverse Effects

First-line anti-tuberculosis drugs carry significant potential adverse effects that can disrupt treatment. Nurses must monitor patients closely during home visits and clinic follow-ups:

  • Isoniazid: Associated with peripheral neuropathy; concurrent pyridoxine (vitamin B6) supplementation prevents this complication.
  • Rifampicin: Induces liver enzyme activity and harmlessly turns body fluids (urine, sweat, tears) a bright red-orange color.
  • Pyrazinamide: Can cause hyperuricemia, leading to joint pain or gout.
  • Ethambutol: Can cause optic neuritis, manifesting as blurred vision or loss of red-green color discrimination.
