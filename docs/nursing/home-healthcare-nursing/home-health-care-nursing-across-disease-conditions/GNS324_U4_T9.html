Stroke-Related Neurological Impairment

Pathophysiology and Types of Stroke

A stroke occurs when blood supply to a part of the brain is suddenly interrupted or reduced, depriving brain tissue of oxygen and nutrients. Brain cells begin to die within minutes. Understanding the underlying mechanism guides acute management and rehabilitation planning in Nigerian clinical settings.

  • Ischemic stroke: Caused by a blockage or clot in a blood vessel supplying the brain, accounting for the majority of cases.
  • Hemorrhagic stroke: Caused by a weakened blood vessel that ruptures and bleeds into the surrounding brain tissue, often linked to poorly controlled hypertension.

Common Neurological Deficits in Stroke Survivors

The location and size of the brain injury dictate the specific functional losses observed in a patient. Hemiparesis or hemiplegia affects the side of the body opposite to the brain hemisphere that suffered the lesion.

Sensory loss, visual field cuts like homonymous hemianopsia, and neglect of one body side frequently complicate physical recovery. When the left hemisphere is damaged, language impairments such as aphasia often emerge. Right-hemisphere strokes tend to cause impulsive behavior and spatial-perceptual deficits.

Activities of Daily Living and Functional Independence

Restoring independence in self-care activities such as feeding, bathing, dressing, and toileting is a primary goal of stroke rehabilitation. Nurses evaluate baseline abilities using standard functional scales to track progress. Task breakdown, environmental modification, and the use of adaptive devices bridge the gap between permanent deficit and personal autonomy. Encouraging the patient to use their affected limb during daily tasks prevents learned nonuse.

Managing Mobility, Spasticity, and Fall Risks

Altered muscle tone presents significant challenges following a stroke. Initially, limbs may be flaccid, but over weeks, spasticity often develops, causing involuntary muscle stiffness and resistance to stretch. Physical therapy, proper positioning, and prescribed antispasmodic medications help manage tone. Falls are a major hazard due to poor balance, altered gait, and spatial neglect. Nurses must implement rigorous fall prevention strategies, including clear walkways, non-slip footwear, and supervised transfers.

Communication and Cognitive Rehabilitation Strategies

Language barriers resulting from aphasia frustrate both patients and caregivers. Expressive aphasia limits a person's ability to speak or write clear words, while receptive aphasia impairs spoken and written language comprehension. Cognitive deficits affecting memory, attention, and executive function further complicate interactions. Nurses should use simple sentences, allow extra time for responses, and incorporate non-verbal cues like gestures and picture boards.

Prevention of Secondary Complications in Home Care

Prolonged immobility and neurological deficits expose stroke survivors to secondary health threats. Pressure injuries develop rapidly over bony prominences when patients cannot reposition themselves. Deep vein thrombosis can occur in paralyzed limbs due to venous stasis. Furthermore, dysphagia places patients at high risk for aspiration pneumonia. Nurses must enforce regular repositioning schedules, encourage hydration, monitor for leg swelling, and ensure safe swallowing techniques during meals.

Caregiver Support and Education for Long-Term Recovery

Family caregivers shoulder a heavy physical and emotional burden during long-term stroke recovery at home. Burnout, depression, and physical strain are common among primary caregivers in resource-limited settings. Comprehensive nursing care includes training family members in safe transfer techniques, feeding safety, and personal stress management. Connecting families with local support groups and respite care options preserves the sustainability of home-based care.
