Anxiety Disorders

Introduction and Pathophysiology

Anxiety is a normal human response to stress, but when it becomes persistent, excessive, and interferes with daily functioning, it crosses into an anxiety disorder. The neurobiological basis involves a dysregulation of neurotransmitters in the central nervous system.

  • Norepinephrine: Often elevated, leading to hyperarousal, increased heart rate, and high alert states.
  • Serotonin: Typically deficient, impairing mood regulation, emotional stability, and impulse control.
  • Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA): The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter is often underactive, failing to calm neural activity.

The amygdala acts as the brain's emotional computer, triggering the fight-or-flight response even when no genuine physical danger exists. The prefrontal cortex, which should logically evaluate threats and calm the amygdala, fails to exert top-down control.

Classification and Common Types

Anxiety disorders manifest in several distinct clinical forms, each with unique diagnostic criteria.

  1. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD): Characterized by chronic, uncontrollable worry about everyday issues like finances, health, or family, lasting for at least six months.
  2. Panic Disorder: Marked by sudden, recurrent panic attacks accompanied by intense physical symptoms like chest pain and palpitations, coupled with a persistent fear of future attacks.
  3. Social Anxiety Disorder: Involves an intense, irrational fear of being scrutinized, judged, or humiliated in social or performance situations.
  4. Specific Phobias: Marked by an extreme, irrational fear of a specific object or situation, such as heights, enclosed spaces, or certain animals.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The development of anxiety disorders stems from a complex interplay of genetic vulnerabilities, personality traits, and environmental stressors. In the Nigerian context, unique psychosocial pressures significantly influence mental health.

  • Economic Hardship: Financial instability, high unemployment rates, and inflation create chronic, low-grade stress that wears down psychological resilience.
  • Socio-Cultural Pressures: Extended family expectations, societal demands for success, and communal living dynamics can heighten performance anxiety.
  • Trauma and Insecurity: Exposure to community violence, kidnapping risks, or sudden displacement leaves lasting psychological scars.
  • Genetics: A family history of mood or anxiety disorders substantially increases individual susceptibility.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Assessing a patient with anxiety requires evaluating both psychological distress and physical manifestations. Somatic complaints frequently mask the underlying psychiatric condition, often leading patients to seek help from general medical clinics first.

Pharmacological and Psychosocial Management

Effective treatment combines medication management with evidence-based talk therapies to restore long-term emotional balance.

  • Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs): Medications like sertraline or fluoxetine are first-line agents, though patients must be informed that full therapeutic benefits take several weeks to emerge.
  • Benzodiazepines: Drugs such as diazepam offer rapid relief for severe acute anxiety, but they carry a high risk of dependence and tolerance, making them suitable only for short-term use.
  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): Helps patients identify and restructure distorted thought patterns and gradual exposure to feared situations.
  • Psychoeducation: Teaching patients and their families about the nature of the disorder reduces stigma and improves adherence to the care plan.

Home Care Nursing Interventions

Nurses supporting clients within the home and community setting play a vital role in promoting daily coping strategies and preventing relapses. Establishing a calm, predictable environment is paramount.

  • Deep Breathing Exercises: Teach diaphragmatic breathing to slow the heart rate and activate the parasympathetic nervous system during early distress.
  • Grounding Techniques: Use the 5-4-3-2-1 sensory method to anchor a panicked patient back to the present moment by identifying sights, sounds, and textures.
  • Lifestyle Modifications: Encourage regular physical exercise, adequate sleep hygiene, and the reduction of excessive caffeine intake, which can mimic or worsen jitteriness.
  • Support System Engagement: Involve trusted family members in care planning while ensuring the patient maintains personal autonomy.

Complications and Crisis Management

Severe, unmanaged anxiety can spiral into debilitating complications that threaten overall health and social stability. Chronic hyperarousal exhausts the body, paving the way for secondary clinical depression, substance abuse as a form of self-medication, and severe social withdrawal.

During an acute panic crisis, safety is the primary nursing priority. The nurse must remain calm, speak in short and simple sentences, and move the patient to a quiet, low-stimulation environment. Never dismiss the patient's physical fears as imaginary, as the terror they feel is entirely real to them. If self-harm or suicidal ideation emerges due to overwhelming despair, immediate psychiatric referral and constant observation are mandatory.
