Nursing Homes

Introduction to Nursing Homes in Nigeria

Nursing homes, often referred to as residential care facilities or senior homes, provide 24-hour accommodation, medical oversight, and personal care for older adults who can no longer live independently. Historically in Nigeria, extended family networks served as the primary caregivers for the elderly. Urban migration, economic shifts, and changing family structures have strained these traditional support systems. Consequently, formal long-term care facilities are emerging, particularly in major cities like Lagos, Ibadan, and Abuja. Nurses working in these settings bridge the gap between acute medical needs and compassionate long-term living support, adapting Western-style geriatric models to fit local cultural expectations.

Types of Residential Care and Senior Living Facilities

Not all elderly care facilities offer the same level of assistance. Understanding these distinctions helps nurses place residents in the most appropriate environment based on their functional capacity.

  • Independent Living Communities: Designed for active seniors who require minimal help with daily routines but benefit from shared security and social amenities.
  • Assisted Living Facilities: Targeted at residents who need moderate help with activities like bathing, dressing, and meal preparation, but do not require round-the-clock skilled nursing.
  • Skilled Nursing Facilities (Nursing Homes): Provide intensive medical care, chronic disease management, and rehabilitation services supervised by registered nurses.
  • Memory Care Units: Specialized, secure units tailored for residents with dementia, Alzheimer's disease, or severe cognitive impairment to prevent wandering.

The Role of the Nurse in Nursing Home Settings

The nurse in a long-term care facility wears many hats: clinician, care coordinator, educator, and advocate. Unlike acute care where the goal is rapid stabilization and discharge, nursing home care focuses on maintaining quality of life, maximizing remaining functional abilities, and managing chronic conditions over months or years. Nurses supervise care assistants, assess subtle changes in a resident's baseline health, and maintain open communication with families. Building a therapeutic rapport with residents is essential, as the facility is their permanent home.

Comprehensive Resident Assessment and Care Planning

Admission to a nursing home triggers a thorough baseline evaluation. Nurses assess physical health, cognitive status, nutritional intake, skin integrity, and psychosocial well-being. Using these findings, an interdisciplinary team—comprising nurses, physicians, physiotherapists, and dietitians—develops an individualized care plan. This living document is reviewed regularly and updated whenever the resident's health status changes. A common clinical mistake is treating the care plan as a static document completed only upon admission rather than a dynamic guide that evolves with the resident's needs.

Promoting Activities of Daily Living and Mobility

Preserving independence is a cornerstone of geriatric nursing. Nurses encourage residents to perform as much of their self-care as possible, stepping in only to assist rather than completely take over tasks. Regular mobility exercises prevent joint contractures, muscle wasting, and deep vein thrombosis. Physiotherapists and nurses work together to ensure proper use of assistive devices like walkers, canes, and wheelchairs. Maintaining physical activity also boosts mental health and reduces feelings of helplessness among residents.

Medication Management in Long-Term Care

Older adults frequently take multiple medications concurrently, a condition known as polypharmacy, increasing their vulnerability to adverse drug reactions and drug interactions. Age-related changes in pharmacokinetics—such as decreased renal clearance and altered liver metabolism—mean drugs stay in the system longer. Nurses must meticulously verify dosages, monitor for side effects, and collaborate with physicians to review and simplify drug regimens. Pill organizers, medication carts, and strict administration protocols help minimize errors in the facility.

Prevention and Management of Common Geriatric Complications

Residents in long-term care face high risks for pressure injuries, falls, aspiration, and urinary tract infections. Preventive nursing interventions form the primary defense. Nurses implement turning schedules every two hours for immobile patients, ensure adequate hydration and nutrition to maintain skin integrity, and clear environmental hazards to prevent falls. Staff must also monitor for silent urinary infections, which often manifest as sudden confusion or delirium in older adults rather than classic burning symptoms.

Ethical, Legal, and Cultural Considerations in Nigerian Nursing Homes

Providing care in Nigerian nursing homes requires balancing professional medical ethics with deep-rooted cultural values. Respect for elders is paramount in Nigerian society, meaning staff must maintain high standards of dignity and polite communication. Families may experience guilt or social stigma when placing a relative in a facility, requiring nurses to offer supportive counseling. Additionally, nurses must navigate issues surrounding informed consent, advance directives, and end-of-life care in a manner that honors both the resident's autonomy and the family's cultural traditions.
