Skilled Care

Introduction and Scope of Skilled Care

Skilled care refers to high-level medical services that can only be performed safely, effectively, and independently by licensed health professionals, such as registered nurses and physical therapists. Unlike custodial care, which focuses on daily living activities like bathing and eating, skilled care targets complex medical conditions requiring continuous clinical evaluation. In nursing homes and long-term care facilities across Nigeria, patients admitted for skilled care typically suffer from chronic, disabling illnesses, recent major surgeries, or strokes. Recognizing the difference between these two care types ensures that healthcare resources are allocated properly and that patients receive the exact level of clinical oversight their condition demands.

Core Principles and Clinical Objectives

The delivery of skilled care is guided by specific objectives aimed at maximizing a patient's independence and preventing secondary complications.

  • Safety: Protecting vulnerable patients from falls, medication errors, and hospital-acquired infections.
  • Restoration: Helping patients regain lost physical or cognitive function through targeted therapies.
  • Maintenance: Stabilizing chronic conditions to prevent sudden health deterioration and unnecessary hospital readmissions.
  • Dignity: Preserving patient autonomy and respect during all nursing procedures.

The Interdisciplinary Team Approach

Providing effective skilled care requires collaboration among various healthcare professionals. The registered nurse often acts as the central coordinator, communicating patient changes to the wider team.

  • Physicians: Diagnose medical conditions and prescribe overall treatment regimens.
  • Registered Nurses: Oversee daily clinical care, administer complex treatments, and monitor responses.
  • Physical and Occupational Therapists: Work to restore mobility, strength, and fine motor skills.
  • Dietitians: Assess nutritional needs and design therapeutic meal plans.
  • Social Workers: Assist with discharge planning, family counseling, and financial resource navigation.

Assessment, Care Planning, and Goal Setting

Care planning in skilled nursing begins with a thorough initial assessment upon admission. Nurses evaluate physiological status, psychological well-being, nutritional state, and functional capacity. Based on these findings, the team establishes measurable, patient-centered goals. These care plans are not static documents; they require continuous re-evaluation. If a patient develops a new symptom or fails to progress, the nurse must update the care plan immediately to reflect the changing clinical reality.

Medication Management and Safety

Medication administration in skilled care units involves high-risk drugs, complex dosing schedules, and vulnerable elderly populations. Nurses must adhere strictly to safety protocols, including checking the right patient, drug, dose, route, and time. Polypharmacy is a frequent challenge in long-term care, requiring nurses to monitor for adverse drug reactions and drug-drug interactions. Furthermore, advanced procedures like administering intravenous medications or managing controlled substances demand meticulous documentation and secure storage.

Documentation, Legal Standards, and Quality

Accurate documentation is both a legal requirement and a cornerstone of safe clinical practice in skilled care. Every assessment, intervention, and patient response must be recorded promptly and objectively. Incomplete records compromise continuity of care and expose the facility to legal liabilities. Quality assurance programs use these documentation records to audit care standards, identify clinical errors, and implement facility-wide improvements. Nurses carry professional accountability to ensure their charting reflects truth and precision at all times.
