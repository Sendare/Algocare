Long-Term Care Facilities

Introduction and Types of Long-Term Care Settings

Long-term care facilities provide medical and personal care services to individuals who can no longer live independently. Residents often include older adults with chronic conditions, physical disabilities, or cognitive impairments. Facilities vary in structure depending on the level of independence the client maintains.

  • Assisted Living Facilities: Provide housing, support services, and minimal healthcare assistance for residents who need help with daily tasks but remain somewhat independent.
  • Skilled Nursing Facilities: Offer 24-hour medical nursing care, rehabilitation services, and intensive management of complex chronic illnesses.
  • Continuing Care Retirement Communities: Allow residents to transition smoothly from independent living to assisted living and skilled nursing within the same campus as their needs change.

The Role and Responsibilities of the Nurse

Nurses in long-term care facilities function as direct caregivers, care coordinators, and resident advocates. Unlike acute hospital settings where patient turnover is rapid, long-term care requires building sustained relationships with residents and their families. The nurse monitors slow, progressive changes in health status, administers medications, manages wounds, and supervises care assistants. Strong leadership and communication skills are essential to guide the multidisciplinary team and ensure individualized care delivery.

Resident Assessment and Care Planning

Structured assessment frameworks guide clinical practice in long-term care. Nurses use standardized tools to evaluate physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functioning upon admission and periodically thereafter. The resulting care plan addresses specific diagnoses, risk factors for decline, and individual preferences. Regular multidisciplinary meetings ensure the plan evolves alongside changes in the resident's health status.

Managing Chronic Illnesses and Geriatric Syndromes

Geriatric syndromes—such as falls, delirium, urinary incontinence, and pressure injuries—frequently complicate chronic disease management in long-term care. Nurses must recognize subtle indicators of failing health, such as acute confusion signaling a urinary tract infection. Proactive intervention strategies focus on maintaining mobility, optimizing nutrition, and preventing skin breakdown.

Promoting Person-Centered Care and Quality of Life

Person-centered care respects the values, choices, and preferences of the individual resident. In institutional settings, routines can easily override personal autonomy if staff do not intentionally prioritize choice. Nurses foster a homelike environment by encouraging residents to participate in daily decisions regarding meals, waking times, and social activities, thereby preserving dignity and emotional well-being.

Safety, Infection Control, and Regulatory Compliance

Residential facilities house vulnerable populations, making infection control and environmental safety critical priorities. Hand hygiene, proper sanitation, and routine immunization campaigns protect residents from outbreaks. Facilities must also adhere to strict regulatory standards regarding fire safety, medication administration errors, and resident rights to ensure a secure living environment.

Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Because long-term care facilities serve as the final home for many residents, providing compassionate end-of-life care is a core nursing responsibility. Palliative care focuses on symptom relief, pain management, and emotional support for both the resident and their family. Nurses collaborate with interdisciplinary teams to honor advance directives, manage comfort measures, and ensure a peaceful, dignified death.
