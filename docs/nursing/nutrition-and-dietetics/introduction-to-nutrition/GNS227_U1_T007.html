Classification of Foods

Introduction to Food and Nutrition in Nursing

Food is any solid or liquid substance that, when ingested, digested, and absorbed, provides the body with nutrients necessary for life, growth, and tissue repair. Nutrition encompasses the processes by which the body takes in and utilizes these food substances. For the nurse caring for patients in Nigerian clinical and community settings, understanding food goes beyond basic eating habits. It forms the foundation of medical nutrition therapy, supporting recovery from infectious diseases like malaria or typhoid, managing chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes, and preventing malnutrition.

Nurses assess dietary intake to identify nutritional deficits that delay wound healing or weaken immune responses. Food classification systems provide a structured framework to evaluate what patients eat, plan therapeutic hospital diets, and deliver effective nutritional counseling to mothers in antenatal and child welfare clinics.

Classification Based on Origin: Plant and Animal Sources

Foods are broadly grouped according to their natural source into plant and animal origins. This division helps nutritionists and nurses evaluate the diversity of a patient's diet and assess protein quality.

  • Plant-Based Foods: Derived from flora, including grains, legumes, tubers, fruits, and vegetables. They supply essential carbohydrates, dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals. While many plant foods contain protein, most are incomplete proteins because they lack one or more essential amino acids.
  • Animal-Based Foods: Derived from fauna, including meat, fish, poultry, eggs, and dairy products. These provide high biological value proteins containing all essential amino acids, alongside iron, zinc, and vitamin B12.

Classification Based on Functions: Energy-Giving, Body-Building, and Protective Foods

Grouping food by its physiological function helps patients and caregivers grasp the practical role of different meals in daily health.

  1. Energy-Giving Foods: Comprise carbohydrates and fats. They supply the calories required for basal metabolism and physical work. In Nigeria, staples like cassava, yam, maize, and rice serve as primary energy sources.
  2. Body-Building Foods: Comprise protein-rich items responsible for growth, muscle maintenance, and tissue repair. Examples include beans, meat, fish, eggs, and groundnuts.
  3. Protective Foods: Comprise vitamins and minerals that regulate metabolic processes and strengthen immunity. Fresh fruits and green leafy vegetables like fluted pumpkin leaves (ugu) fall into this category.

Classification Based on Chemical Composition: Macronutrients and Micronutrients

Chemical classification divides nutrients based on the quantitative amounts the body requires daily.

CategoryRequired QuantityPrimary ExamplesClinical Relevance
MacronutrientsLarge amounts (grams)Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, waterProvide energy and structural mass
MicronutrientsSmall amounts (milligrams/micrograms)Vitamins, mineralsAct as cofactors and enzymes

Macronutrients form the bulk of dietary intake and drive metabolic fuel pathways. Micronutrients, though needed in minute quantities, are critical; deficiencies lead to specific clinical deficiency diseases such as scurvy, goiter, or iron-deficiency anemia.

Classification Based on Nutritive Value: Food Groups

Nutritional science organizes foods into specific groups based on shared nutrient profiles to simplify meal planning and dietary education.

  • Cereals and Grains: Rice, maize, millet, and sorghum. They provide bulk energy through complex carbohydrates and B-complex vitamins.
  • Pulses and Legumes: Beans, cowpeas, soybeans, and lentils. They offer plant proteins, dietary fiber, and iron.
  • Roots and Tubers: Yam, cassava, sweet potatoes, and cocoyam. They serve as rich sources of starchy carbohydrates.
  • Vegetables and Fruits: Provide essential micronutrients, antioxidants, and roughage to prevent constipation.
  • Animal Products: Meat, fish, eggs, and milk. They supply complete protein, calcium, and heme iron.

Perishable vs. Non-Perishable Foods in Dietary Management

Food items behave differently regarding shelf life, storage requirements, and safety. Understanding these characteristics helps nurses guide patients on safe food handling and preventing foodborne illnesses.

  • Perishable Foods: Foods with high moisture content and nutrient density that spoil rapidly at room temperature. Examples include fresh meat, fish, milk, and cut vegetables. They harbor bacteria easily and require refrigeration, freezing, or immediate cooking.
  • Non-Perishable Foods: Foods with low moisture content that can be stored for long periods without rapid spoilage. Examples include grains, dried legumes, sugar, and salt. Proper dry storage keeps them safe from pests and fungal contamination like aflatoxins.

Clinical Application: Assessing Patient Diets Using Food Classifications

Nurses apply food classifications during patient intake assessments and dietary counseling. By analyzing a patient's 24-hour dietary recall through food groups, a nurse can spot nutritional imbalances. For instance, a patient presenting with generalized edema and sparse hair may be consuming an energy-rich diet derived solely from cassava (garri) while lacking body-building protein foods like beans or fish, pointing toward kwashiorkor.

Dietary counseling involves teaching patients how to combine local food items to achieve a balanced plate. Nurses translate these scientific groupings into practical language, ensuring patients understand how to mix cereals and pulses to improve overall nutritional status during recovery from illness.
