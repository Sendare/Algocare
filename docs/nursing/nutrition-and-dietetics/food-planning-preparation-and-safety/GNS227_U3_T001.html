Role of the Nurse in Promoting Nutrition for Inpatients

Introduction to Inpatient Nutritional Care

Proper nutrition is a core pillar of patient recovery in hospital wards across Nigeria. When patients are admitted, their metabolic demands often increase due to trauma, surgery, or infection, while their appetite frequently drops. Inpatient nutritional care bridges this gap, ensuring that the body receives adequate macronutrients and micronutrients to heal tissues, fight off pathogens, and maintain immune competence. Nurses spend the most time at the bedside, placing them in the prime position to identify early signs of poor dietary intake and champion adequate nutrition as a vital sign just like pulse and blood pressure.

Nutritional Screening and Assessment on Admission

Nutritional screening must occur within 24 hours of hospital admission to catch patients at risk of malnutrition early. Nurses use validated screening tools to evaluate recent weight changes, body mass index, and recent dietary intake patterns.

  • Weight History: Unintentional loss of more than ten percent of body weight over six months signals high risk.
  • Appetite Evaluation: Documenting the percentage of hospital meals consumed during the first few days.
  • Clinical Condition: Factoring in diseases that severely impair nutrient absorption or dramatically increase metabolic stress.

Collaborating with Dietitians and the Healthcare Team

No single healthcare professional manages complex inpatient nutrition alone. Nurses act as the central hub of communication between the patient, the clinical dietitian, the attending physician, and the speech-language pathologist. When a nurse notes that a stroke patient coughs during swallowing, they immediately alert the speech therapist and request a dietary consistency review. Similarly, nurses relay calorie count data to dietitians so they can adjust enteral formulas or therapeutic meal plans accurately.

Assisting with Feeding and Mealtime Management

Mealtime in the ward should be an organized, dignified experience rather than a rushed chore. Nurses prepare the environment by clearing away bedside commodes, removing unpleasant odors, and ensuring the patient's hands and face are clean before the tray arrives.

  • Positioning: Elevate the head of the bed to at least 45 degrees to prevent choking and aspiration.
  • Pacing: Allow adequate time between bites, watching for complete swallowing before offering the next spoonful.
  • Dignity: Engage in calm conversation, encourage self-feeding aids if available, and avoid rushing dependent patients.

Monitoring Dietary Intake and Hydration Status

Accurate documentation of what a patient eats and drinks is essential for clinical decision-making. Simply writing 'diet tolerated' is insufficient. Nurses estimate intake using percentages of meals eaten, such as a quarter, half, or all of the food provided. Fluid balance charts track every milliliter of fluid consumed orally or intravenously against output. Spotting dry mucous membranes, concentrated urine, or sudden weight fluctuations helps nurses catch dehydration before acute kidney injury develops.

Managing Enteral and Parenteral Nutrition Support

When patients cannot meet nutritional needs via oral intake, alternative routes become necessary. Enteral nutrition delivers nutrients directly into the gastrointestinal tract via nasogastric or gastrostomy tubes. Nurses check tube placement before every feed, assess residual volumes, and flush lines to prevent clogging. Parenteral nutrition bypasses the gut, infusing concentrated nutrients straight into the bloodstream via a central venous catheter. This specialized route demands strict aseptic technique and regular blood glucose monitoring due to high dextrose concentrations.

Patient and Family Education on Nutrition

Nutritional support does not end at hospital discharge. Nurses prepare patients and their families for home life by explaining specific dietary modifications, therapeutic recipes, and signs of complications. If a patient goes home with a feeding tube, family members must demonstrate competence in flushing the line, checking placement, and administering feeds safely before leaving the ward. Simple, culturally appropriate language ensures that recommendations match local food availability and family financial realities in Nigeria.

Evaluating Outcomes and Documenting Nutritional Care

The final step in nutritional nursing care is evaluating whether the interventions worked. Nurses track weekly weight trends, wound healing progress, and laboratory biomarkers like serum albumin or total lymphocyte counts. Clear documentation links nursing actions directly to patient outcomes. If a nutritional care plan fails to halt weight loss, the nurse updates the care plan and initiates a multidisciplinary review to protect the patient from clinical decline.
