Biochemical Assessment

Introduction to Biochemical Assessment in Nutrition

Biochemical assessment involves measuring blood, urine, or tissue concentrations of nutrients and their metabolites to evaluate nutritional status. This objective method detects early nutritional imbalances before physical signs and symptoms appear in patients. Unlike dietary histories or anthropometric measurements, laboratory tests bypass subjective reporting errors made by clients. Integrating these tests into routine hospital admission screenings helps Nigerian nurses identify hidden malnutrition early. Combining biochemical data with clinical evaluations provides a comprehensive nutritional profile for effective patient care.

Role of Biomarkers in Detecting Nutritional Deficiencies

Biomarkers act as measurable indicators of biological processes, pathogenic states, or pharmacological responses to therapeutic interventions. In nutritional science, static and functional tests serve as the two main categories of biomarkers.

  • Static Tests: Measure the actual nutrient level in body fluids or tissues, such as serum vitamin C or plasma zinc concentrations.
  • Functional Tests: Measure the physiological consequence of a nutrient deficiency, such as measuring enzyme activity that requires a specific vitamin cofactor.

Assessment of Protein-Energy Malnutrition: Albumin, Prealbumin, and Transferrin

Visceral protein status is commonly evaluated using circulating blood proteins synthesized by the liver. While historically popular, these proteins have complex kinetics that require careful clinical interpretation.

A common misconception assumes low serum albumin always points directly to protein malnutrition. In reality, hydration status, liver disease, and systemic inflammation heavily alter these values independently of dietary intake.

Evaluation of Micronutrient Status: Vitamins and Minerals

Evaluating vitamins and minerals requires targeted laboratory assays because these micronutrients operate in trace quantities throughout the body. Serum or plasma levels often reflect immediate dietary intake rather than true tissue stores. For instance, serum calcium is tightly regulated by hormones and does not drop until severe deficiency occurs, making it a poor gauge of dietary calcium intake. Iron status requires a multi-test panel including serum ferritin, serum iron, and total iron-binding capacity to distinguish true deficiency from anemia of chronic disease. Nurses must review these micronutrient panels alongside the patient's clinical presentation and medication history.

Hematological Tests for Anemia and Nutritional Deficiencies

Hematological evaluations provide essential clues about nutritional anemias caused by shortages of iron, folate, or vitamin B12. Key components of a complete blood count include hemoglobin, hematocrit, and red blood cell indices. Mean Corpuscular Volume measures the average size of red blood cells, guiding the diagnostic workup.

  • Microcytic Anemia: Characterized by a low MCV, commonly caused by iron deficiency or thalassemia.
  • Macrocytic Anemia: Characterized by a high MCV, typically resulting from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.
  • Normocytic Anemia: Characterized by a normal MCV, often associated with chronic disease or acute blood loss.

Inflammatory Markers and Their Impact on Nutritional Biomarkers

Systemic inflammation dramatically alters circulating levels of several key nutritional proteins through the acute-phase response. During injury, infection, or surgery, the liver shifts its protein synthesis priorities away from nutrient carriers toward inflammatory defense proteins. Positive acute-phase reactants like C-reactive protein increase rapidly during inflammation. Conversely, negative acute-phase reactants such as albumin, prealbumin, and transferrin decrease sharply, mimicking severe protein-energy malnutrition. Nurses must check C-reactive protein levels alongside any visceral protein assessment to avoid misdiagnosing an inflammatory response as a nutritional emergency.

Interpretation of Biochemical Data in Clinical Practice

Interpreting biochemical data requires a holistic view that integrates laboratory values with the patient's clinical history, physical findings, and dietary habits. Single isolated values can mislead the healthcare team due to biological variability or analytical errors. Nurses should evaluate trends over time rather than relying on a single lab draw. Medication usage, renal function, and hydration status must be factored into every nutritional evaluation. Collaborating with clinical dietitians ensures that biochemical findings translate into safe, effective nutritional support plans.

Limitations and Confounding Factors in Biochemical Testing

Numerous non-nutritional factors can distort biochemical test results, challenging clinical judgment. Renal impairment alters the excretion of metabolites, leading to falsely elevated or depressed values. Liver dysfunction impairs the synthesis of carrier proteins, making nutritional assessment difficult. Hormonal shifts during pregnancy or acute stress change plasma volumes and micronutrient distribution. Recognizing these confounding factors prevents inappropriate nutritional interventions and ensures patient safety.
