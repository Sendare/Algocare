Dietary Supplements

Introduction and Definition of Dietary Supplements

Dietary supplements are products intended to augment the daily diet. They contain one or more dietary ingredients, including vitamins, minerals, herbs, amino acids, and enzymes. Unlike conventional foods, these products usually come in concentrated forms such as tablets, capsules, softgels, liquids, or powders. In clinical practice across Nigeria, patients frequently use these products alongside prescribed medications, making it vital for nurses to understand what they are and how they function within the body.

Regulatory Framework and Safety Standards in Nigeria

The regulation of dietary supplements in Nigeria falls primarily under the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC). NAFDAC mandates that imported and locally manufactured supplements must undergo registration to verify safety and basic quality before public sale. However, gaps in enforcement and the prevalence of open drug markets mean that substandard or unregistered products still circulate. Nurses must educate patients on checking for valid NAFDAC registration numbers on product labels before purchase.

Common Types of Dietary Supplements and Their Purposes

Various supplements target different physiological needs. Vitamins and minerals bridge gaps in daily nutritional intake. Herbal preparations, such as garlic or bitter leaf extracts, are used for traditional health maintenance. Protein powders and amino acids support muscle recovery, particularly among athletes and recovering patients.

  • Multivitamins: Provide a broad spectrum of daily micronutrients to prevent mild deficiencies.
  • Calcium and Vitamin D: Support bone density and prevent osteopenia or rickets.
  • Iron supplements: Treat or prevent iron deficiency anaemia, a common concern in pregnant women.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids: Promote cardiovascular health and lower triglyceride levels.

Nutrient Deficiencies and High-Risk Populations

Certain groups in the Nigerian population face a higher risk of specific nutrient deficiencies due to physiological demands, socioeconomic factors, or dietary restrictions. Pregnant and lactating mothers require higher amounts of folate, iron, and calcium. Infants and toddlers consuming restricted cereal-based diets may develop protein-energy malnutrition or micronutrient shortfalls. Elderly individuals often experience reduced absorption capacity for Vitamin B12 and calcium. Recognizing these vulnerable groups helps nurses anticipate nutritional support needs during community outreaches and hospital admissions.

Potential Risks, Toxicity, and Adverse Drug Interactions

The public perception that natural products are completely safe is a common misconception. High doses of fat-soluble vitamins like Vitamin A and D can accumulate in the body and cause toxicity. Herbal supplements can also alter the metabolism of conventional medicines by inducing or inhibiting liver enzymes. For instance, St. John's wort lowers the efficacy of many drugs, while garlic or ginkgo supplements can increase bleeding risks when combined with anticoagulants like warfarin or aspirin.

Nursing Assessment and Patient History Taking

During admission or outpatient triage, nurses must actively inquire about supplement use. Many patients do not mention herbal remedies or vitamins during medication reconciliation because they view them as food rather than drugs. The nursing assessment should capture the specific brand name, dosage, frequency, and reason for taking each supplement. Uncovering this information prevents missed drug interactions and gives a clearer clinical picture of the patient's overall health habits.

Dietary Counselling and Patient Education Strategies

Effective patient education bridges the gap between commercial marketing claims and clinical reality. Nurses must advise patients to obtain primary nutrients from a balanced diet of local foods rather than relying entirely on synthetic pills. When patients do choose to take supplements, education should focus on adhering to recommended daily allowances, purchasing certified products, and reporting unusual side effects immediately. Emphasizing open communication ensures patients feel safe discussing alternative therapies with their medical team.
