Nutritional Needs of the Elderly

Introduction and Physiological Changes in Aging

Aging brings progressive alterations in body composition and organ function that directly change nutritional requirements. Basal metabolic rate slows down primarily due to the loss of active muscle mass, known as sarcopenia. Consequently, total daily energy needs drop, but the need for high-nutrient-dense foods remains high or even increases.

  • Decreased Lean Body Mass: Reduces overall calorie requirements.
  • Reduced Gastric Acid Secretion: Impairs absorption of certain vitamins.
  • Altered Sense of Taste and Smell: Can diminish appetite and interest in eating.
  • Slower Gastrointestinal Motility: Frequently leads to constipation and early satiety.

Macronutrient Requirements

Even though total energy needs decline, protein requirements per kilogram of body weight often increase to help preserve remaining muscle mass and support immune function. Carbohydrates should mostly come from complex sources rich in fiber to combat chronic constipation. Dietary fats are necessary for hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption, but saturated fats should be limited to protect cardiovascular health.

Essential Micronutrients and Supplementation

Older adults face distinct vulnerabilities regarding micronutrients. Decreased skin synthesis, reduced sun exposure, and atrophic gastritis impair the absorption of Vitamin D and Vitamin B12. Calcium is vital for bone density, working alongside Vitamin D to prevent osteomalacia and fractures. Iron deficiency anemia can still occur, though it is more frequently linked to chronic disease or GI blood loss rather than simple dietary lack.

Fluid Balance and Hydration Challenges

Dehydration is one of the most frequent preventable causes of hospital admission among older adults. Several physiological shifts contribute to this risk:

  • Blunted Thirst Mechanism: The sensation of thirst diminishes with age, meaning seniors may not feel thirsty even when dehydrated.
  • Decreased Total Body Water: Aging bodies hold less water reserve than younger bodies.
  • Impaired Renal Concentrating Ability: Kidneys lose some ability to conserve water under stress.
  • Voluntary Fluid Restriction: Some patients restrict fluids due to fear of urinary incontinence or mobility issues.

Nutritional Assessment and Screening

Early identification of malnutrition allows nurses to intervene before complications arise. Validated screening tools, such as the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), are routinely used in clinical and community practice to score risk factors like recent weight loss, mobility issues, and psychological stress. Anthropometric measurements, including mid-upper arm circumference and calf circumference, help estimate muscle mass when traditional height and weight are unreliable due to spinal curvature like kyphosis.

Promoting Adequate Food Intake

Encouraging adequate nutrition requires addressing environmental and social barriers as much as dietary composition. Many older adults eat poorly simply because they eat alone or struggle with poorly fitting dentures. Nursing staff can enhance intake by ensuring an upright dining posture, offering small and frequent meals rather than three large plates, assisting with opening food packaging, and maintaining a calm, pleasant dining atmosphere.
