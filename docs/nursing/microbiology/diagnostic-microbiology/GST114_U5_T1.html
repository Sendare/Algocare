Collection of Specimens for Microbiological Investigation

Introduction to Microbiological Specimen Collection

Microbiological specimen collection is the process of gathering clinical samples from a patient to identify causative agents of infectious diseases. Accurate identification of pathogens guides targeted antimicrobial therapy, improving patient outcomes and combating resistance. Poor collection technique often introduces skin flora or environmental contaminants, yielding misleading laboratory results that lead to inappropriate antibiotic use. Nurses play a frontline role in ensuring samples capture the actual site of infection rather than colonizing flora.

General Principles and Universal Precautions

Safe collection demands strict adherence to infection prevention and control guidelines. Universal precautions treat all biological fluids as potentially infectious.

  • Hand hygiene: Perform before and after patient contact, and immediately after removing gloves.
  • Personal protective equipment: Wear clean non-sterile gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection based on the anticipated splash risk.
  • Aseptic technique: Maintain sterility of collection devices and avoid touching sterile components to non-sterile surfaces.

Equipment, Reagents, and Transport Media

Different microorganisms require specific collection containers and chemical environments to survive outside the human body. Delicate pathogens like *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* die quickly if allowed to dry out or if exposed to cool temperatures.

  • Swabs: Dacron or rayon tips with plastic shafts are preferred; wooden shafts and cotton tips often contain fatty acids that inhibit bacterial growth.
  • Transport media: Semi-solid gels such as Amies or Stuart medium maintain moisture and pH balance without promoting rapid bacterial multiplication.
  • Sterile containers: Required for bulk fluids like urine, sputum, and cerebrospinal fluid to prevent external contamination.

Collection Techniques for Blood and Urine Specimens

Blood cultures require meticulous skin antisepsis to prevent contamination from cutaneous flora like *Staphylococcus epidermidis*. Scrub the venipuncture site using chlorhexidine or iodine preparations and allow adequate drying time before needle insertion.

For urine, the midstream clean-catch method flushes out distal urethral contaminants. Instruct female patients to clean the labia front-to-back, and male patients to clean the meatus, before passing the initial stream into the toilet and collecting the middle portion in a sterile jar. Catheterized specimens must be drawn from the designated port on the tubing, never from the collection bag.

Collection Techniques for Respiratory and Wound Specimens

Respiratory samples like sputum require deep coughing from the lungs rather than simple saliva clearance. Collect these specimens early in the morning before the patient eats, rinsing the mouth with water first to minimize oral debris.

Wound specimens must never be collected from superficial pus or old drainage crusts, as these reflect secondary surface colonizers rather than the true pathogen driving the infection. Clean the wound surface gently with sterile normal saline to remove surface exudate, then rotate a sterile swab deep within the active, clean base of the wound or aspirate fluid using a sterile syringe.

Specimen Labeling, Documentation, and Request Forms

An unlabelled or mislabelled specimen is useless and dangerous, forcing a repeat collection that delays patient care. Label every specimen container directly at the bedside immediately after collection in the presence of the patient, never beforehand.

Required identifiers on the label include:

  • Patient full legal name and hospital identification number
  • Specific anatomical site and source of the specimen
  • Date and exact time of collection
  • Signature or initials of the collecting nurse

Accompanying request forms must clearly state suspected clinical diagnoses and current antibiotic therapy to guide the laboratory on which specific media or staining techniques to employ.

Storage, Preservation, and Transportation to the Laboratory

Delays in transport allow fragile pathogens to die and robust contaminants to multiply, distorting quantitative and qualitative results. Most bacteriology specimens should reach the laboratory within two hours of collection. If transport is delayed, refrigerate specimens like urine at 4 degrees Celsius to halt bacterial overgrowth. Blood culture bottles, however, must never be refrigerated; store them at room temperature if transport is delayed. Cerebrospinal fluid and other critical sterile body fluids require immediate hand-delivery to the laboratory without temperature extremes.

Preventing Contamination and Ensuring Patient Safety

Contaminated specimens waste hospital resources, cause patient anxiety, and trigger unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions. Minimizing contamination requires strict adherence to aseptic technique, avoiding contact between specimen containers and non-sterile surfaces, and changing gloves if they touch unsterile areas during the procedure. Patient safety is further protected by identifying patients using two unique identifiers before collection, verifying allergy status when applying skin antiseptics, and disposing of sharp instruments safely into rigid puncture-proof containers immediately after use.
