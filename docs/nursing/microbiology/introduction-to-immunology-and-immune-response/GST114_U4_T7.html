Abnormal Immune Responses

Introduction to Immune System Dysregulation

The immune system relies on a delicate balance to protect the host while preventing self-damage. When this system fails to regulate itself, responses can become overactive, misdirected against native tissues, or entirely absent. These breakdowns in immune tolerance and competence fall into three broad categories: hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, and immunodeficiency. Understanding these disruptions is essential for managing chronic inflammatory conditions and preventing opportunistic infections in vulnerable hospital patients.

Hypersensitivity Reactions: Types I through IV

Hypersensitivity reactions represent overactive immune responses that cause tissue injury. The Gell and Coombs classification divides them into four distinct types based on the underlying mechanism:

  • Type I (Immediate): Mediated by IgE antibodies, leading to mast cell degranulation and histamine release. Examples include anaphylaxis and allergic rhinitis.
  • Type II (Cytotoxic): IgG or IgM antibodies bind to cellular antigens, causing cell lysis via complement activation. Examples include blood transfusion reactions.
  • Type III (Immune Complex): Antigen-antibody complexes deposit in tissues, triggering intense inflammation. Examples include systemic lupus erythematosus.
  • Type IV (Delayed): T-cell mediated reactions that take 24 to 72 hours to develop. Examples include contact dermatitis and the tuberculin skin test.

Autoimmune Diseases and Pathophysiology

Autoimmunity occurs when the immune system loses self-tolerance, mounting a destructive response against the body's own cells and tissues. Normally, developing lymphocytes that react to self-antigens are eliminated or suppressed. When this fails, autoantibodies and autoreactive T cells cause chronic inflammation and organ damage. Genetic predisposition, hormonal influences, and environmental triggers like viral infections often combine to spark these chronic conditions.

Primary and Secondary Immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiencies leave individuals vulnerable to recurrent, severe, and unusual infections. They are split into two groups:

  1. Primary Immunodeficiencies: Inherited genetic defects that affect specific components of the immune system, such as B cells, T cells, or complement proteins.
  2. Secondary Immunodeficiencies: Acquired later in life due to external factors like malnutrition, immunosuppressive medications, aging, or viral infections such as HIV.

Transplantation Immunology and Graft Rejection

When a patient receives an organ transplant, the recipient's immune system inspects the donor tissue for foreign markers known as human leukocyte antigens (HLAs). If the immune system identifies the tissue as foreign, it mounts an attack to destroy the graft. Rejection can be hyperacute, acute, or chronic, depending on how quickly the immune system responds. To prevent this, transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppressive therapy to dampen their immune response and protect the new organ.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Testing

Diagnosing immune disorders requires a careful clinical history, physical examination, and targeted laboratory investigations. Common diagnostic tests include complete blood counts with differential to check white blood cell subsets, measurement of specific immunoglobulin levels, autoantibody panels like ANA for autoimmune diseases, and skin testing for allergies. Nurses play a vital role in preparing patients for these tests, explaining procedures, and monitoring for adverse reactions during diagnostic challenges.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Care for patients with immune dysregulation centers on infection prevention, symptom management, and medication adherence. For immunocompromised patients, strict aseptic technique and protective isolation precautions are necessary. For patients with overactive immune systems, nurses monitor the side effects of immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory therapies. Comprehensive education helps patients recognize early signs of infection or flare-ups, empowering them to seek timely medical intervention.
