Non-specific Body Defences

Introduction to Non-Specific Body Defences

Non-specific body defences, also known as innate immunity, represent the body's immediate, generalized response to any foreign invader. Unlike adaptive immunity, which tailors its attack to specific pathogens and develops memory, innate defences are present from birth and act identically regardless of how many times the body has encountered a particular pathogen.

These mechanisms provide the first two tiers of immune protection. The first tier blocks pathogens from entering the internal environment, while the second tier deploys internal cellular and chemical agents if a breach occurs. Because these defenses do not require prior sensitization, they mount a rapid counteroffensive within minutes or hours of tissue invasion.

First Line of Defence: Barriers and Secretions

The first line of defense acts as a physical and chemical shield preventing pathogens from entering deep tissues. The skin and mucous membranes form the primary structural barriers.

  • Skin: The outer layer of keratinized cells provides a tough, dry, and nearly impenetrable surface. Periodic shedding of surface skin cells also removes colonized microbes.
  • Mucous Membranes: Lining the respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts, these moist surfaces trap incoming particles in sticky mucus.
  • Cilia: Microscopic hair-like projections in the respiratory tract sweep trapped mucus and foreign debris upward toward the throat where it can be swallowed or expelled.
  • Epithelial Secretions: Tears, saliva, and sweat flush surfaces and contain protective enzymes.

Chemical and Biochemical Defences

Chemical barriers complement physical structures by creating hostile microenvironments that inhibit microbial survival.

  • Gastric Acid: Hydrochloric acid in the stomach creates a strongly acidic pH (1.5 to 3.5) that destroys most ingested microorganisms.
  • Lysozyme: An antibacterial enzyme found in tears, saliva, and nasal secretions that hydrolyzes bacterial cell walls, particularly gram-positive organisms.
  • Sebum and Sweat: Sebum secreted by sebaceous glands contains fatty acids and lactic acid, maintaining an acidic skin pH that retards bacterial growth.
  • Defensins: Small antimicrobial peptides produced by mucous membranes that disrupt microbial cell membranes.

Second Line of Defence: Cellular Components and Phagocytosis

When pathogens breach the first line of defense, internal non-specific mechanisms take over. Phagocytes are the principal cellular agents responsible for engulfing and destroying foreign particles and cellular debris.

  • Neutrophils: The most abundant white blood cells, which arrive quickly at sites of acute infection to phagocytose bacteria.
  • Macrophages: Differentiated monocytes residing in tissues that engulf larger debris and present antigens to jumpstart adaptive immunity.
  • Natural Killer (NK) Cells: Lymphocytes that do not phagocytose; instead, they lyse virus-infected cells and tumor cells by releasing perforins and granzymes.

Phagocytosis occurs in distinct stages: chemotaxis, adherence, ingestion, formation of a phagolysosome, and enzymatic destruction.

The Inflammatory Response

Inflammation is a localized tissue reaction to injury, infection, or irritation, designed to contain the injury, destroy pathogens, and prepare the site for healing. The four cardinal signs are redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), and pain (dolor), often accompanied by loss of function.

The process begins when damaged cells and mast cells release chemical mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes. These mediators cause vasodilation, increasing blood flow to the area (causing redness and heat), and increase capillary permeability, allowing fluid and plasma proteins to leak into interstitial spaces (causing swelling).

Swelling puts pressure on nerve endings, triggering pain. Increased permeability also allows neutrophils and monocytes to marginate and emigrate into the tissue via diapedesis to phagocytose invaders.

Fever as a Systemic Defence Mechanism

Fever, or pyrexia, is a systemic elevation of core body temperature regulated by the hypothalamus. It serves as a vital component of the innate immune response.

During infection, phagocytes engulf bacteria and release endogenous pyrogens, such as interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor. These signaling molecules travel to the hypothalamus and trigger the synthesis of prostaglandins, which reset the body's thermal set point upward.

Elevated core temperatures benefit the host by inhibiting the reproduction of many temperature-sensitive pathogens, increasing metabolic rates to accelerate tissue repair, and enhancing the mobility and activity of white blood cells. However, extremely high fevers require nursing intervention to prevent cellular damage and excessive metabolic strain.

Antimicrobial Proteins: Complement and Interferons

Specialized proteins circulating in body fluids provide an additional layer of non-specific defense.

  • The Complement System: A group of over thirty plasma proteins that work in a cascade. When activated by pathogen surfaces or antibodies, complement proteins promote inflammation, coat microbes to enhance phagocytosis (opsonization), and assemble into a Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) that punches holes in bacterial cell walls, causing lysis.
  • Interferons: Signaling proteins produced by virus-infected cells. Interferons bind to neighboring uninfected cells, stimulating them to synthesize enzymes that block viral replication, effectively halting the spread of the infection.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Care

Understanding non-specific defences directly guides nursing practice, particularly in infection control, patient assessment, and supportive care. Nurses must recognize that medical interventions can inadvertently compromise these natural barriers.

Invasive procedures such as catheterization, intravenous cannulation, and endotracheal intubation bypass the first line of defence (skin and mucous membranes), creating entry points for nosocomial pathogens. Strict aseptic technique during these procedures is paramount.

Nurses monitor vital signs for systemic indicators of innate responses, such as elevated temperature or localized inflammation around surgical incisions. Furthermore, nurses educate patients on maintaining skin integrity, proper nutrition, and adequate hydration to support optimal immune function.
