Classification of Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and Immune Response

Immunity is the body's physiological capability to ward off foreign invaders, pathogens, and abnormal cells. The immune system achieves this through a coordinated network of cells, tissues, and organs known as the immune response. When a microorganism breaches physical barriers like the skin or mucous membranes, internal defense mechanisms activate to neutralize the threat and maintain homeostasis.

Innate Versus Adaptive Immunity

Immunity is broadly categorized into two major branches based on how the body recognizes and responds to threats:

  • Innate Immunity: Present from birth, non-specific, and responds rapidly to any foreign substance without prior exposure.
  • Adaptive Immunity: Acquired over time, highly specific to a particular pathogen, and develops a memory for faster future responses.

Active Immunity Pathways

Active immunity occurs when the body's own immune system generates antibodies in response to the presence of a pathogen. This process provides long-lasting protection due to the formation of memory cells.

  1. Natural Active Immunity: Acquired when a person is infected naturally by a disease-causing organism and recovers.
  2. Artificial Active Immunity: Acquired through vaccination, where a killed or weakened antigen is introduced to trigger antibody production safely.

Passive Immunity Pathways

Passive immunity involves the transfer of pre-formed antibodies from another person or animal. Because the recipient's own immune system does not produce these antibodies, protection is immediate but temporary, lasting only a few weeks or months until the foreign antibodies degrade.

  • Natural Passive: Transferred from mother to fetus across the placenta or through breast milk.
  • Artificial Passive: Administered via injections of immune globulins or antisera, such as tetanus antitoxin.

Humoral Versus Cell-Mediated Immunity

Adaptive immunity operates through two major arms that work cooperatively to eliminate pathogens.

FeatureHumoral ImmunityCell-Mediated Immunity
Main CellsB lymphocytes (B cells)T lymphocytes (T cells)
Primary ActionProduces antibodies in body fluidsDestroys infected or abnormal cells directly
TargetExtracellular pathogens like bacteriaIntracellular pathogens like viruses

Humoral immunity focuses on neutralizing threats floating in blood and lymph, whereas cell-mediated immunity deals with cells already compromised by internal invaders.

Clinical Relevance and Nursing Implications

Understanding immune classification guides clinical nursing interventions. Nurses assess vaccination histories to ensure patients develop artificial active immunity against preventable diseases. In immunocompromised patients, such as those with HIV or undergoing chemotherapy, nurses implement strict infection control measures because both innate and adaptive defenses may be compromised. Recognizing the difference between active and passive protection also helps in administering immunoglobulin therapy safely.
