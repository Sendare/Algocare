General Principles and Practice of Immunology

Introduction to Immunology and the Immune System

Immunology is the study of the immune system, which serves as the body's primary defense network against harmful pathogens, foreign substances, and abnormal cell growths like tumors. The immune system is broadly categorized into two major branches: innate immunity, which provides immediate, non-specific protection, and adaptive immunity, which mounts tailored, antigen-specific responses with memory capabilities. Understanding these principles helps nurses recognize why certain patients, such as those on immunosuppressive drugs or living with HIV, face heightened infection risks. The primary goal of this defense network is self-nonself discrimination, allowing the body to destroy invaders while sparing healthy host tissues. When this recognition fails, autoimmune disorders arise, causing the body to attack its own cells.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

The immune response relies on two complementary systems that differ in speed, specificity, and memory.

  • Innate Immunity: Present from birth, acts rapidly within minutes to hours, and is non-specific. Physical barriers like skin and mucous membranes, chemical barriers like stomach acid, and phagocytic cells make up this first line of defense.
  • Adaptive Immunity: Develops after exposure to specific pathogens, acts more slowly during the first infection, and creates immunological memory. This system utilizes T and B lymphocytes to mount a targeted, potent defense upon subsequent exposures.

While innate immunity lacks memory and reacts identically to repeated exposures, adaptive immunity improves its response time and strength with every subsequent encounter with the same pathogen.

Cells and Tissues of the Immune System

Immune cells originate in the bone marrow from hematopoietic stem cells and mature in primary lymphoid organs such as the bone marrow and thymus. Secondary lymphoid organs, including the spleen, lymph nodes, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), serve as sites where mature immune cells encounter antigens and initiate adaptive responses.

Antigens, Epitopes, and Antibody Structure

An antigen is any substance that the immune system recognizes as foreign and capable of triggering an immune response. Specific regions on the antigen molecule that bind to antibodies or T-cell receptors are called epitopes. Antibodies, also known as immunoglobulins, are Y-shaped proteins produced by plasma cells.

The typical antibody structure consists of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains. The tips of the Y-shape form the Fab region, containing the variable domain responsible for specific antigen binding. The base of the Y-shape forms the Fc region, which interacts with immune cell receptors to mediate effector functions like complement activation and phagocytosis enhancement.

Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) and Antigen Presentation

The Major Histocompatibility Complex is a set of cell surface proteins essential for the acquired immune system to recognize foreign molecules. MHC molecules act like molecular display trays, holding peptide fragments of antigens on the cell surface for T cells to inspect.

  • MHC Class I: Found on all nucleated cells, presenting intracellular peptides (such as viral proteins) to cytotoxic T cells.
  • MHC Class II: Found exclusively on professional antigen-presenting cells like macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells, presenting extracellular peptides to helper T cells.

Without proper antigen presentation via MHC molecules, T cells cannot detect infected or abnormal cells within the body.

Immune Responses: Humoral and Cell-Mediated Mechanisms

Adaptive immunity operates through two major arms that work cooperatively to clear infections.

  1. Humoral Immunity: Mediated by B lymphocytes and antibodies dissolved in bodily fluids. B cells encounter antigens, mature into plasma cells, and secrete antibodies that neutralize toxins and coat pathogens for destruction.
  2. Cell-Mediated Immunity: Mediated by T lymphocytes. Helper T cells release cytokines to activate other immune cells, while cytotoxic T cells directly induce apoptosis in virus-infected or malignant host cells.

A balanced response requires both arms; humoral immunity primarily targets extracellular microbes, whereas cell-mediated immunity handles intracellular threats that hide inside host cells where antibodies cannot reach.

Hypersensitivity Reactions and Immunodeficiencies

Immune responses can sometimes cause tissue damage or fail to protect the host adequately. Hypersensitivity reactions represent overactive immune responses classified into four types: Type I (immediate IgE-mediated allergies like anaphylaxis), Type II (cytotoxic antibody-mediated reactions like transfusion mismatches), Type III (immune complex-mediated reactions), and Type IV (delayed cell-mediated reactions like contact dermatitis).

Conversely, immunodeficiencies occur when the immune system is absent or impaired. Primary immunodeficiencies are inherited genetic defects, whereas secondary immunodeficiencies are acquired due to factors like malnutrition, immunosuppressive medications, or infections such as HIV. Nurses must monitor immunocompromised patients rigorously because standard signs of infection may be muted or absent.

Vaccination and Immunization Principles

Vaccination utilizes the adaptive immune system's memory to protect individuals from infectious diseases without causing full-blown illness. Vaccines introduce harmless antigens—such as killed pathogens, live attenuated strains, or recombinant proteins—prompting the primary immune response and establishing memory B and T cells.

Active immunization stimulates the host's own immune system to produce lasting antibodies and cellular memory. Passive immunization involves the direct administration of pre-formed antibodies, such as tetanus antitoxin or rabies immunoglobulin, providing immediate but temporary protection without stimulating long-term host memory. Herd immunity occurs when a large percentage of a population becomes immune, indirectly protecting vulnerable individuals who cannot be vaccinated.

Diagnostic Immunology and Nursing Applications

Diagnostic immunology uses antigen-antibody reactions in laboratory settings to detect diseases, monitor immune status, and verify blood compatibility. Common techniques include ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay), agglutination tests for blood typing, and Western blotting to identify specific proteins.

Nurses play a vital role in diagnostic immunology by collecting accurate blood samples, monitoring patients for adverse transfusion reactions, and providing pre- and post-test education. Ensuring correct specimen labeling and timing is crucial because collection errors can lead to false laboratory results, incorrect diagnoses, and inappropriate patient management.
