Acquired Immune Deficiencies

Introduction to Acquired Immune Deficiencies

Unlike primary immune deficiencies which stem from genetic abnormalities present at birth, acquired immune deficiencies develop later in life due to external factors. These secondary conditions damage a previously normal immune system, leaving the individual vulnerable to opportunistic infections and malignancies. Understanding the distinction between congenital and acquired immune loss guides proper clinical history taking and diagnostic prioritization in Nigerian healthcare settings.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Immune System Decline

The gradual destruction of the immune network typically targets specific cellular components, most notably CD4+ T-helper lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. As these regulatory cells deplete, cell-mediated immunity collapses, impairing cytokine signaling and antibody production. This systemic breakdown prevents coordinated defense responses against viral, fungal, and bacterial invaders.

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and Progression to AIDS

HIV infection follows a well-documented trajectory from acute seroconversion to clinical latency, culminating in Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS). During the latent phase, viral replication continues steadily inside lymph nodes while CD4 counts slowly decline. When the CD4 count drops below 200 cells per microliter, or when defining opportunistic illnesses appear, the clinical status officially transitions to AIDS.

Secondary Immunodeficiencies Caused by Malnutrition and Infections

  • Protein-Energy Malnutrition: Deprives the body of essential amino acids required for antibody synthesis and cellular proliferation, particularly affecting mucosal immunity.
  • Measles: Causes profound temporary immune amnesia by depleting previously established memory T and B cells, increasing susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections.
  • Malaria: Chronic parasitic infection induces splenic exhaustion and alters cytokine profiles, weakening responses to unrelated vaccines and pathogens.

Iatrogenic Causes of Acquired Immune Deficiency

  • Corticosteroids: Suppress inflammation and lymphocyte proliferation, blunting the body's natural defense mechanisms during long-term therapy.
  • Chemotherapy Agents: Target rapidly dividing cells indiscriminately, leading to profound neutropenia and bone marrow suppression.
  • Immunosuppressants: Medications used post-organ transplantation deliberately dampen immune activity to prevent graft rejection, creating high infection risks.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluating acquired immune deficiency requires a thorough history focusing on recurrent infections, unexplained weight loss, and exposure risks. Laboratory investigations centre on complete blood counts with differential, CD4+ T-cell enumeration, and serological assays like ELISA and Western blot for specific viral etiologies. Nurses must monitor for subtle signs of oral thrush, persistent fevers, and generalized lymphadenopathy.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Care plans for immunodeficient patients prioritize infection prevention, nutritional support, and medication adherence. Nurses enforce strict hand hygiene, implement protective isolation when necessary, and educate patients on safe food preparation to avoid foodborne pathogens. Monitoring vital signs for early trends of sepsis remains a critical daily responsibility.

Prevention, Treatment, and Public Health Considerations in Nigeria

Public health strategies in Nigeria focus on expanding access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), preventing mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT), and managing overlapping burdens of malaria and tuberculosis. Community health nurses play a vital role in reducing social stigma, promoting voluntary counseling and testing (VCT), and ensuring uninterrupted drug supplies at primary healthcare centres.
