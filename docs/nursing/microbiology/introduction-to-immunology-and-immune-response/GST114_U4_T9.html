Anaphylaxis

Introduction to Anaphylaxis and Immune System Response

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic, and rapidly progressive hypersensitivity reaction that can be fatal within minutes if untreated. It represents a Type I IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. When a susceptible individual encounters a trigger, the immune system overreacts by producing large amounts of specific Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies. These antibodies bind to the surface of mast cells and basophils. Upon re-exposure, the offending antigen cross-links with the cell-bound IgE, triggering degranulation. This explosive release of stored inflammatory mediators—such as histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes—causes widespread vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and smooth muscle contraction across multiple organ systems.

Common Triggers and Etiologies in Clinical Practice

Anaphylactic triggers vary widely, though certain categories dominate clinical presentations in healthcare settings. Food allergens are the leading cause in children and young adults, while medications and insect stings are more common among older populations.

  • Food: Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, milk, eggs, and wheat.
  • Medications: Beta-lactam antibiotics (especially penicillin), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and intravenous contrast media.
  • Insect Stings: Venom from bees, wasps, hornets, and ants.
  • Latex: Natural rubber latex found in surgical gloves and catheters.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Recognition

Symptoms typically appear within seconds to minutes after allergen exposure. Involving two or more body systems strongly points toward a diagnosis of anaphylaxis.

Body SystemClinical Signs and Symptoms
SkinUrticaria (hives), angioedema, flushing, pruritus
RespiratoryDyspnea, wheezing, bronchospasm, stridor, throat tightness
CardiovascularHypotension, tachycardia, dizziness, syncope, weak pulse
GastrointestinalNausea, vomiting, cramping abdominal pain, diarrhea

Upper airway swelling and lower airway bronchospasm represent the most life-threatening respiratory manifestations.

Nursing Assessment and Rapid Triage

Immediate recognition saves lives. When an unstable patient arrives, the nurse must rapidly evaluate the airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCDE framework). Ask conscious patients about recent food consumption, new medications, or insect stings. Assess for stridor, voice changes, or drooling, which signal impending upper airway obstruction. Measure blood pressure and pulse immediately, noting any signs of distributive shock such as a thready pulse or profound hypotension. Nurses must never leave a deteriorating patient alone and should activate emergency response teams instantly.

Emergency Medical Management and Pharmacological Intervention

Epinephrine is the absolute cornerstone of anaphylaxis management and must be administered intramuscularly without delay. Delays in giving epinephrine increase the risk of fatal outcomes.

  1. Epinephrine Administration: Inject 1:1000 solution into the anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh. The adult dose is 0.3mg to 0.5mg.
  2. Positioning: Place the patient flat with legs elevated to support venous return. If the patient is vomiting or struggling to breathe, allow a comfortable sitting position.
  3. Oxygen Therapy: Deliver high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask to maintain adequate tissue saturation.
  4. Fluid Resuscitation: Infuse rapid boluses of isotonic crystalloids (normal saline) to counteract distributive shock from vasodilation.
  5. Adjunct Medications: Administer H1 and H2 antihistamines along with systemic corticosteroids to prevent prolonged or secondary reactions.

Complications, Bifiphasic Reactions, and Differential Diagnosis

Even after successful initial treatment, nurses must remain vigilant. A biphasic reaction can occur, where symptoms return 1 to 72 hours later without any new exposure to the trigger. This occurs in up to 20% of cases, which is why extended monitoring is mandatory.

Differential diagnoses that mimic anaphylaxis include:

  • Acute Asthma Exacerbation: Features wheezing and dyspnea, but lacks hives, angioedema, or hypotension.
  • Vasovagal Syncope: Presents with dizziness and hypotension, but typically features bradycardia and pale, clammy skin rather than tachycardia and hives.
  • Scombrotoxic Fish Poisoning: Mimics an allergic reaction due to histamine release from spoiled fish, but does not involve IgE mechanisms.

Patient Education and Prevention Strategies

Preventing future episodes relies on comprehensive patient education before discharge. Patients and caregivers must learn how to identify early warning signs and avoid identified allergens completely. Individuals with confirmed severe allergies should receive a prescription for an autoinjector, such as an EpiPen, and receive hands-on training on its proper use. Emphasize that the autoinjector must be carried at all times. Additionally, advising patients to wear a medical alert bracelet ensures rapid identification if they are found unresponsive during a future emergency.
