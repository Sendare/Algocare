Standard Precautionary Measures

Core Principles of Standard Precautions

Standard precautions form the primary tier of infection prevention in healthcare settings. Every patient must be treated as potentially infectious for bloodborne pathogens and other infectious agents, regardless of their diagnosis or presumed infection status. These measures apply universally to blood, all body fluids, secretions, excretions except sweat, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. Integrating these practices into daily bedside nursing routines protects both patients and healthcare workers from cross-contamination.

  • Universal application: Applies to all patients in all healthcare settings at all times.
  • Risk reduction: Minimizes the transmission of microbial agents from recognized and unrecognized sources.
  • Foundation of safety: Serves as the baseline level of infection control before adding transmission-based precautions.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Techniques

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most critical measure to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Nurses must perform hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water depending on the specific clinical situation. Handwashing with soap and water remains mandatory when hands are visibly soiled, contaminated with blood or body fluids, or after caring for patients with known spore-forming organisms like Clostridium difficile.

  1. Alcohol-based hand rub: Preferred for routine clinical work when hands are not visibly dirty, taking 20 to 30 seconds to cover all surfaces.
  2. Plain soap and water: Essential for mechanical removal of dirt and organic material, lasting 40 to 60 seconds.
  3. Surgical hand scrub: Performed before entering the operating theatre using an antiseptic agent to eliminate transient flora and reduce resident flora.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment (PPE) creates a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and potential infectious agents. The selection of specific items depends entirely on the anticipated type of exposure, whether through contact, droplets, or airborne transmission. Putting on and removing PPE requires a deliberate sequence to avoid self-contamination.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Unsafe injection practices place both patients and nursing staff at grave risk of bloodborne infections such as Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Needles and syringes are single-use devices and must never be reused for another patient, even if the needle is changed. Medication vials should be treated as single-dose whenever possible to prevent multi-patient contamination.

  • No recapping: Never recap used needles using two hands; use a mechanical scoop device if recapping is absolutely unavoidable.
  • Immediate disposal: Drop sharps directly into a puncture-resistant container immediately after use.
  • Container placement: Position safety disposal boxes at eye level and within arm's reach in every medication preparation area.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette target the containment of respiratory pathogens spread through droplet or airborne routes. Patients, visitors, and staff must be educated on controlling respiratory secretions at the source. Signs should be posted at facility entrances instructing individuals with respiratory symptoms to cover their mouths and noses.

  • Covering: Use disposable tissues when coughing or sneezing, and dispose of them immediately into the nearest waste bin.
  • Alternative: Cough or sneeze into the inner elbow if tissues are unavailable rather than bare hands.
  • Masking: Offer a surgical mask to coughing individuals in waiting areas and separate them from others by at least one meter.

Environmental Cleaning and Disinfection

Healthcare environments harbor microbial pathogens on frequently touched surfaces like bed rails, call buttons, and bedside tables. Routine cleaning removes dust and organic soil, while disinfection inactivates specific pathogenic microorganisms. Environmental cleaning protocols rely on hospital-approved disinfectants mixed to exact manufacturer specifications.

  • High-touch surfaces: Cleaned and disinfected at least twice daily and between patient occupancy changes.
  • Cleaning direction: Wipe surfaces from clean areas to dirty areas, and from high levels down toward the floor.
  • Contact time: Ensure the disinfectant remains wet on the surface for the specified contact time to achieve microbial kill.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Healthcare Waste

Proper segregation of healthcare waste protects waste handlers, the community, and the environment from biological and chemical hazards. Color-coded bin systems ensure that infectious waste is separated from general municipal refuse right at the point of generation.

  • Black or brown bins: General household waste like office paper, food wrappers, and packaging.
  • Yellow bins with biohazard symbol: Infectious waste contaminated with blood, body fluids, dressings, and disposable linen.
  • Puncture-resistant sharps boxes: Used needles, blades, scalpels, and broken glass ampoules.

Management of Occupational Exposure

Accidental needlestick injuries and mucosal splashes require immediate, standardized action to mitigate the transmission of bloodborne viruses such as HIV and Hepatitis B. Delaying initial first aid or reporting reduces the effectiveness of post-exposure prophylaxis.

  1. Immediate first aid: Wash needle-stick wounds immediately with soap and water; flush exposed mucous membranes or eyes with copious amounts of clean water or saline.
  2. Report immediately: Notify the infection control unit or supervisor right away to document the incident and initiate evaluation protocols.
  3. Post-exposure assessment: Evaluate the source patient's infection status and administer prophylaxis medication within hours if indicated.
