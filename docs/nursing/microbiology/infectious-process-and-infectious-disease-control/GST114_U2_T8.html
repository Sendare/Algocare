Handling and Disposal of Sharps

Introduction and Classification of Sharps

Sharps are devices with sharp points or edges that can puncture or lacerate skin, posing a major occupational hazard in healthcare settings. Correct identification of these items is the first step in preventing accidental exposure among nursing staff, doctors, patients, and waste handlers. Common examples include:

  • Hypodermic needles: Hollow needles used for injections or blood drawing.
  • Suturing needles: Curved or straight needles attached to thread for closing wounds.
  • Scalpel blades: Detachable surgical blades used for tissue cutting.
  • Lancets: Small pointed devices used for capillary blood sampling, such as glucose testing.
  • Broken glass: Ampoules, vials, or contaminated laboratory slides that can cause cuts.

Risks of Needle-Stick Injuries and Bloodborne Pathogens

Accidental puncture wounds from contaminated sharps expose healthcare workers to serious, life-altering infections. The severity of a needle-stick injury depends on the pathogen load, the depth of the puncture, and the hollow bore size of the needle. The three primary bloodborne pathogens transmitted through sharps injuries are:

  1. Hepatitis B Virus (HBV): Carries the highest transmission risk following a single prick due to high viral stability.
  2. Hepatitis C Virus (HCV): Chronic infection risk is significant, though immediate post-exposure treatment options exist.
  3. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): Carries a lower statistical transmission rate per needle-stick than HBV, but causes severe, lifelong immune compromise.

Beyond these major three, injuries can transmit malaria, syphilis, and viral hemorrhagic fevers if the patient is infected.

Standard Precautions and Safe Handling Practices

Preventing injuries relies on strict adherence to standard precautions during every clinical procedure. Never recap used needles using two hands, as this is a leading cause of accidental self-inoculation. If recapping is unavoidable due to procedural constraints, use a single-handed scoop technique or a mechanical device holding the cap.

Never bend, break, or shear used needles by hand. Transport used sharps safely at the point of use by bringing a portable sharps container directly to the bedside rather than carrying loose needles across the ward. Discard items immediately after use without leaving them exposed on treatment trolleys or patient beds.

Sharps Containers and Disposal Procedures

Sharps containers must be puncture-resistant, leak-proof on the sides and bottom, and fitted with a secure lid to prevent spillage. These containers are typically color-coded yellow in many healthcare settings to indicate biohazardous waste.

Position sharps containers at eye level and within arm's reach wherever sharps are used. Never allow a container to overfill. Seal and replace the container permanently when it reaches the three-quarters full line, or when the fill line indicator is reached. Do not shake, press down with hands, or reach inside a sharps container to retrieve dropped items.

Immediate Actions Following a Needle-Stick Injury

When a needle-stick injury occurs, immediate first aid takes priority over administrative tasks. Wash the affected puncture site immediately with clean running water and soap. Do not scrub, suck, or squeeze the wound, as this can encourage pathogen entry into damaged capillaries.

Report the incident immediately to the ward charge nurse, infection control officer, or occupational health unit. Rapid assessment is vital because post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV is most effective when initiated within hours of exposure. Document the details of the incident, including the source patient when known, to guide medical evaluation.
