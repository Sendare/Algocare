Source, Transmission and Course of Infection

Introduction to the Chain of Infection

Infectious diseases do not appear out of nowhere; they follow a predictable path known as the chain of infection. Understanding this chain helps nurses prevent the spread of micro-organisms in hospital and community settings in Nigeria. The chain consists of six distinct links that must all be present for an infection to happen:

  1. Infectious Agent: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives and multiplies, like a human, animal, or contaminated water source.
  3. Portal of Exit: The route the pathogen uses to leave the reservoir, such as respiratory secretions, blood, or stool.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen moves from the reservoir to a new host.
  5. Portal of Entry: The path the pathogen takes to enter the new host, such as broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract.
  6. Susceptible Host: A person who lacks immunity or resistance to ward off the invading pathogen.

Breaking even one link in this sequence stops the infection from spreading to another patient or staff member.

Sources and Reservoirs of Infection

A reservoir serves as the home base for a pathogen, allowing it to survive, grow, and multiply. Reservoirs can be living organisms or inanimate objects in the environment.

  • Human Reservoirs: People with active signs of disease, as well as asymptomatic carriers who harbor the pathogen without showing symptoms. Malaria and typhoid fever often involve human carriers in local communities.
  • Animal Reservoirs: Animals that carry zoonotic infections. For example, Lassa fever is spread primarily through multi-mammate rats found in West African homes.
  • Environmental Reservoirs: Soil and water can harbor spores or bacteria. Tetanus spores live in ordinary soil, entering the body through puncture wounds.

Healthcare environments themselves can act as reservoirs if medical equipment, sinks, and high-touch surfaces are not thoroughly cleaned and disinfected between patients.

Modes of Transmission: Direct and Indirect

Once a pathogen leaves its reservoir, it needs a vehicle or pathway to reach a new host. Transmission is broadly categorized into direct and indirect methods.

Direct transmission involves immediate, physical transfer of microbes from an infected person to a susceptible host. This happens through kissing, touching, sexual intercourse, or droplet spread during coughing or sneezing within a close range of one meter.

Indirect transmission relies on an intermediary object or creature:

  • Vehicle-borne: Contaminated food, water, biological fluids, or medications.
  • Vector-borne: Living creatures like mosquitoes transmitting the malaria parasite.
  • Airborne: Tiny droplet nuclei remaining suspended in the air over long distances, such as the bacteria causing tuberculosis.

Fomites—inanimate objects like stethoscopes, pens, or bedside tables—frequently mediate indirect contact spread in busy hospital wards.

Portals of Entry and Exit in the Human Host

Pathogens must find a way out of their current host and a way into a new one. These exit and entry portals often mirror each other.

Common portals include:

  • Respiratory Tract: Droplets and aerosols leave via the nose and mouth during coughing and enter the same way when inhaled.
  • Gastrointestinal Tract: Pathogens exit through stool or vomit and enter through contaminated food or water via the oral route.
  • Genitourinary Tract: Sexually transmitted infections exit and enter through mucous membranes of reproductive organs and the urinary tract.
  • Skin and Mucous Membranes: Broken skin, surgical wounds, or needle puncture sites allow direct entry into deep tissues and the bloodstream.

Nurses managing surgical wounds or urinary catheters must maintain strict sterility to seal off these vulnerable entry points.

Susceptibility and Host Factors

Not everyone exposed to a pathogen develops an infection. Susceptibility depends heavily on the individual's overall resistance and immune status.

Age plays a major role; very young infants have immature immune systems, while elderly adults experience immunosenescence. Nutritional status is equally critical. Protein-energy malnutrition, common in certain impoverished regions, impairs antibody production and white blood cell function. Chronic illnesses like diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, or undergoing chemotherapy significantly lower the host's defense mechanisms.

Medical interventions can also induce temporary susceptibility. Invasive devices like indwelling urinary catheters or endotracheal tubes bypass natural defenses, making it much easier for opportunistic hospital-acquired pathogens to establish an infection.

The Course and Stages of an Infectious Disease

When a pathogen successfully enters a susceptible host and overcomes local defenses, the infection progresses through four distinct clinical stages:

  1. Incubation Period: The time between initial pathogen entry and the appearance of the first symptoms. The microbe is multiplying, but the patient feels completely normal.
  2. Prodromal Stage: A brief period characterized by vague, non-specific symptoms like mild fatigue, malaise, or low-grade fever. The pathogen continues to multiply.
  3. Illness Stage: The patient experiences specific signs and symptoms classic to the disease, such as a rash in measles or severe diarrhea in cholera. Immune defenses and medical treatments battle the pathogen here.
  4. Convalescent Stage: Tissue repair occurs and symptoms gradually subside until the patient returns to their baseline health status.

Patients can remain contagious during different stages depending on the specific organism involved.

Nursing Interventions for Breaking the Chain of Infection

Nursing practice revolves around interrupting the chain of infection to protect patients, visitors, and healthcare staff.

Hand hygiene remains the single most effective method to stop transmission via direct and indirect contact. Nurses must perform handwashing with soap and water or use alcohol-based hand rubs before and after every patient interaction.

Standard and transmission-based precautions provide structural barriers:

  • Standard Precautions: Applied to all patients, treating body fluids as potentially infectious by using gloves and proper waste disposal.
  • Contact Precautions: Gowns and gloves for multi-drug resistant organisms.
  • Droplet Precautions: Surgical masks when working close to patients with respiratory infections.
  • Airborne Precautions: N95 respirators and negative-pressure rooms for airborne threats like tuberculosis.

Proper sterilization of surgical instruments, safe injection practices, and timely immunizations further reinforce these protective barriers in clinical settings.
