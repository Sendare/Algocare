General Signs and Symptoms of Infectious Process

Introduction to Infectious Processes

An infectious process begins when a pathogen invades a host, multiplies, and triggers a tissue response. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. The human body responds through defense mechanisms that manifest as specific clinical signs. Recognizing these signs early allows nurses to implement timely interventions and prevent complications like sepsis. Infections can remain localized to a specific tissue or spread throughout the body, shifting from local discomfort to systemic crisis.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Infection

The lifecycle of an infection follows a predictable timeline in the human host:

  1. Incubation period: Time between pathogen entry and the appearance of first symptoms; the microbe is multiplying silently.
  2. Prodromal stage: Mild, nonspecific symptoms begin, such as general fatigue and malaise.
  3. Illness stage: Acute, specific signs and symptoms of the particular infection peak.
  4. Convalescent stage: Return to normal health as the body eliminates the infectious agent.

Systemic Manifestations: Fever and Chills

Fever is a hallmark systemic response mediated by pyrogens resetting the hypothalamic thermostat. When the body temperature set point rises, peripheral vasoconstriction and shivering occur to generate heat, clinically observed as chills. Once the new set point is reached, the patient feels warm and flushed. Monitoring fever curves helps determine the nature of the infection, whether intermittent, remittent, or sustained.

Inflammatory Responses and Localized Signs

Local infection triggers the classic signs of inflammation: redness, heat, swelling, pain, and loss of function. Increased blood flow to the site brings immune cells and plasma proteins, causing erythema and localized warmth. Capillary permeability leads to fluid exudation, creating edema that presses on nerve endings and produces pain. Recognizing these local signs distinguishes a localized abscess or wound infection from a generalized illness.

Hematological Changes in Infection

Blood tests reveal distinct patterns during infections. Leukocytosis, an elevated white blood cell count, is typical in bacterial infections as the bone marrow accelerates production. A 'left shift,' meaning an increase in immature neutrophils (bands), indicates severe acute bacterial infection. Conversely, certain viral infections may cause leukopenia. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate rise significantly during active infectious processes.

Gastrointestinal and Neurological Symptoms

Systemic infections frequently disrupt non-immune organ systems. Gastrointestinal symptoms like nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and anorexia occur due to circulating toxins or direct mucosal irritation. Neurological manifestations range from mild lethargy, confusion, and headache to severe encephalopathy. Elderly patients often present with acute confusion or delirium as the primary indicator of a urinary tract or respiratory infection, masking classic fever responses.

Clinical Assessment and Vital Sign Monitoring

Systemic infection alters vital signs predictably. Tachycardia often develops early as the body attempts to increase cardiac output to meet metabolic demands and support immune cell transport. Tachypnea accompanies fever due to increased metabolic rate and acid-base compensation. Blood pressure may drop if the infection progresses to vasodilation and septic shock. Nurses must track these trends diligently to detect clinical deterioration early.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Care

Management of infectious processes centers on supporting host defenses, controlling sources of infection, and administering prescribed therapies. Nurses administer antimicrobial medications precisely on schedule to maintain therapeutic blood levels. Hydration management replaces fluid lost through fever and tachypnea. Monitoring laboratory values, maintaining strict asepsis during wound care, and isolating infectious patients prevent nosocomial spread.
