Microbiology of Water

Introduction to Water Microbiology

Water microbiology examines the microorganisms that inhabit aquatic environments, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa. These microscopic entities play a crucial role in global nutrient cycles, breaking down organic waste and maintaining ecological balance in rivers, lakes, and oceans. While many aquatic microbes are harmless or beneficial, water can also serve as a vehicle for dangerous pathogens. Understanding how these organisms survive, multiply, and spread through water systems forms the scientific foundation for preventing water-related epidemics in healthcare and community settings.

Microbial Flora of Natural Water Bodies

Natural water bodies host diverse microbial populations shaped by temperature, pH, sunlight availability, and nutrient concentration.

  • Autotrophic Microbes: Algae and cyanobacteria perform photosynthesis near the surface, producing oxygen vital for aquatic life.
  • Heterotrophic Bacteria: These organisms thrive in deeper zones, consuming dissolved organic compounds and decaying plant or animal matter.
  • Autochthonous Flora: Species that are native and naturally adapted to live and reproduce in specific aquatic habitats.
  • Allochthonous Flora: Transient microorganisms washed into water bodies from soil, agricultural runoff, or sewage systems.

Sources and Types of Water Pollution

Water pollution occurs when foreign contaminants compromise the safety and ecological integrity of water resources. Contamination sources are generally categorized by their origin and point of entry.

Water-borne Pathogens and Diseases

Water acts as a primary transmission vehicle for a wide range of infectious agents. When drinking or domestic water sources become contaminated with faecal matter, pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and parasites spread rapidly through communities.

  • Cholera: Caused by *Vibrio cholerae*, leading to severe watery diarrhea and rapid, life-threatening dehydration.
  • Typhoid Fever: Caused by *Salmonella typhi*, characterized by sustained high fever, systemic illness, and gastrointestinal distress.
  • Amebic Dysentery: Caused by the protozoan *Entamoeba histolytica*, resulting in painful, bloody stools.
  • Hepatitis A: A viral infection transmitted via contaminated water, attacking the liver and causing jaundice, fatigue, and nausea.

Indicator Organisms and Water Quality Testing

Testing water for every known pathogen is impractical, expensive, and time-consuming. Instead, microbiologists test for indicator organisms—specifically coliform bacteria like *Escherichia coli*—which normally inhabit the intestines of humans and warm-blooded animals.

The presence of coliforms in a water sample signals recent faecal contamination and implies that dangerous pathogens may also be present. Standard testing methods include the membrane filtration technique and the multiple-tube fermentation test to estimate the Most Probable Number (MPN) of coliforms per 100 millilitres of water.

Water Treatment and Purification Methods

Transforming raw, contaminated surface water into safe drinking water requires a multi-step municipal treatment process.

  1. Coagulation and Flocculation: Chemicals like alum are added to clump tiny suspended particles together into heavier aggregates called flocs.
  2. Sedimentation: Water sits in large basins so heavy flocs and suspended solids settle to the bottom by gravity.
  3. Filtration: Water passes through beds of sand, gravel, and charcoal to remove remaining microscopic impurities and microbes.
  4. Disinfection: Chlorine, ozone, or ultraviolet light is applied to kill any remaining bacteria, viruses, and protozoan cysts before distribution.

Public Health Nursing Implications in Nigeria

Community and public health nurses in Nigeria frequently encounter water-borne disease outbreaks linked to inadequate rural and urban infrastructure. Limited access to potable water and reliance on untreated streams or shallow boreholes contribute to recurrent episodes of cholera, typhoid, and gastroenteritis.

Nurses play a vital frontline role through health education on household water treatment—such as boiling, solar disinfection, and safe storage. Furthermore, nurses participate in contact tracing, epidemiological surveillance, and community mobilization during epidemic responses, collaborating with local health authorities to improve environmental sanitation.
