Classification of Health Care Waste

Introduction and Importance of Health Care Waste Management

Health care waste encompasses all the waste generated during medical diagnosis, treatment, immunization, or research involving humans and animals. Nurses produce a significant portion of this daily output at the bedside. Proper segregation is the cornerstone of effective waste management. Mixing hazardous materials with ordinary refuse multiplies disposal costs and converts harmless domestic rubbish into dangerous infectious threats. Safe handling practices protect waste handlers, patients, and the wider community from accidental exposure, needle-stick injuries, and environmental contamination.

General Non-Hazardous Health Care Waste

Approximately 75 to 90 percent of waste produced by health care facilities is general, non-hazardous waste. This category poses no special biological, chemical, or physical hazard. It is comparable to domestic household waste and can be handled by municipal waste disposal systems.

  • Food leftovers: Kitchen scraps and organic refuse from patient dietary trays.
  • Office paper: Administrative documents, patient files made of paper, and cardboard packaging.
  • Uncontamined plastics: Water bottles, plastic wrappers, and packaging materials that never touched patients.
  • Garden waste: Sweepings from hospital grounds, leaves, and organic yard trimmings.

Infectious and Pathological Waste

Infectious waste contains pathogens in sufficient concentration to cause disease. This includes materials saturated with blood or body fluids, laboratory cultures, and isolation waste from patients with contagious diseases. Pathological waste consists of human tissues, organs, body parts, and recognizable fetuses. Both categories demand rigorous handling to prevent nosocomial outbreaks and community contamination. Standard disposal methods typically involve high-temperature incineration or autoclaving followed by secure shredding.

Sharps Waste and Injury Prevention

Sharps include any object that can puncture or cut skin, such as needles, scalpels, infusion sets with needles, and broken glass. Sharps injuries expose nurses and waste handlers to blood-borne pathogens like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV.

Safety rules for sharps management:

  • Never recap needles after patient use.
  • Do not overfill sharps puncture-proof boxes beyond the designated fill line.
  • Dispose of the entire needle and syringe unit directly into the sharps container without manual disassembly.

Pharmaceutical, Cytotoxic, and Chemical Waste

Pharmaceutical waste includes expired, unused, or contaminated drugs, vaccines, and sera. Cytotoxic waste is a specialized, highly hazardous subset consisting of drugs with teratogenic, mutagenic, or carcinogenic properties, commonly used in cancer treatment. Chemical waste comprises discarded solid, liquid, and gaseous chemicals from diagnostic procedures, cleaning agents, and laboratory reagents. These substances can poison water sources or cause severe chemical burns if dumped improperly into standard drains.

Color-Coding and Containerization Standards in Nigeria

Standardized color-coding ensures that health care waste is easily sorted and handled safely across all Nigerian health facilities. While specific institutional guidelines apply, national frameworks generally align with global standards:
