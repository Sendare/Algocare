Water Pollution

Introduction and Definition of Water Pollution

Water pollution occurs when harmful substances contaminate a water body, severely degrading its quality and making it toxic to humans, animals, and aquatic life. This contamination typically arises from human activities that introduce foreign matter faster than natural ecological systems can break down or dilute. In many Nigerian communities, improper disposal of domestic waste and industrial effluent directly into rivers and streams compromises basic water safety. Pure water in nature does not exist in an absolute chemical sense; it always contains dissolved minerals. Pollution specifically denotes an excess of harmful agents that disrupt the natural balance and utility of the water resource.

Major Types and Classifications of Water Pollutants

Water pollutants span diverse chemical, physical, and biological categories that determine their persistence and toxicity in the environment.

  • Biological pollutants: Pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and parasitic protozoa originating from sewage and faecal matter.
  • Chemical pollutants: Heavy metals like lead and mercury, synthetic pesticides, fertilizers, and industrial solvents.
  • Physical pollutants: Suspended solids, plastics, thermal pollution from power plants, and radioactive isotopes.

Primary Sources and Causes of Water Pollution

Pollution sources are broadly divided into point sources, which discharge waste from a distinct, identifiable location like a factory pipe, and non-point sources, which diffuse contaminants over a wide area such as agricultural runoff.

  • Industrial discharge: Factories releasing untreated chemical waste directly into local drainage systems.
  • Agricultural runoff: Fertilizers and pesticides washed by rain from farmlands into nearby rivers.
  • Domestic sewage: Improper disposal of household greywater and blackwater into open gutters and water channels.

Microbiological Pathogens and Waterborne Diseases

Microbial contamination of drinking water remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in developing regions. Pathogens enter water systems primarily through faecal-oral routes caused by inadequate sanitation infrastructure.

  • Cholera: Caused by *Vibrio cholerae*, leading to severe watery diarrhea and rapid dehydration.
  • Typhoid fever: Caused by *Salmonella typhi*, characterized by sustained high fever and gastrointestinal distress.
  • Amoebic dysentery: Caused by parasitic protozoa resulting in bloody diarrhea and abdominal cramps.

Environmental and Ecological Impact of Water Pollution

When excess nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizers enter aquatic ecosystems, they trigger rapid algal blooms. As these massive algal populations die and decompose, aerobic bacteria consume vast amounts of dissolved oxygen. This process, known as eutrophication, creates hypoxic dead zones where fish and other aquatic organisms suffocate and die. Beyond oxygen depletion, persistent chemical toxins accumulate in the tissues of aquatic life, moving up the food chain and threatening predators, including humans.

Methods of Water Quality Assessment and Testing

Evaluating water safety involves physical, chemical, and microbiological testing parameters.

  • Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD): Measures the amount of oxygen microorganisms require to decompose organic matter in water.
  • pH testing: Determines whether water is too acidic or alkaline due to industrial chemical runoff.
  • Coliform bacteria count: Serves as a standard indicator organism test to detect faecal contamination in drinking water.

Wastewater Treatment and Purification Technologies

Municipal wastewater treatment employs multiple stages to transform contaminated sewage into safe effluent.

  1. Screening and Sedimentation: Physical removal of large debris and heavy grit through settling tanks.
  2. Biological treatment: Utilization of microorganisms in aeration tanks to break down dissolved organic pollutants.
  3. Disinfection: Application of chemical agents like chlorine, or physical methods like UV irradiation, to kill remaining pathogens.

Nursing Interventions and Public Health Prevention Strategies

Nurses play a vital role in mitigating the health impacts of water pollution through community advocacy and clinical education. Healthcare providers teach households simple water purification techniques such as boiling, filtration, and solar disinfection when piped water is compromised. Public health nurses also conduct community outreach to identify outbreaks of waterborne diseases early, ensuring prompt isolation and treatment. Collaborative efforts with local sanitation agencies help reinforce proper waste disposal practices and protect vulnerable populations from preventable infections.
