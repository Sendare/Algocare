History and Development of Microbiology

Introduction and Early Beliefs about Disease

Microbiology is the study of microscopic organisms, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa. Before the invention of microscopes, people could not see these tiny agents. Instead, ancient societies blamed diseases on bad air, evil spirits, or an imbalance in bodily fluids known as humors. Healers relied on rituals, herbal remedies, and bloodletting because the true physical cause remained completely hidden from human sight.

Spontaneous Generation versus Biogenesis

For centuries, natural philosophers believed in spontaneous generation—the idea that living creatures could arise suddenly from non-living matter. Maggots appearing on decaying meat seemed to prove that filth generated life on its own.

Through careful experimentation, scientists eventually disproved this notion. Francesco Redi showed that meat kept away from flies did not breed maggots. Later, Louis Pasteur used swan-neck flasks to prove that sterile broth remained clear until exposed to airborne dust containing microorganisms, establishing the law of biogenesis: life arises only from pre-existing life.

Evolution of Microscopy

The birth of microbiology depended entirely on optical technology.

  • Antonie van Leeuwenhoek: Built simple single-lens microscopes in the seventeenth century and became the first person to observe bacteria and protozoa, which he called animalcules.
  • Robert Hooke: Improved compound microscope designs and published detailed drawings of biological structures, coining the term cell.
  • Modern Electron Microscopes: Developed in the twentieth century, these use beams of electrons instead of light to visualize extremely tiny structures like viruses with high magnification.

The Germ Theory of Disease and Koch's Postulates

The germ theory of disease established that specific microscopic organisms cause specific infectious illnesses. Robert Koch provided definitive proof for this concept while investigating anthrax in cattle.

To link a microbe to a specific disease, Koch created a rigid set of experimental guidelines known as Koch's postulates:

  1. The microorganism must be present in every case of the disease.
  2. The pathogen must be isolated and grown in pure culture.
  3. The cultured microbe must cause the disease when introduced into a healthy host.
  4. The same microorganism must be re-isolated from the experimentally infected host.

Milestones in Immunology and Chemotherapy

As scientists identified the culprits behind infections, they sought ways to protect the body and treat illnesses. Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who contracted cowpox were immune to deadly smallpox, leading to the world's first vaccination procedure using cowpox material. Decades later, Louis Pasteur developed attenuated vaccines for rabies and anthrax.

Treatment changed drastically when Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928. A mold contaminant accidentally killed bacteria on his petri dish, ushering in the antibiotic era and saving millions from bacterial infections.

Relevance to Contemporary Nursing Practice in Nigeria

Understanding the history of microbiology directly impacts daily nursing care in Nigerian hospitals and community clinics. Hand hygiene, aseptic technique, and proper waste disposal trace their roots directly to historical discoveries by pioneers like Ignaz Semelweis and Joseph Lister.

Nurses manage patients suffering from infectious diseases such as malaria, tuberculosis, and Lassa fever. Knowing how microorganisms spread, reproduce, and develop drug resistance enables nurses to break the chain of infection, administer antibiotics correctly, and educate the public on sanitation and vaccination.
