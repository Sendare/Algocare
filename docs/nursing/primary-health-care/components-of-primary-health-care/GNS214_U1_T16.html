Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology

Sickle cell anaemia is a hereditary blood disorder marked by abnormal hemoglobin, known as hemoglobin S (HbS). Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, this abnormal hemoglobin polymerizes into rigid fibers. These stiff fibers distort the flexible, biconcave red blood cells into a crescent or sickle shape.

These fragile, misshapen cells have a drastically reduced lifespan, dropping from the normal 120 days down to about 10 to 20 days, which triggers chronic hemolytic anaemia. Furthermore, their rigid structures cause them to stick to vascular endothelium and clump together, blocking microvascular blood flow and causing localized ischemia, pain, and eventual organ damage.

Genetic Inheritance and Carrier Screening

Sickle cell anaemia follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. A child must inherit two abnormal hemoglobin genes—one from each parent—to develop the disease. Individuals who inherit only one abnormal gene are known as sickle cell carriers, possessing the HbAS genotype (trait), while those with two abnormal genes have the HbSS genotype.

  • HbAA: Normal adult hemoglobin with no trait or disease.
  • HbAS: Sickle cell trait; carriers are generally asymptomatic but can pass the gene to offspring.
  • HbSS: Sickle cell anaemia disease state.
  • HbSC: Another variant form combining hemoglobin S and hemoglobin C.

Pre-marital and neonatal screening programs are vital public health tools in Nigeria to identify carriers and counsel couples on reproductive risks.

Clinical Manifestations and Common Symptoms

The clinical presentation of sickle cell anaemia varies widely from person to person, but chronic fatigue, pallor, and jaundice are nearly universal due to ongoing hemolysis and anaemia. Jaundice occurs because the rapid breakdown of red blood cells overwhelms the liver's ability to conjugate and excrete bilirubin.

Children often present with dactylitis, also known as hand-foot syndrome, which is painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by micro-infractions in the small bones. Splenomegaly is common in early childhood, but repeated infarctions eventually lead to autosplenectomy, leaving the patient highly vulnerable to encapsulated bacterial infections.

Identifying and Managing Sickle Cell Crises

A sickle cell crisis represents an acute exacerbation of symptoms that requires urgent medical intervention. The types of crises include:

  • Vaso-occlusive crisis: The most common type, where sickled cells block blood vessels, causing severe ischemic pain in the bones, chest, or abdomen.
  • Sequestration crisis: Blood pools massively in the spleen or liver, causing rapid organ enlargement, severe anaemia, and hypovolemic shock.
  • Aplastic crisis: Temporary cessation of red blood cell production, frequently triggered by parvovirus B19 infection.
  • Hemolytic crisis: An accelerated rate of red blood cell destruction leading to worsening anaemia and jaundice.

Nursing management focuses on adequate hydration, aggressive pain relief with analgesics, oxygen therapy if oxygen saturation is low, and treating any underlying infection.

Diagnostic Tests and Laboratory Investigations

Accurate diagnosis relies on hematological and biochemical evaluations. The gold standard confirmatory test is hemoglobin electrophoresis, which separates different types of hemoglobin based on their electrical charge and movement through a medium.

  • Full Blood Count (FBC): Typically reveals low hemoglobin levels, reduced packed cell volume, and elevated reticulocyte counts as the bone marrow tries to compensate.
  • Sickling Test: A quick screening method using reducing agents like sodium metabisulfite to induce sickling in blood smears, though it cannot distinguish between trait and disease.
  • Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate: Usually low because sickle cells do not stack together easily like normal disc-shaped red blood cells.

Primary Health Care Prevention and Public Health

Public health strategies for sickle cell anaemia center on prevention, early detection, and reducing complication rates in endemic regions. Routine immunization programs protect asplenic patients from severe infections caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and meningococci.

Prophylactic daily oral penicillin is commonly prescribed for young children to prevent pneumococcal sepsis. Additionally, community education campaigns target pre-marital counseling to reduce the incidence of affected births through informed partner choices.

Holistic Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nurses play a central role in managing sickle cell anaemia by promoting comfort, preventing crises, and empowering families through education. Care plans must address physical pain while recognizing the emotional and psychological toll of a chronic, unpredictable illness.

Patient education focuses on avoiding known crisis triggers:

  • Maintaining high daily fluid intake to prevent blood hyperviscosity.
  • Dressing appropriately to avoid extreme cold temperatures that cause vasoconstriction.
  • Preventing hypoxia by avoiding high altitudes or strenuous overexertion.
  • Practicing good hygiene and avoiding crowded places to lower infection risks.

Long-Term Complications and Multi-Disciplinary Follow-Up

Chronic ischemia and recurrent infarctions over many years lead to progressive damage across multiple organ systems. Multi-disciplinary care is essential to monitor and manage these long-term complications:

  • Avascular Necrosis: Infarction of the femoral or humeral heads, causing chronic joint pain and mobility issues.
  • Leg Ulcers: Slow-healing wounds around the ankles due to poor skin perfusion and minor trauma.
  • Stroke: Cerebral vessel occlusion or hemorrhage, requiring neurological monitoring and sometimes chronic blood transfusions.
  • Chronic Kidney Disease: Damage to renal medullary blood vessels leading to hyposthenuria and eventual renal failure.
