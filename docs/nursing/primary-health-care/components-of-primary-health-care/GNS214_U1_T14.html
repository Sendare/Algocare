Hypertension

Definition and Blood Pressure Classification

Hypertension is a persistent elevation of systemic arterial blood pressure where the force of blood against artery walls remains consistently too high. Nurses must understand that a single elevated reading does not confirm a diagnosis; measurements require assessment on at least two separate occasions.

  • Normal: Systolic below 120 mmHg and diastolic below 80 mmHg.
  • Elevated: Systolic 120-129 mmHg and diastolic below 80 mmHg.
  • Stage 1 Hypertension: Systolic 130-139 mmHg or diastolic 80-89 mmHg.
  • Stage 2 Hypertension: Systolic at least 140 mmHg or diastolic at least 90 mmHg.

Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

The development of high blood pressure involves complex interactions between cardiac output and total peripheral vascular resistance. In primary or essential hypertension, no single identifiable medical cause exists, though genetics, aging, and environmental factors contribute heavily. Secondary hypertension stems from an identifiable underlying condition, most commonly chronic kidney disease or endocrine disorders.

Key modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors include:

  • Dietary Sodium: Excessive salt intake leads to fluid retention and increased vascular resistance.
  • Obesity: Excess adipose tissue increases metabolic demand and circulating blood volume.
  • Age and Genetics: Arterial walls stiffen naturally with age, and a family history greatly increases personal risk.
  • Lifestyle Habits: Physical inactivity, chronic stress, heavy alcohol use, and tobacco smoking accelerate vascular damage.

Clinical Manifestations and the 'Silent Killer' Phenomenon

Hypertension is widely known as the silent killer because chronic elevation of systemic blood pressure typically produces no noticeable symptoms for years. Patients often walk around feeling completely healthy while their cardiovascular system sustains silent, progressive damage. When symptoms eventually appear, they usually indicate advanced vascular involvement or dangerously high pressure.

Severe or acute blood pressure spikes may present with occipital headaches, dizziness, blurred vision, nosebleeds, and fatigue. Nurses must recognize that waiting for physical symptoms to appear before screening patients is a critical clinical error.

Screening and Clinical Assessment

Regular blood pressure screening in primary health care settings remains the cornerstone of early detection. Nurses must ensure proper technique during every measurement: the patient should sit quietly with feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level for at least five minutes prior to cuff inflation. Using an incorrect cuff size—such as a small cuff on a large arm—falsely elevates readings, leading to misdiagnosis.

Comprehensive assessment involves evaluating target organ damage through history taking, physical examination, and diagnostic tests like urinalysis, serum electrolytes, and electrocardiography.

Non-Pharmacological Management and Lifestyle Modification

Lifestyle interventions are foundational for preventing hypertension, delaying pharmacological therapy, and improving the effectiveness of blood pressure medications. Nurses play a crucial role in counseling patients on sustainable behavioral changes.

  • DASH Diet: Emphasize fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy while reducing saturated fats.
  • Sodium Restriction: Limit daily sodium intake to support fluid balance and vascular relaxation.
  • Weight Management: Maintain a healthy body mass index to reduce cardiac workload.
  • Physical Exercise: Engage in regular aerobic activity, such as brisk walking for thirty minutes most days of the week.
  • Substance Reduction: Moderate alcohol consumption and eliminate tobacco use entirely.

Pharmacological Treatment and Nursing Responsibilities

When lifestyle changes fail to control blood pressure or when initial readings are extremely high, clinicians prescribe antihypertensive medications. Common drug classes include diuretics, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and calcium channel blockers.

Nurses must monitor for common side effects such as orthostatic hypotension, electrolyte imbalances, and persistent dry cough associated with certain agents. Patient education must emphasize adherence, warning individuals never to stop taking their blood pressure medications abruptly, as this can trigger a dangerous rebound hypertensive crisis.

Complications and Hypertensive Emergencies

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension damages blood vessels throughout the body, placing excessive strain on vital organs. Major long-term complications include stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and retinopathy.

A hypertensive emergency is a life-threatening situation where blood pressure climbs extremely high—often exceeding 180/120 mmHg—accompanied by acute damage to target organs like the brain, heart, or kidneys. This requires immediate hospitalization and intravenous medication to lower pressure gradually, preventing permanent organ failure.
