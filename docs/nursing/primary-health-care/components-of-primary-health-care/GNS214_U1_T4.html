HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV/AIDS and Global Burden

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) attacks the immune system, specifically targeting CD4+ T cells, which are vital for defending the body against infections. If left untreated, the virus destroys these cells, eventually leading to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), the most advanced stage of the disease. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden globally, with Nigeria recording a significant portion of affected populations. Early detection and treatment transformation of HIV from a fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition remain central to modern nursing practice.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

Transmission occurs primarily through specific body fluids, including blood, semen, vaginal secretions, and breast milk. Saliva, sweat, and tears do not transmit the virus.

  • Sexual Contact: Unprotected vaginal or anal intercourse remains the leading route of global spread.
  • Blood Exposure: Sharing contaminated needles, unsterilized surgical equipment, or receiving unscreened blood transfusions spreads the virus directly.
  • Perinatal Route: Transmission can happen during pregnancy, labor, or breastfeeding from an untreated mother to her child.

Clinical Stages and Disease Progression

The clinical course of HIV infection progresses through distinct phases if untreated.

StageClinical Description
Acute InfectionFlu-like symptoms occur weeks after exposure with high viral loads.
Clinical LatencyThe virus replicates at low levels; patients may remain asymptomatic for years.
AIDS StageCD4 count drops below 200 cells per microliter, marked by severe opportunistic infections.

Nursing Assessment, Counseling, and Testing

Voluntary Counseling and Testing (VCT) serves as the entry point for care. Nurses must maintain strict confidentiality and provide pre-test and post-test counseling. Assessment involves taking a thorough history, identifying risk behaviors, and evaluating for signs of chronic weight loss, persistent fever, or chronic diarrhea. A compassionate, non-judgmental approach encourages patients to share accurate health information and accept diagnostic testing.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and Patient Management

Antiretroviral Therapy involves combining multiple drugs to suppress viral replication, preserve immune function, and reduce transmission risk. Adherence is critical; missing doses can cause drug resistance and treatment failure. Nurses play a major role in educating patients about side effects, scheduling routine viral load tests, and supporting lifelong compliance to therapy.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Mother-to-child transmission can occur during intrauterine life, intrapartum labor, or postnatal breastfeeding. Comprehensive PMTCT programs provide pregnant women with early ART, safe delivery practices, and infant prophylaxis. Counseling mothers on safer infant feeding options, such as exclusive replacement feeding where acceptable and affordable, helps protect newborns from acquiring the virus.

Managing Opportunistic Infections and Complications

When immunity falls, opportunistic infections exploit the body's vulnerability. Common conditions include tuberculosis, cryptococcal meningitis, candidiasis, and Pneumocystis pneumonia. Nursing care involves prompt identification of symptoms, administering prescribed prophylactic antimicrobials, monitoring vital signs, and ensuring adequate nutrition to support recovery from acute illnesses.

Stigma Reduction, Psychosocial Support, and Home-Based Care

Social stigma remains a major barrier to testing, treatment adherence, and open discussion about HIV. Nurses play a crucial advocacy role in educating communities to dispel myths and reduce discrimination. Home-based care teams provide palliative support, wound care, psychological counseling, and help link families to local support groups and nutritional resources.
