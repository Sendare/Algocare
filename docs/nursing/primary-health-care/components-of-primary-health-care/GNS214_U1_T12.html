COVID-19

Introduction and Etiology of COVID-19

COVID-19 is an infectious disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, a member of the coronavirus family. First identified in late 2019, this single-stranded RNA virus primarily attacks the respiratory system. The viral structure features characteristic spike proteins on its surface, which bind tightly to human ACE2 receptors found abundantly in the respiratory tract. Transmission occurs predominantly through respiratory droplets and aerosols expelled when an infected person breathes, talks, coughs, or sneezes.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

Once SARS-CoV-2 enters the body, it invades host cells via the ACE2 receptors, triggering replication and local tissue damage. In severe cases, the immune system overreacts, producing a dangerous surge of inflammatory cytokines known as a cytokine storm. This massive inflammatory response leads to widespread alveolar damage, capillary leakage, and acute respiratory distress.

  • Droplet transmission: Spread via large respiratory particles within close contact of less than two meters.
  • Airborne transmission: Spread via tiny aerosolized particles that linger in poorly ventilated indoor spaces.
  • Fomite transmission: Spread less commonly by touching contaminated surfaces and then touching the face.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

The clinical presentation of COVID-19 varies widely from asymptomatic infection to critical respiratory failure. Common symptoms include fever, dry cough, fatigue, and loss of taste or smell.

Assessment, Diagnosis, and Triage in Primary Health Care

Early identification at primary healthcare facilities prevents clinic crowding and protects staff. Triage officers must immediately isolate anyone presenting with fever and respiratory complaints. Diagnostic confirmation relies primarily on RT-PCR testing, which detects viral RNA, or Rapid Antigen Diagnostic Tests for quick results at the point of care. Pulse oximetry remains a vital assessment tool to catch silent hypoxemia early, even when patients report feeling relatively comfortable.

Patient Management and Nursing Care

Nursing care for hospitalized COVID-19 patients focuses on supportive therapy, airway management, and hemodynamic stability. Nurses monitor respiratory rate and oxygen saturation continuously. For non-critically ill patients, prone positioning—lying flat on the stomach—improves lung recruitment and ventilation-perfusion matching. Administration of prescribed corticosteroids, anticoagulants, and supplemental oxygen forms the cornerstone of medical therapy.

Complications and Long COVID

While many recover fully within weeks, some individuals experience prolonged health issues termed Long COVID or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2. Acute complications during the illness include acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary embolism, secondary bacterial infections, and acute kidney injury. Long COVID manifests as persistent fatigue, brain fog, chronic cough, and joint pain lasting for months after the initial infection cleared.

Infection Prevention and Control Measures

Effective infection prevention and control protects healthcare workers and stops community spread. Standard precautions must be combined with transmission-based precautions during patient care.

  • Hand hygiene: Frequent washing with soap and water or using alcohol-based hand rub.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Correct use of medical masks, respirators like N95s, face shields, gowns, and gloves.
  • Environmental hygiene: Regular cleaning and disinfection of high-touch surfaces and medical equipment.
  • Airflow management: Ensuring proper ventilation in clinical wards and isolation rooms.

Community Surveillance and Vaccination Strategies

Public health containment relies heavily on robust community surveillance and mass vaccination campaigns. Surveillance tracks infection trends, identifies new variants, and guides policy decisions. Vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce neutralizing antibodies against the spike protein without causing severe disease. Community health nurses play a vital role in vaccine advocacy, addressing hesitancy, and ensuring equitable vaccine distribution across local populations.
