Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Overview of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder marked by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. In Nigerian primary health care centres, the burden of diabetes is rising rapidly, straining local health resources. The condition alters carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism, leading to severe multi-organ dysfunction if left unmanaged. Nurses often serve as the first point of contact, making early recognition vital for halting disease progression.

Pathophysiology and Classification

Diabetes is broadly categorized into distinct clinical types, each driven by unique underlying mechanisms.

  • Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus: Characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Patients require lifelong exogenous insulin.
  • Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: Involves peripheral insulin resistance coupled with a progressive secretory defect in insulin production. It is heavily linked to lifestyle and genetic factors.
  • Gestational Diabetes Mellitus: Glucose intolerance onset or first recognition during pregnancy, driven by placental hormones countering maternal insulin action.

Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations

Identifying patients at risk involves assessing both genetic predisposition and environmental triggers. In Nigeria, urbanization, adoption of westernized diets, and physical inactivity drive the rising prevalence of Type 2 diabetes.

Patients also frequently present with blurred vision, slow-healing skin sores, and persistent fatigue.

Screening, Diagnosis, and Blood Glucose Monitoring

Accurate diagnosis relies on standardized laboratory tests rather than symptoms alone. Healthcare providers utilize fasting plasma glucose tests, oral glucose tolerance tests, or glycated hemoglobin assays. In resource-limited Nigerian primary settings, capillary blood glucose testing using glucometers is standard for routine monitoring. Nurses must instruct patients on proper self-monitoring techniques, emphasizing clean skin preparation and correct strip storage to avoid false readings caused by high ambient humidity.

Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy depends heavily on the specific classification of diabetes. For Type 2 diabetes, oral medications like metformin improve insulin sensitivity and decrease hepatic glucose production. When oral agents fail or in all cases of Type 1 diabetes, exogenous insulin therapy is mandatory. Nurses play a crucial role in teaching correct subcutaneous injection rotation sites, recognizing hypoglycemia, and managing insulin storage safely in environments with irregular electricity supply.

Medical Nutrition Therapy and Lifestyle Modification

Dietary management must respect local food cultures while promoting metabolic control. Nigerian patients benefit from education centered on portion control, increasing dietary fiber through local vegetables, and substituting refined carbohydrates with whole grains and tubers eaten in moderation. Regular physical activity enhances insulin sensitivity and assists with weight management. Nurses must tailor advice to individual financial status, physical ability, and cultural eating patterns.

Prevention and Management of Complications

Chronic hyperglycemia damages blood vessels and nerves over time, resulting in severe microvascular and macrovascular complications. Diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy represent the primary microvascular threats, while cardiovascular disease and stroke form the macrovascular risks. Acute emergencies like diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state require immediate emergency resuscitation, fluid replacement, and intravenous insulin therapy. Routine foot care inspections are non-negotiable to prevent ulceration and subsequent lower limb amputations.

Patient Education and Self-Care Support

Empowering patients to manage their condition daily remains the cornerstone of successful long-term outcomes. Education must cover symptom recognition of hypo- and hyperglycemia, foot inspection routines, and medication adherence strategies. Community-based support groups help reduce stigma, encourage peer learning, and provide emotional backing. Nurses act as educators and advocates, bridging the gap between clinical instructions and home realities.
