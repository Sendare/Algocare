Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and the Primary Health Care Context

Cancer remains a major public health challenge globally and in Nigeria, characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. In the primary health care setting, nurses serve as the first point of contact for patients presenting with suspicious symptoms. Recognizing early warning signs at the grassroots level ensures timely referral to secondary or tertiary oncology centers, which drastically improves survival outcomes.

Pathophysiology: How Cancer Develops

Tumor development follows a multi-step process involving genetic mutations that alter normal cellular behavior.

  1. Initiation: A permanent DNA mutation occurs within a normal cell, often triggered by environmental carcinogens or hereditary factors.
  2. Promotion: Altered cells are stimulated to divide repeatedly by promoting agents, forming a pre-cancerous lesion or benign growth.
  3. Progression: Accumulated genetic changes grant cells invasive properties, allowing them to break through basement membranes and form malignant tumors.
  4. Metastasis: Cancer cells travel through blood vessels or the lymphatic system to establish secondary colonies in distant organs.

Common Types of Cancer in Nigeria

Certain malignancies carry a disproportionately high burden among the Nigerian population due to viral infections, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle factors.

  • Breast Cancer: The most frequently diagnosed malignancy among Nigerian women, often presenting at younger ages compared to Western populations.
  • Cervical Cancer: Strongly linked to persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and late uptake of screening.
  • Prostate Cancer: A leading cause of cancer-related mortality among Nigerian men, characterized by late-stage presentation.
  • Lymphomas and Kaposi Sarcoma: Frequently seen in populations burdened by endemic infectious diseases and immunosuppression.

Early Detection and Screening in Primary Care

Early detection remains the most effective strategy for reducing cancer morbidity and mortality. Primary care nurses play a pivotal role in teaching self-examination techniques, such as monthly breast self-exams and performing clinical screening procedures like visual inspection with acetic acid for cervical lesions. Overcoming cultural stigmas and misconceptions about screening is a core responsibility during community health outreach.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Signs

Thorough physical assessment and history-taking are essential for identifying hidden malignancies. Nurses must remain vigilant for systemic red flags often remembered through standardized warning signs, including unexplained weight loss, persistent fatigue, and non-healing sores. Definitive diagnosis requires tissue biopsy and histopathological analysis, distinguishing benign lesions from malignant ones.

Nursing Management and Supportive Care

Caring for patients undergoing cancer treatment requires holistic nursing interventions addressing physical and psychological needs. Management focuses on mitigating treatment side effects such as myelosuppression, nausea, and alopecia. Nurses monitor laboratory values, manage chronic cancer pain using the WHO analgesic ladder, and provide nutritional counseling to prevent cachexia.

Community Education, Prevention, and Palliative Care

Comprehensive cancer care extends beyond acute hospital treatments into community outreach and end-of-life support. Palliative care improves the quality of life for patients and families facing life-threatening illnesses through symptom relief and psychosocial support. Public health nurses lead educational campaigns to dispel cultural myths regarding cancer causes, promoting early presentation and destigmatizing the disease.
