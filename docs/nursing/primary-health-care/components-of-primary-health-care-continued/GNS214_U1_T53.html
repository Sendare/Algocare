Family Planning

Introduction and Definition of Family Planning

Family planning encompasses the educational, comprehensive medical, and social activities that enable individuals, including minors and couples, to determine freely the number and spacing of their children and to select the means by which this may be achieved. It is not merely about limiting family size; it includes fertility awareness, management of infertility, and preconception health. By allowing parents to space their pregnancies, family planning ensures that each child is born into a situation where adequate physical, emotional, and financial resources are available. In the context of Nigerian healthcare, integrating family planning into routine primary care is a cornerstone strategy for population management, maternal wellness, and overall national development.

Health Benefits for Mothers and Children

Adequate birth spacing directly correlates with reduced rates of maternal and infant mortality. When pregnancies occur too closely together—less than twenty-four months apart—the mother's body lacks sufficient time to replenish depleted nutrient stores such as iron and folate, increasing the risk of anaemia, gestational hypertension, premature rupture of membranes, and postpartum haemorrhage. For the infant, closely spaced siblings face higher probabilities of low birth weight, intrauterine growth restriction, and early childhood stunting. Promoting effective contraception breaks this cycle of depletion, allowing maternal physiological recovery and fostering healthier developmental milestones for growing children.

Classification of Contraceptive Methods

  • Hormonal methods: These include oral contraceptive pills, injectable depot preparations like DMPA, implants, and hormonal intrauterine devices that suppress ovulation or alter cervical mucus.
  • Barrier methods: Male and female condoms, diaphragms, and cervical caps that physically block sperm from reaching the ovum while also offering protection against sexually transmitted infections.
  • Intrauterine devices (IUDs): Long-acting reversible copper-bearing or hormonal devices placed inside the uterine cavity to create a spermicidal environment.
  • Permanent methods: Surgical sterilisation procedures such as bilateral tubal ligation for women and vasectomy for men, intended for those who have completed their family size.
  • Natural family planning: Behavioural approaches like the calendar rhythm method, basal body temperature tracking, and cervical mucus observation to identify the fertile window.

Client Assessment and Counselling

Effective family planning counselling relies on the GATHER framework: Greet the client respectfully, Ask about their reproductive goals, Tell them about available methods, Help them make an informed choice, Explain how to use the chosen method, and Return for follow-up. Nurses must conduct a thorough medical history screening using the Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use to rule out contraindications such as severe hypertension, active thromboembolism, or undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding. Counselling must be completely voluntary, devoid of provider bias, and tailored to the client's lifestyle, cultural background, and future reproductive intentions.

Administration and Management of Services

Providing family planning services within primary healthcare clinics demands meticulous stock management, sterile technique adherence, and accurate record-keeping. Contraceptive commodities must be stored according to manufacturer guidelines regarding temperature and light exposure to maintain pharmacological efficacy. Nurses administer injectables using proper intramuscular or subcutaneous techniques, insert implants under sterile aseptic conditions in the subdermal aspect of the upper arm, and fit IUDs following a strict bimanual pelvic examination. Waste segregation, particularly for used needles, lancets, and contaminated gauze, must follow strict infection prevention protocols to protect both staff and the community.

Managing Side Effects and Complications

Many clients discontinue contraceptive use prematurely due to minor, non-harmful side effects such as irregular spotting, amenorrhoea, mild weight changes, or headaches, particularly with hormonal methods. Nurses play a vital counseling role by anticipating these side effects before initiation, explaining their physiological basis, and offering reassurance or mild symptomatic relief. Conversely, nurses must remain vigilant for severe red-flag complications—such as the severe chest pain, shortness of breath, and severe leg swelling associated with thromboembolism, or the severe pelvic pain and fever indicating pelvic inflammatory disease in IUD users—and facilitate prompt referral when necessary.

Overcoming Barriers and Community Mobilization

In many Nigerian communities, uptake of family planning services faces cultural, religious, and socio-economic hurdles, including myths that contraceptives cause permanent sterility, spousal opposition, and financial constraints. Nurses must collaborate with local opinion leaders, traditional birth attendants, and religious figures to dispel misinformation and promote community-wide health literacy. Outreach programmes, male-involvement campaigns, and youth-friendly service corners help normalise reproductive health discussions and bridge the gap between clinic walls and grassroots populations.
