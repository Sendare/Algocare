Oral Rehydration Therapy

Introduction to Diarrhoeal Diseases and Dehydration in Children

Diarrhoeal disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among under-five children in Nigeria. Frequent, watery stools lead to rapid depletion of water and essential electrolytes, triggering dehydration. When fluid losses exceed intake, the body enters a hypovolemic state that can progress to hypovolemic shock, renal failure, and death within hours if intervention is delayed.

In primary health care settings, recognizing early fluid deficits prevents unnecessary hospital admissions and saves lives. Viral infections, particularly rotavirus, account for a large share of acute watery diarrhoea cases, while bacterial pathogens and parasites also contribute. The primary clinical objective in managing acute watery diarrhoea is not necessarily stopping the stool output immediately, but rather preventing and treating dehydration until the self-limiting illness resolves.

Definition and Principles of Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

Oral Rehydration Therapy refers to the simple, low-cost method of giving a patient a drink of clean water mixed with specific proportions of salt and sugar to treat dehydration caused by diarrhoea. The fundamental scientific breakthrough behind ORT relies on the coupling mechanism in the intestinal mucosa, where the active absorption of glucose drags sodium and water molecules across the cell membrane into the bloodstream, even when secretory diarrhoea is actively flushing fluids out.

  • Safety: ORT can be safely administered at home by mothers or caregivers, reducing the burden on healthcare facilities.
  • Efficacy: It corrects electrolyte imbalances as effectively as intravenous fluids for mild to moderate dehydration.
  • Economy: Ingredients are inexpensive and widely accessible across primary health clinics in resource-limited regions.

The Physiology of Glucose-Coupled Sodium Transport

The small intestine absorbs water and electrolytes continuously. During infectious diarrhoea, secretory mechanisms in the crypt cells pump massive amounts of chloride and water into the gut lumen, overwhelming the absorptive capacity of the villi. However, the sodium-glucose cotransporter-1 (SGLT-1) located in the apical membrane of the enterocyte remains functional during acute diarrhoeal episodes.

When glucose and sodium are present together in the lumen, SGLT-1 actively transports one molecule of sodium and one molecule of glucose into the cell simultaneously. This creates an osmotic gradient that pulls water across the tight junctions and cellular membranes into the extracellular fluid and bloodstream. Crucially, this transport system does not depend on the integrity of the cyclic AMP-mediated secretory channels blocked by cholera toxin, which explains why ORT works effectively regardless of the specific pathogen causing the watery stool.

Types and Composition of Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)

Standard World Health Organization Oral Rehydration Salts have evolved to optimize clinical outcomes and reduce stool output duration. Reduced-osmolarity ORS contains lower concentrations of anhydrous glucose and sodium chloride, which decreases the osmotic pressure of the solution and prevents hypernatraemia.

Assessment of Dehydration Severity in Clinical Practice

Accurate clinical classification guides the appropriate treatment plan for a child with diarrhoea. Nurses assess hydration status by evaluating general appearance, eye appearance, skin turgor, and thirst.

  • No Dehydration: The child is alert, eyes are normal, skin turgor goes back immediately, and drinks normally without eagerness.
  • Some Dehydration: The child appears restless or irritable, eyes are sunken, skin pinch goes back slowly (between one and two seconds), and drinks eagerly with thirst.
  • Severe Dehydration: The child is lethargic or unconscious, eyes are very sunken and dry, skin pinch goes back very slowly (longer than two seconds), and drinks poorly or is unable to drink at all.

Misjudging severe dehydration as 'some' or 'no' dehydration delays urgent intravenous resuscitation, putting the child's life at severe risk.

Administration and Preparation of ORS Solutions

Proper reconstitution and administration ensure safety and effectiveness. A single packet of ORS must be mixed into the exact volume of clean, safe water specified on the packet, usually one liter or a measured local container like a 500ml or 200ml marker bottle. Using too little water creates a hypertonic solution that draws water out of the intestinal tissues, worsening dehydration. Using too much water dilutes the electrolyte concentration, reducing the efficiency of sodium-glucose cotransport.

During the first four hours of treatment for some dehydration, calculate the target volume based on the child's weight (approximately 75 ml/kg). Encourage the mother to feed small, frequent sips using a cup and spoon rather than a feeding bottle, which is difficult to sterilize and can introduce bacterial contamination. If the child vomits, wait ten minutes and then resume feeding slowly.

Complementary Micronutrient Therapy: Zinc Supplementation

Zinc supplementation is a critical component of modern diarrhoeal management in children. Clinical guidelines recommend giving elemental zinc daily for 10 to 14 days during and after an episode of acute diarrhoea. Zinc plays a vital role in restoring mucosal integrity, boosting cellular immunity, and regenerating the brush border enzymes of the small intestine.

  • Infants under 6 months: 10 mg elemental zinc daily for 10-14 days.
  • Children aged 6 months to 5 years: 20 mg elemental zinc daily for 10-14 days.

Administering zinc shortens the duration of the current diarrhoeal episode and reduces the incidence of subsequent diarrhoeal infections over the following two to three months, making it a powerful preventive intervention in primary healthcare.

Primary Health Care and Community Education on ORT

Primary health care clinics serve as the frontline for community education on oral rehydration therapy. Nurses bridge the gap between clinical science and household practice by training mothers, community health workers, and traditional birth attendants to recognize early signs of dehydration and prepare ORS correctly.

Community outreach programs emphasize home-available fluids such as clean water, rice water, or coconut water when ORS packets are not immediately accessible, while reinforcing when to seek professional care. Effective health messaging empowers caregivers to act decisively during the first sign of watery stool, drastically cutting infant mortality rates across Nigerian communities.
