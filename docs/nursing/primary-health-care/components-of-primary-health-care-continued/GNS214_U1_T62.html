Diseases Preventable by Immunization

Introduction to Vaccine-Preventable Diseases in Nigeria

Vaccine-preventable diseases remain a significant public health challenge across Nigeria, disproportionately affecting infants and young children in resource-limited settings. Immunization stands as one of the most cost-effective medical interventions for reducing childhood morbidity and mortality. When a large portion of a community is immunized, herd immunity develops, protecting vulnerable individuals who cannot receive vaccines due to age or medical contraindications. Nurses in Nigerian primary healthcare centers play a frontline role in identifying suspected cases, maintaining community trust, and bridging immunization gaps.

The Expanded Programme on Immunization and National Schedule

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), launched by the World Health Organization and adopted nationally, provides a structured framework for delivering routine vaccines. In Nigeria, the National Programme on Immunization outlines specific ages when antigens must be administered to provide optimal protection before natural exposure occurs.

  • Birth: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) and oral polio vaccine (OPV zero).
  • 6 weeks: Pentavalent vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, rotavirus, and OPV first dose.
  • 10 weeks: Second doses of pentavalent, pneumococcal, rotavirus, and OPV.
  • 14 weeks: Third doses alongside inactivated polio vaccine and the first dose of measles.
  • 9 months: Second dose of measles and yellow fever vaccine.

Tuberculosis and Poliomyelitis Management

Tuberculosis is a chronic bacterial infection caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, presenting with persistent cough, weight loss, and night sweats. The intradermal BCG vaccine prevents severe miliary and meningeal forms of tuberculosis in infants, leaving a characteristic scar on the left upper arm. Poliomyelitis is a viral infection targeting the central nervous system, leading to acute flaccid paralysis. While the oral polio vaccine induces local mucosal immunity in the gut, the Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV) stimulates robust systemic humoral immunity. Eradication efforts in Nigeria rely heavily on meticulous surveillance of acute flaccid paralysis cases.

Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus

The pentavalent vaccine combines protection against five distinct pathogens, including diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus. Diphtheria causes a thick grey pseudomembrane in the pharynx, obstructing airways and releasing systemic toxins. Pertussis, or whooping cough, is characterized by paroxysmal coughing spells ending in a high-pitched inspiratory sound, which can cause severe hypoxia in neonates. Tetanus arises from *Clostridium tetani* spores entering wounds, producing painful muscle spasms and trismus. Nurses must monitor for local swelling and low-grade fever after administering pentavalent doses, reassuring parents that these mild reactions are normal.

Measles, Yellow Fever, and Hepatitis B

Measles is a highly contagious viral illness presenting with a generalized maculopapular rash, high fever, and Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa. Yellow fever is a viral hemorrhagic disease transmitted by infected *Aedes* mosquitoes, characterized by jaundice and organ failure. Hepatitis B is a blood-borne virus that attacks the liver, leading to chronic cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma if contracted perinatally. The first dose of the measles vaccine is scheduled at fourteen months in some older guidelines, but current Nigerian schedules administer it at nine months to match early vulnerability patterns.

Meningococcal Meningitis and Haemophilus Influenzae Type b

Bacterial meningitis and invasive respiratory infections pose severe threats to young children across the meningitis belt of Nigeria. Haemophilus influenzae type b causes severe pneumonia, epiglottitis, and meningitis, and is effectively prevented by the component included in the pentavalent vaccine. Meningococcal disease spreads rapidly through respiratory droplets in crowded environments, causing sudden fever, neck stiffness, and petechial rashes. Nurses must recognize early signs of meningeal irritation, such as positive Kernig and Brudzinski signs, to facilitate rapid medical intervention.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Target Diseases

Clinical assessment of vaccine-preventable diseases requires careful history-taking and physical examination. Nurses look for specific syndromic markers: generalized rashes for measles, respiratory distress and stridor for diphtheria or pertussis, and neurological deficits for polio or meningitis. A common misconception in clinical settings is that a minor upper respiratory tract infection or low-grade fever is a true contraindication to vaccination; in reality, mild acute illnesses do not warrant postponing routine immunization. Assessing the child's immunization card is always the most reliable way to determine actual protection status.

Community Surveillance and Cold Chain Management

The efficacy of vaccines depends entirely on maintaining proper temperature ranges from the manufacturing plant to the point of administration, a system known as the cold chain. Vaccines like polio and measles are heat-sensitive and lose potency if exposed to high ambient temperatures, while freeze-sensitive antigens like tetanus toxoid are damaged if allowed to freeze. Vaccine vial monitors on packaging help nurses check heat exposure. Community surveillance involves active reporting of any unusual disease clusters or paralysis cases to local health authorities to prevent outbreaks before they spread.

Nursing Roles in Vaccine Promotion and Client Education

Nurses act as educators, advocates, and administrators in immunization programs. Dispelling myths, addressing cultural hesitations, and explaining the benefits of completing the full vaccination schedule are core responsibilities during every clinic visit. Nurses must document every dose administered accurately in both the facility register and the child's home-based record card to track defaulters and ensure follow-up. Building open, respectful dialogue with caregivers improves long-term community compliance and coverage rates.
