Interruption of the Pathway of Infection

The Chain of Infection

Infectious diseases do not appear out of nowhere; they follow a predictable sequence known as the chain of infection. Breaking this chain at any point stops the spread of disease.

  • Infectious Agent: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites that cause disease.
  • Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives, grows, and multiplies, including humans, animals, soil, and water.
  • Portal of Exit: The route the pathogen uses to leave the reservoir, such as respiratory droplets, blood, or bodily fluids.
  • Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen travels from the reservoir to a new host, via contact, droplets, airborne particles, or vectors.
  • Portal of Entry: The path through which the pathogen enters the new host, like broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract.
  • Susceptible Host: A person who lacks immunity or physical resistance to overcome the invading pathogen.

Principles of Interrupting Transmission

Controlling infectious diseases relies on blocking the pathways between the source of infection and vulnerable individuals. Infection control policies depend on targeting the weakest links in the chain. When direct contact cannot be avoided, barrier methods like gloves, masks, and gowns create physical blocks. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention to stop cross-contamination in busy Nigerian clinical wards. Sterilizing equipment destroys microbial life before it reaches a sterile body site, while isolating infectious patients removes the source from the general hospital population.

Standard Precautions and Universal Hygiene

Standard precautions apply to all patients receiving care in any healthcare setting, regardless of their diagnosed or suspected infection status. These measures assume that every bodily fluid, secretion, and excretion may contain transmissible infectious agents.

  • Hand Hygiene: Performed before and after direct patient contact, after removing gloves, and after touching contaminated items.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Gloves, masks, eye protection, and gowns selected based on the expected exposure risk.
  • Respiratory Hygiene: Covering the mouth and nose with a tissue or elbow during coughing or sneezing.
  • Safe Injection Practices: Using sterile, single-use needles and syringes for every injection without recapping needles.
  • Environmental Cleaning: Regularly wiping down surfaces with appropriate disinfectant solutions to reduce the bioburden.

Aseptic Techniques and Sterilization

Asepsis means the absence of disease-producing microorganisms. Medical asepsis, or clean technique, reduces the number of organisms and prevents their spread. Surgical asepsis, or sterile technique, eliminates all microorganisms from an object or area.

FeatureMedical AsepsisSurgical Asepsis
GoalReduce number and spread of pathogensEliminate all microorganisms completely
ApplicationMedication administration, routine hygieneSurgery, catheter insertion, wound dressings
EnvironmentClean workspaceSterile field created with sterile drapes

Methods of achieving sterility include autoclaving, which uses pressurized steam to destroy all microbial life including bacterial spores. Chemical sterilants are reserved for heat-sensitive instruments.

Immunization and Host Resistance

Strengthening the susceptible host provides active defense against infection. Immunity can be acquired naturally through contracting a disease or artificially through vaccination. Vaccines introduce weakened or inactivated antigens to stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies without causing the full-scale illness.

Beyond immunizations, host resistance relies on general health status. Poor nutrition, chronic fatigue, stress, and underlying illnesses compromise the immune system, leaving individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Nurses educate communities on balanced diets, adequate rest, and completing routine vaccination schedules to build robust host defenses.

Environmental Sanitation and Vector Control

Controlling the external environment prevents pathogens from reaching humans through water, food, or vectors. Safe water supply systems, proper sewage disposal, and food hygiene are foundational public health measures.

In tropical environments like Nigeria, vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue fever demand rigorous vector control. Mosquito nets treated with insecticides, clearing stagnant water pools around housing units, and using insect repellents interrupt the transmission cycle by reducing the vector population. Hospital waste management also falls under environmental sanitation, ensuring infectious sharps and pathological waste are safely incinerated.

Role of the Nurse in Primary Health Care Settings

Nurses act as the frontline educators and implementers of infection prevention strategies in primary health care settings. In local clinics and community outreach programs, nurses teach families about safe drinking water, proper handwashing techniques, and environmental cleanliness. They manage immunization schedules for infants and pregnant women, ensuring herd immunity within the community. During home visits, nurses identify environmental hazards and model safe wound care practices, empowering patients to take charge of their health and break the chain of infection at home.
