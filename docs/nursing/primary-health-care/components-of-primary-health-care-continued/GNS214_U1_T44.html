Concept of Safe Motherhood

Definition and Core Pillars of Safe Motherhood

Safe motherhood refers to the strategies, activities, and services designed to ensure that women experience healthy pregnancies and childbirth, resulting in optimal health for both mother and newborn. It is not merely the prevention of maternal death and illness, but a holistic approach to reproductive health.

  • Family Planning: Ensures individuals have the information and means to plan their families, preventing unwanted pregnancies and dangerous illegal abortions.
  • Antenatal Care (ANC): Provides early detection and management of complications during pregnancy, birth preparedness, and complication readiness.
  • Obstetric Care: Guarantees access to skilled attendance during labor and delivery, alongside immediate emergency care when complications arise.
  • Postnatal Care: Focuses on the critical weeks following delivery to monitor recovery, support breastfeeding, and care for the newborn.

The Three Delays Model in Maternal Mortality

Understanding why women die during childbirth requires looking beyond direct clinical causes like hemorrhage or sepsis. The Three Delays Model helps Nigerian nurses analyze barriers to accessing timely, life-saving obstetric care.

  1. First Delay: Delay in deciding to seek care. This stems from a lack of problem recognition, low status of women, financial constraints, and cultural beliefs.
  2. Second Delay: Delay in reaching a medical facility. Poor road networks, long distances, lack of transport, and high transportation costs trap rural women.
  3. Third Delay: Delay in receiving adequate care once at the health facility. Staff shortages, lack of blood supplies, erratic power supply, and stockouts of essential drugs cause preventable deaths right inside the hospital.

Essential Interventions Across the Continuum of Care

The continuum of care connects healthcare services throughout a woman's life stages, bridging the home, community, and health facilities.

During pregnancy, nurses provide tetanus toxoid vaccination, intermittent preventive treatment for malaria, and iron-folic acid supplementation. Intrapartum care emphasizes skilled birth attendance, monitoring labor using the partograph, and practicing active management of the third stage of labor to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. Postnatal care addresses immediate maternal assessment, newborn resuscitation when necessary, and immediate initiation of breastfeeding. Maintaining this uninterrupted chain of services drastically reduces maternal and neonatal morbidity across Nigeria.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care Services

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC) is categorized into Basic and Comprehensive levels to manage life-threatening complications.

  • Basic EmONC (BEmONC): Provided at primary health centers. Includes administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonic drugs, and anticonvulsants; manual removal of the placenta; removal of retained products; and assisted vaginal delivery alongside newborn resuscitation.
  • Comprehensive EmONC (CEmONC): Provided at hospitals. Includes all BEmONC functions plus blood transfusions and life-saving surgeries like Caesarean sections.

Midwives and nurses working in primary care must recognize when a patient exceeds BEmONC capacity and initiate prompt referral to a CEmONC facility.

Role of the Nurse and Midwife in Safe Motherhood

Nurses and midwives are the backbone of maternal and child health services in Nigeria. Their scope extends beyond clinical delivery rooms to community mobilization and health education. They assess pregnant women, detect deviations from normal physiological parameters, plot partographs accurately during labor, and administer prescribed life-saving medications. Beyond clinical duties, they act as advocates for women's health, counseling families on birth spacing, nutrition, and recognizing danger signs. By fostering a welcoming, culturally sensitive clinical environment, midwives encourage more women to abandon dangerous traditional birth practices in favor of facility-based care.

Community Participation and MultISECTORAL Collaboration

Achieving safe motherhood requires active engagement beyond the walls of the clinic. Communities must be involved in identifying their own health needs and creating local solutions, such as community emergency transport schemes. Traditional birth attendants, village chiefs, and religious leaders act as vital bridges between rural populations and formal health facilities. Intersectoral collaboration brings together ministries of health, education, works, and water resources to address root determinants of maternal mortality, such as poverty, female illiteracy, poor road infrastructure, and lack of potable water.

Monitoring, Challenges, and the Way Forward in Nigeria

Nigeria continues to grapple with high maternal mortality ratios driven by persistent systemic challenges. Inadequate health financing, brain drain of skilled healthcare personnel, frequent drug stockouts, and poor data collection systems hinder progress. Monitoring safe motherhood programs relies on accurate maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) systems, which track every maternal death to learn preventability lessons. Strengthening health systems requires increased budgetary allocation, continuous professional training for midwives, and expanding health insurance coverage to protect vulnerable rural women from catastrophic out-of-pocket health expenditures.
