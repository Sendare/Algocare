Sterilization of Instruments

Introduction and Pre-requisites to Instrument Sterilization

Sterilization is the complete elimination of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial endospores. In Nigerian healthcare facilities, from tertiary teaching hospitals down to primary health centres, proper instrument processing breaks the chain of infection. Before any tool enters a sterilizer, it must undergo meticulous decontamination and cleaning.

Decontamination involves soaking used instruments in a 0.5% chlorine solution for ten minutes immediately after use to neutralize blood-borne pathogens like HIV and Hepatitis B. Following decontamination, thorough physical cleaning with water, brush, and detergent removes organic matter such as blood, tissue, and mucus. Organic debris shields microorganisms from the sterilizing agent, rendering the process ineffective. Cleaning must always precede sterilization because dirt cannot be sterilized; it can only be baked on.

Physical Methods: Autoclaving and Steam Sterilization

The autoclave uses pressurized steam to achieve high temperatures that destroy all microorganisms rapidly. It remains the gold standard for heat-stable instruments in most clinical settings.

Successful steam sterilization relies on four interdependent factors:

  • Temperature: Typically set at 121°C (250°F) or 134°C (273°F).
  • Pressure: Usually 15 pounds per square inch (psi) above atmospheric pressure at 121°C.
  • Time: Varies based on whether items are wrapped or unwrapped, often taking 15 to 30 minutes.
  • Contact: Steam must freely circulate and touch every surface of the instrument.

Overloading the autoclave chamber is a frequent error that blocks steam penetration and results in unsterilized equipment.

Dry Heat and Alternative Sterilization Techniques

When instruments are prone to rusting or cannot tolerate moisture, dry heat sterilization using a hot air oven is employed. This method relies on conduction; heat is absorbed by the outer surface and passes inward. It requires higher temperatures, such as 160°C for two hours or 170°C for one hour, and is ideal for sharp cutting instruments, glassware, and powders.

Boiling water is sometimes used in resource-limited primary care settings when electricity or gas for autoclaves fails. However, boiling at 100°C for 20 minutes only achieves high-level disinfection, not true sterilization, because it does not reliably destroy bacterial endospores. Chemical sterilants like glutaraldehyde are reserved for heat-sensitive items but demand strict adherence to immersion times and safety gear.

Monitoring and Quality Control

Ensuring that an instrument is genuinely sterile requires routine quality checks across three categories:

  1. Mechanical indicators: Gauges on the sterilizer that display temperature, pressure, and time during the cycle.
  2. Chemical indicators: Tape, strips, or special labels that change color when exposed to specific temperatures or chemical conditions, confirming exposure but not absolute sterility.
  3. Biological indicators: Vials containing bacterial spores (such as *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* for steam autoclaves) that test if the machine actually kills resilient life forms. This is the only method that confirms true sterility.

Packaging, Storage, and Maintaining Sterility

Sterilization is meaningless if instruments become contaminated before use. Proper packaging materials—such as medical-grade paper, woven muslin cloth, or specialized pouches—must allow the sterilizing agent to enter while keeping microorganisms out after processing.

Once sterilized, packs must be handled carefully. Hot packages should never be placed on cold metal surfaces, as condensation will draw moisture and ambient bacteria through the wrapper, a process known as strike-through contamination. Sterile items must be stored in clean, dry, closed cupboards away from sinks and exterior walls. Storage shelf life is event-related rather than time-related: a pack remains sterile indefinitely unless the wrapper is torn, wet, or punctured.
