Post-Natal Care

Introduction and Timing of Post-Natal Care

Post-natal care refers to the specialized healthcare services provided to the mother and her newborn immediately following childbirth until six weeks postpartum. This critical window marks the transition back to a non-pregnant physiological state for the mother while establishing extrauterine adaptation for the newborn. The World Health Organization recommends a structured schedule of contact to monitor recovery and prevent maternal or neonatal mortality.

  • First contact: Within 24 hours of birth, whether delivery occurred in a health facility or at home.
  • Second contact: Between day 4 and day 7 postpartum.
  • Third contact: At 6 weeks postpartum for a comprehensive clinical review.

Failing to attend these scheduled checks leaves dangerous conditions like postpartum hemorrhage or neonatal sepsis undetected until it is too late.

Physiological Changes and Puerperium Involution

The puerperium is the six-week period following childbirth during which the mother's anatomical and physiological systems return to their pre-pregnant state. The most prominent process is uterine involution, where the muscular organ rapidly contracts, shrinks, and descends into the true pelvis. Immediately after delivery, the fundus is palpable midway between the symphysis pubis and the umbilicus, descending roughly one centimeter per day until it is no longer palpable abdominally by the tenth day.

As the uterus heals, it discharges lochia, a specialized uterine flow that changes in color and composition over time:

  • Lochia rubra: Bright red, occurring from day 1 to day 3, composed mainly of blood, decidual tissue, and mucus.
  • Lochia serosa: Pinkish-brown, occurring from day 4 to day 10, containing serum, leukocytes, and old blood.
  • Lochia alba: Yellowish-white, occurring from day 11 to week 3 or 4, consisting mostly of epithelial cells and mucus.

A foul-smelling lochia or a uterus that remains large and boggy signals subinvolution or uterine infection.

Comprehensive Post-Natal Maternal Assessment

Evaluating a post-natal mother requires a systematic head-to-toe physical examination alongside a psychosocial check. Nurses must measure vital signs regularly, paying close attention to temperature spikes that indicate puerperal sepsis and blood pressure changes that point toward postpartum pre-eclampsia. Inspect the perineum and any episiotomy or tear repair daily using the REEDA scale (Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis, Discharge, and Approximation) to ensure proper wound healing.

Mental health screening is equally vital. The transient hormonal shifts following delivery frequently trigger the postpartum blues, characterized by mild weepiness and anxiety that resolves within two weeks. If these mood disturbances persist or worsen into profound despair, suicidal ideation, or inability to care for the infant, suspect postpartum depression and facilitate immediate psychological intervention.

Newborn Assessment and Routine Care

The transition from the intrauterine environment requires prompt, methodical newborn assessment and ongoing maintenance. Conduct a thorough physical examination to check for congenital abnormalities, evaluate muscle tone, and verify normal respiratory and cardiovascular function. Maintain infant warmth through skin-to-skin contact to prevent cold stress, which rapidly depletes brown fat and causes metabolic acidosis.

Identification and Management of Post-Partum Complications

Post-partum complications can escalate rapidly and threaten maternal survival if detection is delayed. Post-partum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal delivery or 1000 mL following a cesarean section. Uterine atony is the most frequent culprit, managed through aggressive uterine massage, oxytocin administration, and bimanual compression.

Infection is another major hazard. Puerperal sepsis manifests as maternal fever, lower abdominal pain, and abnormal lochia. Ensure strict aseptic technique during examinations and administer prescribed broad-spectrum antibiotics promptly. Monitor closely for deep vein thrombosis by assessing for unilateral calf pain, swelling, and warmth in postpartum mothers who have undergone prolonged immobility.

Post-Natal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Recovery from childbirth places high metabolic demands on the mother's body, particularly for those who are breastfeeding. Nutritional counseling must emphasize an increased intake of protein, iron-rich foods, calcium, and fresh fruits, alongside generous hydration to support milk synthesis. Micronutrient supplementation, including iron and folic acid, should continue throughout the early postpartum weeks to combat anemia.

Personal hygiene prevents ascending reproductive tract infections. Advise mothers to wash their perineal area with clean water from front to back after every voiding or bowel movement and change sanitary pads frequently. Rest is equally critical; advise the mother to sleep when the baby sleeps to avoid exhaustion and support overall physical recovery.

Family Planning and Post-Partum Sexual Health

Resumption of sexual activity after childbirth varies based on physical healing, psychological readiness, and cultural practices. Counsel couples that ovulation can occur before the return of menstruation, meaning a woman can conceive even while exclusively breastfeeding if she relies solely on the menses returning as a guide. Discuss appropriate, safe contraceptive options that do not interfere with lactation.

  • Progestin-only methods: Mini-pills, injectables, and implants are safe during lactation as they do not suppress milk supply.
  • Barrier methods: Condoms prevent both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections without hormonal side effects.
  • Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM): Effective only if the mother is fully breastfeeding, amenorrheic, and the infant is under six months old.

Address dyspareunia gently, explaining that postpartum vaginal dryness caused by low estrogen levels is temporary and managed effectively with water-based lubricants.

Community Integration and Primary Health Care Services

Post-natal care extends beyond the hospital walls into the primary healthcare setting and local communities. Community health extension workers and nurses play a pivotal role in tracing defaulters who miss their home visits or clinic appointments. Outreach programs bridge the gap for mothers in rural Nigerian communities who face transportation hurdles or financial barriers.

Health education during home visits reinforces essential newborn danger signs, such as poor suckling, lethargy, and fever, empowering families to seek timely care. Integrating traditional birth attendants into referral networks ensures that mothers delivered outside conventional facilities still receive proper postpartum assessment and infant immunizations at local primary health centers.
