Control of Diarrhoeal Diseases

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Diarrhoeal Diseases

Diarrhoea remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among under-five children in Nigeria, primarily driven by fluid and electrolyte loss. Clinically defined as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools per day, it disrupts normal intestinal transport mechanisms. Pathophysiologically, acute diarrhoea is categorized based on its mechanism and duration:

  • Secretory Diarrhoea: Toxins stimulate ion secretion into the intestinal lumen, causing copious watery stool without mucosal destruction.
  • Osmotic Diarrhoea: Unabsorbed solutes remain in the bowel lumen, drawing water across the mucosa.
  • Inflammatory/Invasive Diarrhoea: Pathogens invade the intestinal epithelial cells, causing mucosal destruction, blood in stool, and systemic inflammation (dysentery).
  • Persistent Diarrhoea: Episodes lasting 14 days or longer, frequently causing severe nutritional compromise.

Common Causes and Primary Health Care Risk Factors

In Nigerian primary health care settings, diarrhoeal episodes stem from a mix of viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens. Rotavirus is the predominant cause of severe dehydrating gastroenteritis in infants. Bacterial agents such as Escherichia coli, Shigella, and Vibrio cholerae frequently present with high fever or bloody stools. Parasites like Cryptosporidium contribute significantly, especially in immunocompromised children.

Transmission dynamics are heavily influenced by local environmental and socio-economic determinants:

  • Ingestion of fecally contaminated drinking water.
  • Poor personal hygiene, particularly infrequent handwashing with soap after defecation.
  • Inadequate human waste disposal and open drainage systems near homes.
  • Early cessation of exclusive breastfeeding, which deprives infants of maternal passive immunity.

Clinical Assessment and Dehydration Grading

A systematic clinical assessment of a child with diarrhoea requires evaluating general appearance, eye condition, skin turgor, and thirst. The World Health Organization classifies dehydration into three distinct clinical categories to guide immediate fluid management.

Management Using Oral Rehydration Therapy and Zinc

Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) remains the cornerstone of treating children with no or some dehydration. Low-osmolarity Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) solution optimizes the sodium-glucose cotransporter mechanism in the intestinal mucosa, enhancing water absorption even during active diarrhoeal fluid loss. Nurses must teach caregivers how to prepare and administer ORS correctly, using small, frequent sips from a cup rather than a feeding bottle to prevent contamination and aspiration.

Alongside ORS, daily zinc supplementation (10 mg for infants under six months, 20 mg for older children for 10-14 days) is mandatory. Zinc shortens the duration of the illness, reduces stool volume, and prevents future episodes over the subsequent two to three months by supporting mucosal cell regeneration and immune function.

Nutritional Care and Continued Feeding

Starving a child with diarrhoea is an outdated and harmful practice that worsens malnutrition and delays intestinal mucosal repair. Feeding must continue during the illness, and an extra meal should be given daily for at least two weeks after the diarrhoea stops to catch up on lost growth.

  • Breastfed infants: Continue nursing on demand, offering the breast more frequently.
  • Non-breastfed infants and older children: Provide nutrient-dense, locally available foods mashed or soft-cooked.
  • Avoid: Highly sugary drinks and commercial fruit juices, as their high osmolality can worsen osmotic diarrhoea.

Intravenous Therapy and Hospital Referral Indications

While ORS manages most cases, children with severe dehydration or specific complications require urgent intravenous (IV) fluid resuscitation and secondary facility care. Nurses must recognize these danger signs promptly to prevent hypovolemic shock and death.

  • IV Fluid Indication: Severe dehydration where the child cannot drink, is lethargic, or is unconscious (Plan C).
  • Preferred Fluid: Ringer's Lactate solution is preferred over normal saline because it helps correct metabolic acidosis.
  • Referral Triggers: Persistent vomiting rendering oral rehydration impossible, blood in stool (suspected dysentery or intussusception), signs of severe malnutrition, or failure to improve after 48 hours of home or clinic ORS therapy.

Prevention Strategies and Community Health Education

Controlling diarrhoeal diseases requires targeted public health interventions aimed at interrupting transmission cycles within communities. Nurses play a central role in health education during antenatal visits, immunization clinics, and community outreaches.

  • Water Sanitation: Promote boiling or treating drinking water with chlorine-based disinfectants.
  • Hygiene Promotion: Emphasize proper handwashing technique with soap and running water before food preparation and after using the latrine.
  • Immunization: Scale up coverage for rotavirus and measles vaccines, as measles infection frequently precipitates severe, protracted diarrhoea.
  • Exclusive Breastfeeding: Advocate for exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life to protect infants from enteric pathogens.
