Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and Basic Concepts

Immunity is the physiological ability of the human body to recognize self from non-self and defend against foreign pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. The primary structural network responsible for this defense is the immune system, which comprises specialized cells, tissues, and organs circulating throughout the body.

Key components of this defense mechanism include:

  • White blood cells (Leukocytes): The primary cellular defenders originating in the bone marrow.
  • Lymphoid organs: Structures like the lymph nodes, spleen, and thymus where immune cells mature, store, and deploy.
  • Cytokines: Chemical messenger proteins that coordinate the immune response between cells.

When a pathogen breaches physical barriers like the skin or mucous membranes, the immune system triggers a coordinated cascade to neutralize and eliminate the threat.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

Human defense mechanisms are broadly divided into two interrelated branches: innate and adaptive immunity.

  • Innate Immunity: This is the non-specific defense system every person is born with. It provides immediate, generalized protection against any foreign invader without requiring prior exposure. Components include physical barriers (skin, stomach acid), phagocytic white blood cells, and inflammatory responses.
  • Adaptive Immunity: Also called acquired immunity, this system is highly specific to a particular pathogen. It develops over time after exposure to an antigen, producing a targeted response and immunological memory that ensures a faster, stronger reaction upon subsequent encounters.

While innate immunity acts as the first and second lines of defense, adaptive immunity serves as the specialized third line of defense.

Types of Acquired Immunity: Active and Passive

Acquired immunity is classified based on whether the host's own immune system actively produces the defense mechanisms or passively receives them from an external source.

  • Active Immunity: The body is exposed to a pathogen or antigen and mounts its own immune response, generating specific T-cells and B-cells. This type is long-lasting, often lifelong, because of immunological memory.
  • Passive Immunity: Pre-formed antibodies are introduced directly into the body from another source. While this offers immediate protection, it is temporary because the host's body does not manufacture the antibodies and lacks memory cells for that pathogen.

A classic clinical example of passive immunity is the transfer of maternal antibodies across the placenta to a developing fetus or through breast milk.

Natural versus Artificial Immunity Pathways

Immunity can also be categorized by the pathway through which it is acquired: natural or artificial.

In primary health care, understanding these four distinct quadrants (natural active, natural passive, artificial active, artificial passive) helps nurses educate communities on the importance of immunization schedules and post-exposure prophylaxis.

The Role of Antigens and Antibodies

The adaptive immune response relies heavily on the interaction between antigens and antibodies.

An antigen is any substance—usually a protein on the surface of a bacterium, virus, or transplanted organ—that the body recognizes as foreign and capable of triggering an immune response.

An antibody (or immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein produced by specialized white blood cells called plasma cells (derived from B-lymphocytes). Each antibody is uniquely shaped to bind to a specific antigen, much like a lock and key. When antibodies lock onto antigens, they neutralize the pathogen directly or tag it for destruction by other immune cells, such as macrophages.

Herd Immunity and Community Protection

Herd immunity occurs when a sufficiently large percentage of a population has become immune to a specific infectious disease—either through vaccination or prior infections—thereby making the spread of disease from person to person unlikely.

Even individuals who are not eligible for vaccination, such as infants, pregnant women, or immunocompromised patients, receive indirect protection because the chain of transmission is broken within the community. The herd immunity threshold varies depending on how contagious a disease is. For instance, highly contagious viral illnesses like measles require high immunization coverage (often above 95%) to protect the population effectively.

Factors Influencing Immune Response in Primary Health Care

In clinical practice, nurses must recognize that not every patient mounts an equally effective immune response. Several physiological and environmental factors influence immunocompetence:

  • Nutrition: Protein-energy malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies (such as zinc and vitamin A) impair immune cell proliferation.
  • Age: The very young have immature immune systems, while the elderly experience immunosenescence, a gradual deterioration of immune function.
  • Stress: Chronic psychological or physical stress elevates cortisol levels, which suppresses inflammatory and immune pathways.
  • Chronic Illness: Conditions like diabetes, HIV, and renal failure directly compromise leukocyte function and antibody production.

Assessing these variables helps community health nurses identify vulnerable clients who require targeted health education and nutritional support.
