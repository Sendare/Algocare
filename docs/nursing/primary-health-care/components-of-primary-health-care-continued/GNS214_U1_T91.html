Disease Prevention

Introduction to Disease Prevention

Disease prevention encompasses measures aimed at stopping the onset, halting the progression, and reducing the disabling effects of illnesses. In public health practice across Nigeria, prevention strategies target entire communities as well as vulnerable groups. Effective intervention shifts the burden of disease away from overstretched healthcare facilities by addressing root determinants of health such as poor sanitation, inadequate nutrition, and limited health literacy.

Levels of Prevention: Primordial and Primary

Prevention is categorized into distinct stages based on the timeline of disease development.

  • Primordial prevention: Focuses on establishing and maintaining conditions that minimize social and environmental hazards to health, such as developing clean water policies and promoting active lifestyles before risk factors appear.
  • Primary prevention: Aims to protect healthy individuals from specific agents before exposure occurs. Key activities include routine childhood immunizations, health education on safe sex, and distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets to prevent malaria.

Levels of Prevention: Secondary and Tertiary

When prevention before exposure fails, secondary and tertiary measures limit damage.

  • Secondary prevention: Focuses on early diagnosis and prompt treatment of asymptomatic disease to halt progression. Examples include cervical cancer screening and blood pressure checks in community clinics.
  • Tertiary prevention: Targets individuals with established, symptomatic disease to reduce complications, restore function, and improve quality of life. Cardiac rehabilitation programs and physiotherapy for stroke survivors fall into this category.

Immunization and Prophylactic Measures

Immunization remains one of the most cost-effective public health interventions. Vaccines stimulate the adaptive immune system to produce antibodies without causing the actual disease, conferring active immunity. Passive immunity, conversely, is acquired through the administration of pre-formed antibodies, such as tetanus antitoxin. Chemoprophylaxis, such as giving intermittent preventive treatment for malaria during pregnancy (IPTp), provides temporary protection against specific infections in high-risk populations.

Screening and Early Detection Strategies

Screening involves applying tests to apparently healthy populations to identify individuals at high risk of a disease or those in early preclinical stages. A valid screening test must demonstrate high sensitivity, ensuring true cases are not missed, and high specificity, avoiding false positives. Common community screening programs target conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and breast abnormalities. A positive screening test must always be followed by diagnostic confirmation and linkage to care.

Community Health Education and Disease Control

Health education empowers individuals and communities to take charge of their health by modifying behaviors and improving living conditions. Nurses utilize participatory learning methods, workshops, and media campaigns to address local health priorities such as hygiene practices, vector control, and maternal nutrition. Successful disease control programs require active community participation rather than top-down imposition, ensuring cultural relevance and long-term sustainability.

The Role of the Nurse in Prevention Programs

Nurses act as frontline coordinators, educators, and advocates in all levels of disease prevention. In primary health care settings, nurses conduct immunizations, perform community outreach, run antenatal clinics, and screen for non-communicable diseases. By collaborating with local leaders and interprofessional teams, nurses bridge the gap between complex health policies and grassroots community needs.
