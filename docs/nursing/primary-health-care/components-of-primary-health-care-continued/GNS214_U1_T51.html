Care of the Newborn

Immediate Care at Birth and Apgar Scoring

The birth of a newborn demands prompt, organized actions to establish extrauterine life. Immediately upon delivery, the primary duty of the attending nurse or midwife is clearing the airway if necessary and drying the infant vigorously to stimulate breathing and prevent heat loss. Skin-to-skin contact with the mother is initiated immediately for stable infants to promote bonding and stabilize temperature.

Assessment of the newborn's physiological transition relies heavily on the Apgar score, recorded at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth. A third score at 10 minutes is added if the 5-minute score remains below 7.

  • Appearance: Evaluates skin color, noting central cyanosis versus generalized pinkness.
  • Pulse: Measures heart rate, typically via auscultation or palpation of the cord base.
  • Grimace: Observes reflex irritability in response to suctioning or tactile stimulation.
  • Activity: Checks muscle tone through flexion and extension of the extremities.
  • Respiration: Assesses the strength and regularity of crying and breathing efforts.

Each category receives a score of 0, 1, or 2, culminating in a total out of 10. A score between 7 and 10 indicates good transition, 4 to 6 signifies moderate depression requiring resuscitation efforts, and 0 to 3 represents severe distress demanding immediate advanced intervention.

Physiological Adaptations of the Newborn

Transitioning from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires rapid, massive changes across all major organ systems. The respiratory system takes over gas exchange as fluid clears from the alveoli and pulmonary blood vessels dilate, drastically reducing pulmonary vascular resistance. Concurrently, the ductus arteriosus, foramen ovale, and ductus venosus begin their functional closure in response to increased blood oxygen levels and the removal of the low-resistance placenta.

Metabolically, the newborn must maintain adequate blood glucose levels independently. Hepatic glycogen stores break down rapidly after birth, putting preterm and small-for-gestational-age infants at high risk for hypoglycemia. Renal function is initially immature, marked by a low glomerular filtration rate that limits the ability to concentrate urine or handle fluid overloads. Stool elimination starts with the passage of meconium, a thick, dark, tarry substance composed of amniotic fluid, cellular debris, and mucus accumulated during gestation.

Routine Newborn Assessment and Physical Examination

A systematic physical examination establishes a baseline and uncovers congenital anomalies. The nurse observes general appearance, posture, skin color, and breathing patterns before touching the infant, as handling can alter vital signs.

Vital signs follow a specific normal range: a heart rate of 120 to 160 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 30 to 60 breaths per minute, and an axillary temperature between 36.5°C and 37.5°C. Palpation of the fontanelles provides crucial neurological data; an anterior fontanelle that feels bulging may indicate increased intracranial pressure, whereas a sunken fontanelle suggests dehydration.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns are exceptionally vulnerable to rapid heat loss due to a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, thin skin, and an absence of shivering mechanisms. Heat transfers away from the infant through four distinct physical mechanisms:

  1. Evaporation: Loss of heat when amniotic fluid or bath water turns to vapor on the skin.
  2. Conduction: Direct transfer of heat to cooler objects the infant touches, such as cold scales or tables.
  3. Convection: Flow of heat away from the body by air currents, like drafts from open windows or air conditioners.
  4. Radiation: Transfer of heat to cooler solid surfaces not in direct contact with the infant, such as cold exterior walls or windows.

To combat these pathways, nurses dry infants immediately at birth, pre-warm radiant warmers, wrap babies in warm blankets, and keep heads covered with caps. When hypothermia sets in, newborns burn brown adipose tissue (brown fat) to generate heat. This non-shivering thermogenesis consumes large amounts of oxygen and glucose, leading rapidly to hypoglycemia and respiratory distress.

Cord Care and Prevention of Infection

The umbilical stump is a potential entry point for systemic bacterial infections in neonates. Standard practice focuses on keeping the cord stump clean and dry to encourage natural mummification and separation, which typically occurs within 7 to 14 days.

Nurses instruct mothers to fold diapers below the level of the umbilicus to prevent urine contamination and allow air circulation. In many resource-limited settings, topical application of chlorhexidine digluconate is recommended to reduce bacterial colonization and prevent omphalitis. Caregivers must avoid applying traditional substances such as saliva, herbs, oils, or toothpaste to the fresh stump, as these introduce harmful pathogens. Signs of localized infection include persistent foul odor, purulent discharge, and redness or swelling extending onto the surrounding abdominal skin.

Initiation and Promotion of Breastfeeding

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life maximizes infant survival and establishes a robust milk supply through hormonal stimulation. Colostrum, the thick yellowish initial milk, provides concentrated protein, vitamins, and vital maternal antibodies that protect the fragile gut.

Successful latching depends on proper positioning where the infant's body aligns straight with the mother, tummy-to-tummy. The baby's mouth should encompass a large portion of the areola, not just the nipple, with lips flared outward like a fish. Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended for the first six months of life without supplementary water or formula. Nurses assess feeding effectiveness by monitoring swallowing sounds, wet diaper counts, and steady weight gain following the initial physiological weight loss.

Immunization and Prophylactic Medications in the Neonatal Period

Neonatal prophylaxis safeguards the newborn against preventable infectious diseases immediately following birth. Administering tetracycline or erythromycin ophthalmic ointment into the conjunctival sac prevents ophthalmia neonatorum caused by maternal gonorrhea or chlamydia exposure during passage through the birth canal.

Vitamin K administration is mandatory to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn. Because newborn intestines lack the bacterial flora needed to synthesize vitamin K independently, a single intramuscular injection of phytomenadione is given within the first hour of life to promote normal blood clotting. Additionally, the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine for tuberculosis and the birth dose of the oral polio vaccine or Hepatitis B vaccine are administered before discharge, adhering strictly to national immunization schedules.

Identifying Common Neonatal Complications and Danger Signs

Recognizing deviation from normal transition saves lives in the neonatal period. Nurses educate mothers and families on key danger signs that require urgent health facility visitation.

  • Jaundice: Yellow discoloration of the skin and sclera appearing within the first 24 hours or spreading to the palms and soles indicates pathological hyperbilirubinemia.
  • Respiratory distress: Marked by persistent tachypnea, nasal flaring, sternal retractions, and grunting.
  • Temperature instability: Recurrent hypothermia or fever without clear environmental cause.
  • Feeding intolerance: Complete refusal to feed or frequent forceful vomiting.
  • Lethargy or convulsions: Inability to wake up normally or abnormal twitching movements.

Jaundice is especially common due to immature liver enzymes breaking down fetal red blood cells, but high levels of unconjugated bilirubin risk crossing the blood-brain barrier and causing kernicterus, a permanent neurological condition. Prompt recognition and phototherapy intervention prevent this severe complication.
