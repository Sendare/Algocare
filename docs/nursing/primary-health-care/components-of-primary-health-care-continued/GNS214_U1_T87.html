Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases

A communicable disease is an illness caused by a specific infectious agent or its toxic products that arises through transmission of that agent from an infected person, animal, or inanimate reservoir to a susceptible host. These conditions remain a major public health challenge across Nigeria, contributing significantly to hospital admissions and childhood mortality. Pathogens responsible for these illnesses include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Understanding how these diseases establish and spread is the primary step for any nurse working in community or hospital settings.

Key Epidemiological Terms and Concepts

Epidemiology relies on specific vocabulary to track and measure disease frequency within a population.

  • Endemic: The constant presence of a disease or infectious agent within a given geographic area or population group.
  • Epidemic: An unexpected increase in the number of disease cases in a specific area above what is normally expected.
  • Pandemic: An epidemic that has spread over several countries or continents, affecting a large number of people.
  • Incidence: The number of new cases of a disease developing in a specific population during a specified time period.
  • Prevalence: The total number of existing cases, both old and new, in a given population at a specific point in time.

The Chain of Infection

Infectious diseases spread through a continuous sequence known as the chain of infection. Breaking any single link in this chain stops the transmission of illness.

  1. Infectious Agent: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, or parasites.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives, grows, and multiplies, such as humans, animals, or soil.
  3. Portal of Exit: The path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir, including respiratory tracts, blood, or the digestive system.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen travels to a new host, whether through direct contact, vectors, or airborne droplets.
  5. Portal of Entry: The manner in which the pathogen enters the susceptible host, such as broken skin or mucous membranes.
  6. Susceptible Host: An individual who lacks immunity or resistance to ward off the invading pathogen.

Modes of Transmission

Pathogens utilize various pathways to move from a source to a new host.

  • Direct Transmission: Physical transfer of microbes between an infected host and a susceptible individual, such as through touching, kissing, or sexual intercourse.
  • Indirect Transmission: Transfer through an intermediate step, which can involve inanimate objects like contaminated bedding, medical equipment, or water supplies.
  • Vector-Borne Transmission: Spread by living carriers, most commonly mosquitoes, ticks, or flies that carry the pathogen from one victim to another.
  • Airborne Transmission: Inhalation of droplet nuclei or dust particles that remain suspended in the air over long distances.

Common Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Clinical practice in Nigerian health facilities frequently encounters specific endemic and epidemic-prone diseases. Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity, presenting with intermittent fevers, chills, and severe anemia. Lassa fever, a viral hemorrhagic fever, spreads through contact with infected multi-mammate rat excreta or body fluids of patients. Tuberculosis (TB) continues to burden the healthcare system, characterized by a chronic productive cough, night sweats, and weight loss. Cholera outbreaks occur seasonally, often linked to contaminated water sources, leading to rapid dehydration from watery diarrhea.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

Detecting communicable diseases requires a thorough history and physical examination alongside laboratory investigations. Nurses must look for characteristic symptom patterns, such as rash distributions, fever curves, and respiratory distress. Diagnostic confirmation often relies on rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), microscopy, stool cultures, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays. Early identification not only guides individual patient management but also triggers prompt epidemiological reporting to prevent wider community outbreaks.

Prevention and Control Strategies

Controlling the spread of infectious illnesses depends on multi-layered public health interventions. Immunization programs build community immunity against vaccine-preventable scourges like measles, polio, and yellow fever. Environmental sanitation, including safe waste disposal and access to clean drinking water, reduces waterborne and vector-borne risks. Standard precautions and isolation protocols in hospitals protect both patients and healthcare workers from nosocomial transmission.

The Role of the Nurse in Primary Health Care

Nurses act as the frontline workforce in managing and preventing communicable diseases within primary health care settings. Responsibilities include administering vaccines, conducting contact tracing during outbreaks, and educating communities on hygiene practices. Nurses also ensure proper waste segregation to prevent accidental needle-stick injuries and biological contamination. Through active surveillance and community mobilization, nurses bridge the gap between policy guidelines and bedside care.
