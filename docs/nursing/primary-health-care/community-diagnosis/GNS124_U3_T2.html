Types of Health Needs and Community Diagnosis

Introduction to Health Needs in Primary Health Care

A health need is a deficiency in health that can be addressed by an intervention. In primary health care, identifying these needs accurately determines where resources go. Without understanding what a community lacks, interventions become guesswork. Nurses working at the grassroots level must recognize that a community's lack of infrastructure, clean water, or basic immunization access all constitute foundational health needs.

Classification of Health Needs: Felt, Expressed, Normative, and Comparative

Health needs are categorized into four distinct types to help nurses and public health planners design appropriate responses:

  • Felt needs: What people say they feel or want, such as a community requesting a maternity clinic.
  • Expressed needs: Felt needs that are turned into action, observed through the utilization of services, like attending an antenatal clinic.
  • Normative needs: Deficiencies defined by health professionals or experts based on established standards, such as immunization schedules.
  • Comparative needs: Disparities occurring when one group receives better health services than another similar group.

Concept and Importance of Community Diagnosis

A community diagnosis is a comprehensive assessment of the health status of a population in relation to its social, physical, and environmental context. It goes beyond treating individual patients to examining the collective health of an entire village or neighborhood. Community health nurses use this diagnosis to uncover root causes of diseases, such as poor drainage causing high malaria rates. This process empowers the community by involving them directly in uncovering their own health vulnerabilities.

Steps in Conducting a Community Diagnosis

Conducting a community diagnosis requires a systematic approach to ensure reliable findings.

  1. Pre-survey planning: Securing administrative approval and engaging community leaders.
  2. Data collection: Gathering primary and secondary health information.
  3. Data collation and analysis: Organizing figures, calculating rates, and spotting trends.
  4. Diagnosis formulation: Identifying the actual health problems and underlying causes.
  5. Action planning: Creating solutions alongside community members.
  6. Evaluation: Assessing the success of implemented interventions.

Data Collection Methods and Community Profiling

Gathering accurate information relies on a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods. Primary data collection involves direct interaction with the population through interviews, focus group discussions, and observation during house-to-house visits. Secondary data involves reviewing existing records, such as clinic registers, birth and death certificates, and census reports. Community profiling pulls all these findings together to paint a clear picture of the geography, leadership structures, and occupational hazards present in the area.

Identifying and Prioritizing Community Health Problems

A community often faces multiple health problems simultaneously, but resources are limited. Nurses cannot tackle everything at once, making prioritization essential. Criteria for prioritization include the magnitude of the problem (how many people are affected), the severity of the consequences, community concern, and the feasibility of solving it. Tools like the Hanlon method or simple scoring matrices help community health teams rank issues objectively.

Planning and Implementing Community-Based Interventions

Once priorities are set, the nurse collaborates with the community to draft an action plan. This plan outlines specific objectives, activities, responsible persons, timelines, and required resources. Implementation brings the plan to life through community mobilization, health education campaigns, and organizing clinics. Involving local stakeholders ensures sustainability long after external funding or nursing students leave the area.

Monitoring and Evaluation of Community Health Programs

Monitoring is the routine tracking of project activities to ensure work stays on schedule. Evaluation measures the final impact and effectiveness of the program against its original objectives. Together, these processes tell the healthcare team whether their interventions reduced disease rates or improved health habits. Findings from evaluations guide future health planning and policy adjustments.
