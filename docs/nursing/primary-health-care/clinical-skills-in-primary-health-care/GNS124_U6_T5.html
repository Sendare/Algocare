Nutritional Assessment

Introduction to Nutritional Assessment in Primary Health Care

Nutritional assessment is a systematic method used to evaluate the nutritional status of individuals or populations. In primary health care settings across Nigeria, nurses use this process to identify malnutrition early, whether it is undernutrition or overnutrition. Early detection prevents complications related to deficiency states like kwashiorkor, marasmus, or chronic non-communicable diseases linked to poor diet.

The assessment process follows a structured framework often remembered by the acronym ABCD: Anthropometric, Biochemical, Clinical, and Dietary methods. Each component provides distinct data that, when combined, offer a complete picture of a patient's health and nutritional reserves. Nurses must approach this process with cultural awareness, recognizing local dietary staples, economic realities, and traditional food taboos that influence what patients eat.

Anthropometric Measurements

Anthropometry involves obtaining physical measurements of the human body and comparing them against reference standards. These metrics reflect body composition and growth patterns, making them vital for both pediatric care and adult evaluation.

  • Weight: Measures total body mass. In infants and children, weight-for-age tracks growth velocity, while adult weight helps calculate body mass index.
  • Height or Length: Measured standing up for adults and older children, or recumbent for infants under two years.
  • Body Mass Index (BMI): Calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, used to screen for underweight, overweight, and obesity.
  • Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC): A rapid screening tool using a color-coded tape to detect severe acute malnutrition in community settings, especially for children aged 6 to 59 months.

Clinical Nutritional Assessment

Clinical examination relies on inspecting parts of the body that show rapid cell turnover for signs of nutrient deficiencies. Tissues such as the skin, eyes, hair, oral cavity, and nails often reveal early evidence of nutritional failure before laboratory values drop significantly.

  • Hair: Dry, sparse, easily pluckable hair or the flag sign suggests protein deficiency.
  • Eyes: Bitot's spots, dryness, or corneal clouding point directly to vitamin A deficiency.
  • Mouth: Angular stomatitis, glossitis, and bleeding gums indicate deficiencies in B-complex vitamins or vitamin C.
  • Skin: Follicular hyperkeratosis and bruising suggest deficiencies in essential fatty acids or fat-soluble vitamins.

Biochemical and Dietary Assessment

Biochemical tests provide objective verification of nutritional status by measuring nutrient levels or their byproducts in body fluids. Common tests include hemoglobin levels to screen for iron-deficiency anemia, serum albumin to evaluate visceral protein status, and electrolyte panels to check hydration and metabolic balance. While accurate, biochemical measures can be altered by inflammation and infection, which nurses must account for during interpretation.

Dietary assessment examines the quantity and quality of food consumed. Methods include 24-hour dietary recalls, where the patient lists everything eaten in the past day; food frequency questionnaires, which track how often specific food groups are consumed over time; and food diaries, where intake is recorded prospectively. In many Nigerian communities, relying solely on dietary recall can be challenging due to shared pot eating habits, requiring skilled probing by the nurse.

Socio-Economic and Environmental Assessment

A patient's nutritional health is deeply connected to their living conditions, income, education, and access to resources. A clinical diagnosis of malnutrition is often rooted in poverty, food insecurity, or poor sanitation rather than just poor food choices.

Nurses must evaluate household purchasing power, agricultural cycles, food storage facilities, and access to potable water. In local communities, seasonal food scarcity—often called the 'hungry season' before the harvest—contributes heavily to acute malnutrition spikes. Understanding these environmental factors ensures that nutritional care plans are realistic and sustainable for the household.

Synthesizing Findings and Nursing Care Planning

The final stage of nutritional assessment brings all data points together. The nurse analyzes anthropometric, clinical, biochemical, and dietary findings to form an accurate nursing diagnosis, such as *Imbalanced Nutrition: Less than Body Requirements* or *Risk for Overweight*.

Based on this diagnosis, the nurse collaborates with the patient and family to create a feasible care plan. Interventions may include therapeutic feeding, micronutrient supplementation, dietary counseling using locally available foods, or referrals to social support programs. Evaluating the success of the care plan requires continuous monitoring of weight trends, clinical signs, and dietary changes over time.
