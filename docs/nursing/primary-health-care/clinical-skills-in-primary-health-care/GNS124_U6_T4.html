Diagnostic Techniques

Clinical History Taking and Physical Examination

Clinical history taking and physical examination form the foundational diagnostic techniques available to the nurse in primary health care settings. History taking involves systematically gathering subjective data regarding the patient's chief complaint, history of present illness, past medical history, and social background. Physical examination follows using the cardinal techniques of inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. These bedside assessments require no complex equipment yet provide crucial diagnostic clues that direct subsequent laboratory testing or immediate nursing interventions.

Essential Laboratory Investigations in Primary Care

Primary care clinics rely on a core set of laboratory investigations to confirm diagnoses, monitor chronic conditions, and screen for endemic diseases common in Nigeria.

  • Malaria Microscopy and RDT: Used for definitive or rapid detection of malaria parasites in febrile patients.
  • Packed Cell Volume (PCV): Measures the proportion of blood volume occupied by red blood cells to detect anemia.
  • Urinalysis: Assesses urine for glucose, protein, blood, and signs of infection using chemical test strips.
  • Blood Glucose Testing: Utilizes glucometers or laboratory assays to screen for and monitor diabetes mellitus.

Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs) and Point-of-Care Testing

Rapid diagnostic tests and point-of-care testing have transformed healthcare delivery by bringing diagnostics directly to the patient side. These assays yield results within minutes, allowing prompt clinical decision-making without waiting for central laboratory processing. Common examples in Nigerian primary healthcare centers include malaria antigen tests, HIV screening kits, and pregnancy tests. Nurses must ensure proper storage of test kits according to manufacturer temperature specifications, as heat and humidity can degrade the reagents and cause false-negative outcomes.

Specimen Collection, Handling, and Transportation

Accurate laboratory results depend entirely on the quality of the collected specimen. Contaminated, hemolyzed, or improperly labeled samples compromise diagnostic integrity and patient safety. Nurses must verify patient identity using at least two identifiers before collection.

Proper collection containers must be used for specific tests, such as sterile bottles for microbiological culture or EDTA bottles for complete blood counts. Once collected, specimens require timely handling and adherence to cold chain protocols if transportation to an external laboratory is necessary.

Interpreting Diagnostic Results for Nursing Care

Interpreting diagnostic results requires comparing patient values against established reference ranges while considering the clinical context. A test result outside the normal range does not exist in isolation; it must be correlated with clinical signs and symptoms. For instance, a low hemoglobin level must be interpreted alongside signs of fatigue, pallor, and shortness of breath. Nurses utilize these interpretations to formulate nursing diagnoses, evaluate treatment effectiveness, and recognize critical values that demand immediate physician notification.

Quality Assurance and Safety in Diagnostics

Quality assurance in diagnostic procedures ensures that test results are reliable, reproducible, and safe for both patients and healthcare personnel. This involves routine equipment calibration, adherence to standard operating procedures, and proper disposal of infectious waste. Universal precautions must be maintained during any procedure involving blood or body fluids. Nurses play a frontline role in maintaining safety by wearing appropriate personal protective equipment, preventing needle-stick injuries, and properly labeling all diagnostic materials.
