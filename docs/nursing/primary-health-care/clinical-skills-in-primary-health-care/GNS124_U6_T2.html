Recording and Reporting Skills

Purpose and Importance of Health Records

Health records serve as the permanent chronological account of a patient's health history, diagnostic findings, treatment interventions, and clinical responses over time. In Nigerian primary health care clinics, accurate documentation ensures seamless continuity of care when different nurses take over shifts. Beyond direct patient management, health records provide the essential data backbone for public health surveillance, resource allocation, and epidemiological research. Without reliable data entry at the grassroots level, health authorities cannot accurately forecast vaccine needs, track disease outbreaks like measles or cholera, or evaluate the success of maternal and child health programs.

Types of Health Records and Reporting Tools

  • Family Health Folder: A comprehensive file housing the medical history of all household members, commonly used in primary health care.
  • Antenatal Care (ANC) Card: A patient-held card tracking maternal vitals, tetanus immunization, and pregnancy progression.
  • Child Health Card (Immunization Card): A portable document tracking childhood vaccinations, growth monitoring charts, and milestone achievements.
  • Facility Registers: Heavy bound books kept at the clinic for daily recording of specific services, such as outpatient attendance, delivery logs, and immunization tallies.
  • Monthly Summary Forms: Standardized reporting templates used to aggregate daily facility data for onward transmission to Local Government Health Authorities.

Principles of Accurate and Ethical Documentation

Clinical documentation must be contemporaneous, meaning entries are made during or immediately after the patient interaction, never hours later from memory. Nurses must write legibly, use approved medical abbreviations, and sign every entry with their full name and professional designation. Objectivity is mandatory; document observed physical findings and patient statements rather than subjective personal opinions or judgmental remarks about patient behavior. When a charting mistake occurs, draw a single neat line through the error, write 'correction', and initial it—never use correction fluid or scratch out text illegibly.

Data Collection and Tallying at the Facility Level

Data collection starts at the service delivery point where nurses use tally sheets or tick marks in registers as patients receive care. At the close of each working day, these daily marks must be summed up accurately to avoid cumulative errors in monthly reports. Staff members should cross-check tally numbers against actual patient attendance to ensure consistency. Maintaining discipline in daily tallying prevents last-minute panic and falsification of figures when monthly summary deadlines approach.

Compiling and Submitting Monthly Health Summaries

Monthly health summaries aggregate daily facility data into standardized District Health Information System (DHIS2) formats or paper summary sheets. The nurse-in-charge reviews these reports for completeness and internal consistency before the official submission deadline, typically the fifth day of the following month. Timely submission allows Local Government Area (LGA) monitoring officers to compile district-wide statistics for state and national health planning. Late submissions disrupt the national supply chain and delay emergency health interventions.

Common Errors in Documentation and How to Avoid Them

  • Omission: Leaving out vital signs, drug dosages, or exact administration times, which compromises patient safety.
  • Delayed Documentation: Writing notes long after the event, leading to inaccurate memory recall.
  • Illegibility: Poor handwriting that misleads subsequent caregivers regarding drug names or dosages.
  • Falsification: Backdating or entering unperformed procedures to cover up missed care.

To avoid these pitfalls, facilities must enforce peer review of records, maintain routine charting audits, and uphold a professional commitment to truth and accuracy.

Confidentiality, Security, and Legal Aspects of Health Records

Patient records are confidential legal documents protected by professional codes of ethics and national privacy laws. Nurses must never discuss patient diagnoses in public areas, leave open charts unattended on desks, or permit unauthorized persons to access file cabinets. In a court of law, improperly documented care is legally interpreted as care that was never performed. Therefore, meticulous charting protects both the patient's right to privacy and the nurse against clinical negligence claims.
