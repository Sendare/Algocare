Questionnaire

Introduction to Questionnaires in Nursing Research

A questionnaire is a structured data collection instrument consisting of a series of written questions and other prompts designed to gather information from respondents. Within nursing and healthcare research, questionnaires serve as a primary tool for measuring abstract concepts that cannot be directly observed, such as patient satisfaction, pain intensity, health-related quality of life, and clinical knowledge levels among nursing staff. Because clinical environments in Nigeria and globally face time constraints, questionnaires provide an efficient method to capture quantitative and qualitative data from large samples across dispersed hospital wards or community health centers simultaneously.

Types of Questionnaire Items and Response Formats

Questionnaire items generally fall into two main structural categories: closed-ended and open-ended questions.

  • Closed-ended items: Provide pre-determined options for respondents to choose from, facilitating rapid quantitative analysis. Common formats include dichotomous items (yes/no), multiple-choice items, and Likert scales which measure attitudes or intensity of agreement along a continuum.
  • Open-ended items: Allow respondents to answer in their own words, capturing rich, unexpected qualitative insights but requiring complex thematic coding during data analysis.

Selecting the appropriate format depends on whether the research objective demands precise numerical trends or deep narrative context.

Principles of Questionnaire Design and Construction

Constructing a reliable questionnaire requires rigorous adherence to linguistic and structural principles. Questions must be clear, concise, and devoid of medical jargon unless surveying specialized clinicians. A frequent pitfall in item writing is the double-barreled question, which asks two distinct things within a single sentence, forcing an ambiguous response. For instance, asking patients if they found the hospital ward clean and the nurses polite creates confusion if one condition was met and the other was not. Additionally, researchers must avoid leading questions that subtly nudge the respondent toward a desired answer, as well as emotionally loaded terms that skew data objectivity. Question ordering also matters; placing demographic or sensitive items at the very beginning can cause survey fatigue or dropouts, whereas placing them later builds rapport.

Validity and Reliability in Questionnaire Development

  • Validity: Refers to whether the questionnaire actually measures what it purports to measure. Face validity evaluates whether the tool appears valid on the surface to experts, while content validity ensures that the instrument adequately covers all facets of the construct being studied.
  • Reliability: Relates to the consistency and stability of the measurement tool. Test-retest reliability checks stability over time by administering the same questionnaire twice, whereas internal consistency (frequently measured using Cronbach's alpha) evaluates whether different items measuring the same construct yield similar results.

Modes of Questionnaire Administration

Questionnaires can be administered through various modes, each carrying distinct advantages and logistical challenges. Self-administered questionnaires can be handed out in paper format within outpatient clinics or distributed digitally via web links, promoting anonymity and reducing interviewer bias. However, digital delivery may exclude elderly or rural populations lacking internet access or digital literacy. Conversely, interviewer-administered questionnaires—conducted face-to-face or via telephone—allow clarification of ambiguous items and yield higher completion rates, but are resource-intensive and risk interviewer bias where researchers unintentionally influence participant responses through tone or body language.

Pre-testing and Piloting the Questionnaire

Before launching a full-scale study, researchers must conduct a pilot study by administering the draft questionnaire to a small sample representing the target population. This rehearsal phase identifies ambiguous wording, confusing instructions, formatting flaws, and inaccurate time estimates. If respondents consistently misinterpret a specific item or skip a section, the researcher can refine the instrument before investing significant financial and human resources into the primary study. Data gathered during the pilot phase are typically excluded from the final research analysis.

Ethical Considerations in Questionnaire-Based Studies

Ethical protection remains paramount when designing and distributing questionnaires in healthcare settings. Researchers must obtain formal informed consent from all participants, ensuring they understand the study's purpose, potential risks, and voluntary nature. Participants must possess the absolute right to withdraw from completing the questionnaire at any point without facing negative consequences to their medical care or employment. Furthermore, strict measures for anonymity or confidentiality must be maintained to protect sensitive health data from unauthorized disclosure.
