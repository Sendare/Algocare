Population

Introduction to Population in Nursing Research

In nursing research, the word population means the entire group of people that the researcher wants to study and apply their findings to. This is different from everyday language, where population usually refers to everyone living in a country or a city. If a nurse researcher is studying how a new wound care dressing works on patients with diabetic foot ulcers, the population is every single person in the world who has a diabetic foot ulcer. Because it is impossible to study every person in that global group, researchers must carefully define who belongs in their study so they know exactly who their results will apply to later.

Target Population Versus Accessible Population

Researchers break the broader concept of population down into two distinct groups to make studies practical.

  • Target population: The ideal, large group of people the researcher ultimately wants to apply their findings to. For example, all children with asthma in Nigeria.
  • Accessible population: The specific group of people the researcher can actually reach and recruit from. For example, children receiving asthma treatment at three teaching hospitals in Lagos.

While the target population represents the dream scope of the study, the accessible population represents reality. A common mistake among student researchers is claiming their results apply to the entire target population when they only sampled from a very small, localized accessible population.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Once the accessible population is identified, researchers use rules called inclusion and exclusion criteria to select exact participants.

  • Inclusion criteria: The specific characteristics a person must have to be part of the study, such as age, a specific diagnosis, or being able to speak English.
  • Exclusion criteria: The characteristics that disqualify a person from the study, even if they meet the inclusion rules. This often includes things that might confuse the study results, like severe cognitive impairment, pregnancy during a drug trial, or having a secondary terminal illness.

These rules protect patient safety and ensure that the results of the study are actually caused by the variable being tested, rather than outside factors.

Sampling Techniques and Frame Development

A sampling frame is the actual physical or digital list of everyone in the accessible population—such as a hospital admission register or a school roster. From this list, researchers use sampling techniques to choose who will take part. These techniques generally fall into two categories:

  1. Probability sampling: Every person in the population has a known, equal chance of being chosen. This includes simple random sampling and stratified sampling, and it is best for making strong generalizations.
  2. Non-probability sampling: Participants are chosen based on convenience or availability, such as asking patients who walk into a clinic today to fill out a questionnaire. This is easier and cheaper, but carries a higher risk of bias.

Determining Sample Size in Healthcare Studies

Deciding how many patients to include in a study is a critical step. If a sample is too small, the researcher might miss a real, important clinical effect. If the sample is too large, resources, time, and money are wasted, and patients might be subjected to unnecessary research burdens.

Healthcare researchers use statistical power calculations, software tools, or standard formulas to determine the right sample size. Factors that influence this number include how much variation exists in the population, how strong the expected treatment effect is, and the acceptable margin of error. In qualitative nursing research, sample size is determined differently—by data saturation, which means interviewing people until no new information or themes appear.

Addressing Bias and Generalizability

Research findings are only useful if they reflect reality and can be applied to real-world nursing practice. Generalizability refers to how well the results from a specific sample can be applied to the wider target population.

However, sampling bias can ruin generalizability. This happens when certain groups in a population are left out or underrepresented, making the sample unrepresentative. For example, if a nurse studies hospital patient satisfaction by only giving surveys to patients who are well enough to walk around the lobby, the unhappy bedridden patients are completely missed. To protect the integrity of nursing evidence, researchers must use random selection, clear criteria, and careful recruitment to minimize bias.
