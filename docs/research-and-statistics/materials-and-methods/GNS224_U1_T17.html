Sample

Introduction to Sampling in Nursing Research

Nursing research rarely investigates an entire population due to constraints of time, finance, and logistics. Instead, researchers select a subset of individuals to represent the whole. A sample is the specific group of participants from whom data are gathered, while the population encompasses the entire aggregation of cases meeting designated criteria. Effective sampling bridges individual study findings and broad clinical application. When a subset accurately reflects the broader group, results possess strong external validity, allowing clinical guidelines to be safely implemented across hospital wards and community health posts throughout Nigeria.

Target Population Versus Accessible Population

Distinguishing between who we want to study and who we can actually reach is foundational to study design.

  • Target Population: The entire group of people or elements that the researcher wishes to generalize findings to. For example, all patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes in Nigeria.
  • Accessible Population: The subset of the target population that is available and reachable for the study, such as patients attending the diabetic clinic at a specific university teaching hospital in Lagos.

Failing to separate these two groups often leads to over-generalized conclusions that do not hold true outside the immediate study setting.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Boundaries must be established to determine who qualifies for a study and who must be left out. Inclusion criteria specify the mandatory characteristics participants must possess to be eligible. Conversely, exclusion criteria define characteristics that disqualify individuals, even if they meet the initial entry requirements. These safeguards protect vulnerable patients, reduce confounding variables, and clarify exactly which clinical demographic the final data represents.

Probability Sampling Techniques

Probability sampling relies on random selection, ensuring every member of the accessible population has a known, non-zero probability of being chosen. This approach minimizes selection bias and allows researchers to calculate sampling error.

  1. Simple Random Sampling: Every individual is assigned a number, and a lottery or random number generator picks the participants.
  2. Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into homogenous subgroups, and random samples are drawn proportionally from each.
  3. Cluster Sampling: Naturally occurring groups are randomly selected instead of individuals, useful when populations are geographically dispersed across large regions.

Non-Probability Sampling Techniques

When random selection is impractical or impossible, non-probability sampling is utilized. Because not every member has a chance of being chosen, the risk of sampling bias increases, limiting statistical generalization.

  • Convenience Sampling: Enrolling readily available people who happen to meet the criteria, such as patients currently sitting in an outpatient waiting room.
  • Quota Sampling: Establishing numerical targets for specific subgroups to ensure basic representation without true random selection.
  • Purposive Sampling: Deliberately handpicking individuals who possess specialized knowledge or unique experiences relevant to the phenomenon under study.

Sample Size Determination and Power

Determining how many participants are needed is critical for scientific rigor. If a sample is too small, the study may lack statistical power to detect true clinical differences, resulting in a false negative. Conversely, an excessively large sample wastes resources and unnecessarily exposes subjects to burden. Factors influencing sample size calculations include the anticipated effect size, significance level, and planned data analysis methods. Formal power analysis software or established statistical tables guide researchers in arriving at an defensible number before data collection begins.

Sampling Bias and Error

No sample mirrors a population perfectly. Sampling error represents the natural, random variation that occurs because data is collected from a subset rather than the whole group. This type of error decreases as sample size increases. Sampling bias, however, is a systematic flaw where certain members of a population are overrepresented or underrepresented due to poor sampling technique, such as relying purely on volunteers. Rigorous design, transparent reporting, and randomized selection help control these threats to study integrity.
