Sample

Introduction to Sampling in Nursing Research

Nursing research relies heavily on studying a smaller group of people to understand a much larger health issue. Imagine trying to check the blood pressure of every pregnant woman in a country—it is simply impossible due to time, money, and distance. Instead, researchers select a manageable subgroup. This process is called sampling. The broader group that the researcher wants to study and apply findings to is the population, while the smaller group actually measured is the sample. Good nursing care is built on evidence-based practice, and trustworthy evidence requires a well-chosen sample that truly mirrors the characteristics of the patients nurses care for every day.

Target Population Versus Accessible Population

When planning a study, nurses must clearly define who they want to study. The target population is the ideal group of people the researcher wants to generalize the findings to—for example, all patients with type 2 diabetes living in Nigeria. However, a researcher cannot possibly reach every single diabetic patient across every remote village and city. Therefore, they work with an accessible population, which is the specific group they can actually reach and recruit, such as patients attending the diabetic clinic at a specific teaching hospital. Recognizing the gap between these two groups helps nurses determine how far they can safely stretch their research conclusions.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To keep a study focused and safe, researchers set boundaries for who can join. Inclusion criteria are the mandatory characteristics a participant must have to be part of the study, such as being over 18 years old and diagnosed with hypertension. Exclusion criteria are the conditions or characteristics that disqualify someone from participating, even if they meet the inclusion rules. For instance, a researcher might exclude pregnant women or patients with severe cognitive impairment to protect vulnerable groups or prevent skewed results. Clear criteria protect patient safety and ensure that the study measures what it intends to measure.

Probability Sampling Techniques

When every single person in an accessible population has a known, non-zero chance of being chosen, the researcher is using probability sampling. This approach minimizes bias and gives the strongest confidence that the sample represents the larger population.

  • Simple Random Sampling: Every member is assigned a number, and a lottery or computer program picks participants entirely by chance.
  • Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into subgroups (such as male and female nurses), and random samples are drawn from each subgroup.
  • Systematic Sampling: Every *k*-th person on a master list (like every 10th patient on the ward register) is selected after a random start.
  • Cluster Sampling: Naturally occurring groups, such as wards across different hospitals, are randomly selected, and patients within those groups are studied.

Non-Probability Sampling Techniques

In many real-world clinical settings, obtaining a complete list of every patient in a population is impossible. Non-probability sampling relies on the researcher's judgment or accessible networks rather than random selection.

  • Convenience Sampling: Selecting participants who are easy to reach, such as interviewing students currently sitting in a lecture hall.
  • Quota Sampling: Ensuring specific proportions of groups are met (e.g., stopping when 20 male and 20 female patients are recruited) without random selection.
  • Purposive Sampling: Hand-picking participants who have expert knowledge or unique experiences relevant to the phenomenon being studied.
  • Snowball Sampling: Asking early participants to refer other people they know who fit the study criteria, often used for hard-to-reach populations.

Sample Size Determination and Power

Deciding how many participants to include is a critical step in research design. A sample that is too small may miss important health trends and commit a Type II error (failing to find a real difference). Conversely, an excessively large sample wastes resources. Statistical power is the ability of a study to detect a true difference or relationship when one actually exists; a power of 0.80 is the standard benchmark in healthcare research. Factors like the expected effect size, variability in patient responses, and the chosen statistical test all guide nurses in calculating the correct sample size before data collection begins.

Sampling Bias and Error

No sample is a 100% perfect mirror of its population. Sampling error is the natural, random variation that occurs simply because researchers study a subset instead of the whole group—it is expected and can be reduced with larger sample sizes. On the other hand, sampling bias is a systematic flaw where certain members of the population are overrepresented or underrepresented because of faulty sampling methods. For example, conducting a survey on physical fitness exclusively at a gym creates severe bias. Recognizing and minimizing bias ensures that nursing research findings remain valid and clinically useful.
