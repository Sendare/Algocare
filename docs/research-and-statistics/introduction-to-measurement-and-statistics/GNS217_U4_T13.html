Chi-Square Test

Introduction to the Chi-Square Test

The **Chi-Square Test** is a non-parametric statistical method used primarily to examine relationships between categorical variables. Unlike parametric tests such as the t-test, which deal with continuous numerical data and assume a normal distribution, Chi-Square evaluates frequency counts falling into different categories or classifications. In nursing research, researchers frequently collect categorical data, such as gender, triage categories (priority 1, 2, 3), smoking status, or the presence or absence of a hospital-acquired infection. The test evaluates whether the observed distribution of data significantly differs from what would be expected by random chance alone.

Underlying Assumptions and Requirements

For a Chi-Square test result to be valid, specific methodological assumptions must be met: - **Random Sampling**: The data must be derived from a random sample drawn from the population of interest. - **Independent Observations**: Each subject or observation can contribute to only one cell in the contingency table. Repeated measures on the same patient violate this rule. - **Adequate Cell Count**: The expected frequency in any single cell of the table should generally be at least 5 to ensure reliable mathematical approximation.

Goodness-of-Fit vs. Test of Independence

The Chi-Square family includes two distinct tests suited for different research designs: 1. **Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test**: Evaluates whether a single categorical variable matches an expected population distribution. For example, testing whether the proportion of blood types in a local clinic matches the known national blood type distribution. 2. **Chi-Square Test of Independence**: Examines whether two categorical variables are associated with each other. For example, investigating whether smoking status (smoker versus non-smoker) is associated with the development of chronic bronchitis.

Formulating Hypotheses for Chi-Square Analysis

Before running calculations, researchers must clearly establish their null and alternative hypotheses. For a **Test of Independence**, the null hypothesis states that there is no relationship or association between the two categorical variables; they are independent. The alternative hypothesis states that a significant relationship or association does exist between the variables. For a **Goodness-of-Fit test**, the null hypothesis claims that the sample data fits the specified expected distribution, whereas the alternative hypothesis asserts that it does not fit.

Calculating Expected and Observed Frequencies

Chi-Square analysis compares what is actually seen in the data against what would be expected if the null hypothesis were true. - **Observed Frequencies (O)**: The actual counts recorded during data collection, such as the number of patients who develop pressure ulcers in a specific ward. - **Expected Frequencies (E)**: The theoretical counts calculated mathematically based on row and column totals. The formula for expected frequency in a contingency table is: \[ E = \frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Column Total}}{\text{Grand Total}} \] The overall Chi-Square statistic sums the squared differences between observed and expected frequencies, divided by those expected frequencies.

Interpreting Chi-Square Test Results and P-Values

Once the Chi-Square test statistic is computed using statistical software or manual calculation, it is compared against a critical value from the Chi-Square distribution table, or evaluated using an associated **p-value**. If the resulting p-value is less than the chosen significance level (commonly set at \( \alpha = 0.05 \)), the researcher rejects the null hypothesis. This indicates that the observed discrepancies between actual and expected counts are too large to be attributed to random sampling error alone, signaling a statistically significant association.

Application and Reporting in Nursing Research

Proper reporting of Chi-Square results in research manuscripts requires specific statistical details. Authors should include the calculated test statistic value, the **degrees of freedom (df)**, and the exact p-value. For example, results are typically reported in APA style like this: \( \chi^2(2, N = 150) = 6.42, p = 0.04 \). Nurses reading literature must evaluate whether the reported association holds clinical relevance, keeping in mind that statistical significance does not automatically mean clinical importance in patient care outcomes.
