Inferential Statistics

Introduction to Inferential Statistics in Nursing Research

Nursing research often aims to improve patient care, but we rarely have the time or resources to study every single patient in the world. Instead, inferential statistics allows nurse researchers to study a smaller group of patients and make logical guesses or generalizations about a much larger population. While descriptive statistics simply summarize data—such as calculating the average blood pressure of a ward—inferential statistics takes the next step. It tests whether an intervention actually works or if the results happened purely by chance. For example, if a new wound care protocol speeds up healing in fifty trial patients, inferential statistics helps us decide if that protocol will work just as well for all patients admitted to the hospital.

Populations and Samples: The Basis of Inference

Every study begins by defining the population, which is the entire group of individuals we care about—such as all patients with type 2 diabetes in Nigeria. Because studying an entire population is practically impossible, researchers select a smaller, manageable subset called a sample. To ensure our statistical inferences are trustworthy, this sample must accurately reflect the larger population through proper random sampling. If a nurse researcher only recruits patients from a single private clinic in an affluent neighborhood, the sample is biased, and any statistical inference made will fail to represent public hospital patients.

Formulating Research and Statistical Hypotheses

Before running any calculations, researchers set up formal guesses called hypotheses. The null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no real difference or relationship between groups—for instance, saying a new pain medication works no better than a placebo. The alternative hypothesis (Ha) states that a real difference or effect does exist. Statistical tests are designed not to prove the alternative hypothesis directly, but to test if we have enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis.

Understanding P-Values and Significance Levels

Once the data is analyzed, the test spits out a p-value (probability value). This number tells us the likelihood that our results happened purely by chance if the null hypothesis is actually true. A p-value of 0.03 means there is a 3% chance the results are a fluke. Nurses compare this p-value to a pre-set significance level (alpha), usually set at 0.05. If the p-value is lower than 0.05, we call the result statistically significant and reject the null hypothesis.

Type I and Type II Errors in Clinical Decision Making

Statistics is not foolproof, and making decisions based on samples always carries a risk of error. A Type I error (False Positive) happens when a nurse researcher concludes that a new intervention works when it actually does not. A Type II error (False Negative) happens when the researcher concludes there is no effect when the intervention actually does work. In clinical practice, a Type I error might introduce an expensive, useless drug, while a Type II error might cause us to ignore a life-saving therapy.

Common Parametric Tests: t-Tests and ANOVA

When our data follows a normal, bell-shaped distribution and involves continuous measurements, we use parametric tests.

  • Independent t-test: Compares the means of two separate groups, such as testing post-op pain scores between a music therapy group and a control group.
  • Paired t-test: Compares means from the same group at two different times, such as measuring patient anxiety scores before and after a nursing briefing.
  • ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): Compares the means of three or more groups at once, such as comparing recovery times across four different hospital wards.

Non-Parametric Alternatives for Nursing Data

Not all nursing data fits neatly into a normal bell curve. When data is skewed, measured in categories, or based on small sample sizes, parametric tests become unreliable. In these cases, we use non-parametric tests which do not assume a normal distribution. For example, the Mann-Whitney U test serves as the non-parametric alternative to the independent t-test, while the Chi-Square test is used to analyze categorical data, such as comparing the number of smokers versus non-smokers who develop pressure ulcers.

Interpreting Results for Evidence-Based Nursing Practice

Reading a statistical report in a nursing journal requires looking beyond just the p-value. While a low p-value tells us an effect is statistically significant, it does not tell us if the effect is clinically meaningful for patient care. Nurses must evaluate effect size to see how large the difference actually is. If a new medication lowers blood pressure by a statistically significant 1 mmHg, but costs a fortune and causes severe side effects, it lacks clinical significance. Integrating statistical findings with clinical expertise forms the backbone of safe, evidence-based nursing practice.
