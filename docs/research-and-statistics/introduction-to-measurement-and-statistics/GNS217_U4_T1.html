Scales of Measurement

Introduction to Measurement in Nursing Research

Measurement is the foundation of evidence-based nursing practice and research. It involves assigning numbers or labels to objects, events, or characteristics according to specific rules. When we collect data—whether measuring a patient's temperature, recording their marital status, or scoring their pain level—we are using a measurement scale.

Understanding these scales helps nurses choose the correct statistical tests and interpret research findings accurately. Without clear measurement rules, data becomes meaningless and clinical decisions lose their scientific backing. Every variable studied in nursing care fits into one of four primary levels of measurement.

Nominal Scale: Categorization and Naming

The nominal scale is the simplest level of measurement. It classifies data into distinct, mutually exclusive categories that have no inherent numerical value or order. Numbers assigned to nominal categories serve only as labels.

  • Blood type: Categories include A, B, AB, and O.
  • Gender: Categories include male, female, or other.
  • Marital status: Categories include single, married, divorced, or widowed.
  • Hospital ward: Categories include surgical, medical, pediatric, or emergency.

You cannot perform mathematical operations like addition or division on nominal data. You can only count the frequency of items falling into each category.

Ordinal Scale: Ranking and Ordering

The ordinal scale goes a step beyond the nominal scale by placing data into a logical order or rank. However, the intervals or distances between the ranks are not necessarily equal or known.

  • Patient mobility status: Independent, needs assistance, or completely bedbound.
  • Cancer staging: Stage I, Stage II, Stage III, or Stage IV.
  • Likert scale survey responses: Strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree, strongly disagree.

While we know that Stage III cancer is more advanced than Stage II, we cannot say that the physical difference between Stage I and II is mathematically identical to the difference between Stage III and IV. Ordinal data tells us 'greater than' or 'less than', but not 'how much greater'.

Interval Scale: Equal Intervals with No True Zero

An interval scale possesses all the properties of an ordinal scale, plus the added feature of equal, standardized intervals between adjacent units. Crucially, an interval scale has no true zero point; the zero is arbitrary and does not mean the total absence of the property being measured.

  • Temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit: Zero degrees does not mean the complete absence of heat; it is simply a point on the scale.
  • Standardized intelligence tests: An IQ score of zero does not mean an absolute absence of intelligence.

Because zero is arbitrary, you cannot calculate true ratios. For example, 40 degrees Celsius is not twice as hot as 20 degrees Celsius, even though the number is twice as large.

Ratio Scale: Equal Intervals and Absolute Zero

The ratio scale is the highest and most precise level of measurement. It includes all the properties of an interval scale—equal intervals and ordered categories—with the addition of an absolute true zero point.

  • Patient weight in kilograms: Zero kg means an absolute absence of weight.
  • Pulse rate or heart rate: Zero beats per minute means cardiac arrest.
  • Fluid intake and output in milliliters: Zero ml means no fluid has passed.

Because a true zero exists, you can make meaningful ratio statements. A patient weighing 80 kg is literally twice as heavy as a patient weighing 40 kg. Most physical measurements in nursing care utilize the ratio scale.

Comparison of the Four Scales of Measurement

Reviewing the characteristics side by side helps clarify how these four scales build upon one another. Each higher scale incorporates the mathematical rules of the scales below it while adding new properties.

Choosing the Appropriate Scale for Nursing Data

Selecting how to measure a clinical variable determines what conclusions you can draw. Sometimes researchers can choose how to collect data. For example, a nurse studying patient age can record the exact number of years (ratio scale) or group patients into age brackets like 20-30, 31-40 (ordinal scale).

Collecting data at a higher level of measurement preserves more detailed information. You can always convert a ratio variable down into ordinal or nominal categories later, but you can never recover precise ratio values once data is collected in broad categories. Always aim to collect data at the highest possible scale that is practical in the clinical setting.
