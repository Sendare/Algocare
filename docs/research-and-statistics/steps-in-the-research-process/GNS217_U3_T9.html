Research Objectives

Introduction to Research Objectives

Research objectives are the clear, concise declarative statements that express what a study aims to achieve. They act as the operational compass for the entire investigation, transforming a broad area of clinical curiosity into a focused, manageable inquiry. When a nursing researcher in a busy Lagos teaching hospital decides to investigate post-operative pain management, the initial idea is often expansive. Objectives narrow this broad clinical interest down into specific targets. They provide the structural framework that guides methodology, data collection strategies, and analytical approaches. Without explicit objectives, studies easily lose direction, collecting vast amounts of irrelevant data while failing to answer the primary clinical question.

The Purpose and Importance of Objectives in Nursing Research

In evidence-based nursing practice, research objectives hold immense value because they directly influence patient care outcomes. They determine the exact boundaries of a study, preventing scope creep and ensuring resources are utilized efficiently.

  • Direction: Keeps the research team focused on specific clinical variables rather than wandering into unrelated topics.
  • Methodology Guidance: Dictates whether a quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods design is most appropriate.
  • Evaluation Metric: Provides the ultimate benchmark against which findings are measured during data analysis.
  • Communication: Clearly informs reviewers, ethical committees, and readers what the study intends to discover.

Relationship Between Research Questions, Hypotheses, and Objectives

Beginner researchers often confuse objectives, questions, and hypotheses, yet each serves a distinct role while maintaining a tight logical bond. Objectives are declarative statements of intent, research questions translate those intents into interrogative formats, and hypotheses offer tentative predictions of the expected relationships between variables.

ElementFormatPrimary Function
ObjectiveDeclarative statementStates what the study will do
Research QuestionInterrogative sentenceAsks what the study wants to find out
HypothesisPredictive statementProposes an anticipated answer or relationship

For instance, if an objective is to determine the relationship between night shifts and burnout among nurses, the research question asks, 'What is the relationship between night shifts and burnout?', and the hypothesis predicts that nurses on night shifts experience higher burnout levels.

Distinguishing General and Specific Objectives

A well-structured research protocol typically features two tiers of objectives: general and specific.

The general objective is a broad statement summarizing the overall goal of the study. It often incorporates the main variables and the target population. For example: "To investigate the factors influencing exclusive breastfeeding practices among mothers in rural primary health centers."

The specific objectives break down the general objective into smaller, logically connected operational parts. They represent the milestones or steps the researcher will take. Specific objectives for the breastfeeding study might include: identifying maternal knowledge levels regarding exclusive breastfeeding, determining cultural barriers to early initiation, and assessing the influence of partner support on feeding choices. Every specific objective must directly contribute to achieving the general objective.

Using the SMART Criteria for Objective Setting

To ensure research objectives are robust and feasible, many academic institutions and ethics boards require them to follow the SMART framework.

  • Specific: Clearly states what is going to be done without ambiguity. Avoid vague phrasing.
  • Measurable: Includes parameters or indicators that can be observed, quantified, or assessed.
  • Achievable: Realistic given the time, budget, and clinical setting available to the researcher.
  • Relevant: Addresses a genuine gap in nursing practice, policy, or theoretical knowledge.
  • Time-bound: Specifies a clear timeframe for completion, especially relevant for clinical audits and institutional projects.

Action Verbs Commonly Used in Writing Objectives

Precision in objective writing relies heavily on selecting strong action verbs. Vague verbs like "understand," "appreciate," "know," or "study" are discouraged because they are difficult to measure objectively. Instead, nursing researchers use precise verbs that denote observable actions.

For descriptive studies, appropriate verbs include determine, identify, describe, and explore. For analytical or experimental studies, stronger verbs such as compare, examine, evaluate, assess, and establish are preferred. Selecting the correct verb immediately clarifies the level of cognitive engagement and the analytical rigor expected from the study.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid When Writing Research Objectives

Student researchers frequently encounter avoidable traps when drafting objectives. A common error is creating too many specific objectives, which leads to bloated, unmanageable studies. Another pitfall is turning objectives into procedural tasks, such as writing "to administer questionnaires to 50 patients." Administering a questionnaire is a method, not a research objective; the objective is to *assess* the variable being measured by that questionnaire. Additionally, researchers must ensure their objectives do not drift away from the core problem statement established in the introduction.
