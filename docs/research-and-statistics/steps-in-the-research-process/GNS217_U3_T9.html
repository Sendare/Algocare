Research Objectives

Introduction to Research Objectives

Research objectives are the clear, specific statements that tell the reader what a study aims to achieve. In nursing research, these objectives act as the steering wheel for your entire project, keeping your data collection focused and relevant to patient care or clinical practice. Without them, a study easily loses direction, gathering unnecessary information that wastes valuable time and resources.

Think of a research objective as answering the basic question: "What exactly do we want to find out by doing this study?" When writing them, you transform a broad clinical curiosity into concrete, measurable goals. Whether you want to measure the healing time of diabetic foot ulcers using a new dressing or explore nurses' stress levels during night shifts, your objectives set the exact boundaries of your investigation.

The Purpose and Importance of Objectives in Nursing Research

Well-crafted objectives do much more than satisfy academic requirements. In clinical nursing research, they protect patients, ensure ethical use of resources, and guide methodology. When your goals are transparent, institutional review boards can easily judge whether your study poses undue risks to patients.

Furthermore, objectives determine your study design. If your objective is to describe blood pressure control among hypertensive pregnant women, you will use a descriptive survey. If your objective is to test if a new position reduces pressure sore formation, you need an experimental trial. The objective dictates the path.

Relationship Between Research Questions, Hypotheses, and Objectives

Students often confuse research objectives, questions, and hypotheses because they address similar ideas. However, they serve distinct functions in a research proposal:

  • Research Objectives: Declarative statements that start with an action verb, stating what the researcher will do (e.g., "To determine the prevalence of malaria among children under five").
  • Research Questions: Interrogative phrases that ask what the study wants to uncover, often mirroring the objective (e.g., "What is the prevalence of malaria among children under five?").
  • Hypotheses: Tentative predictions about the relationship between two or more variables, mostly used in quantitative, experimental studies (e.g., "Children sleeping under insecticide-treated nets have a lower incidence of malaria").

Distinguishing General and Specific Objectives

Every solid research proposal features a hierarchy of objectives, broken down into two main tiers:

  • General Objective: This gives a broad, high-level overview of the study's ultimate goal. It usually states what will be done, to whom, and in what setting. You typically have only one general objective per study.
  • Specific Objectives: These are smaller, logical stepping stones derived from the general objective. They break down the main goal into manageable, measurable tasks. If your general objective is to evaluate a hypertension education program, your specific objectives might include measuring baseline blood pressure, assessing patients' dietary knowledge, and comparing post-test scores.

Using the SMART Criteria for Objective Setting

To ensure your objectives are clear and achievable, apply the SMART framework. Poorly written objectives lead to vague results that fail to add value to nursing science.

  • Specific: Target a concrete clinical issue without ambiguity.
  • Measurable: Include indicators that can be observed and quantified, such as percentages, scores, or frequencies.
  • Achievable: Ensure the study can realistically be done within your timeframe, budget, and skill level.
  • Relevant: Address a genuine gap in nursing practice or patient care.
  • Time-bound: State a clear timeframe for completion, especially for clinical audits or quality improvement projects.

Action Verbs Commonly Used in Writing Objectives

Because objectives are action-oriented, they must always begin with a strong, active verb. Avoid passive phrasing or vague verbs like "to understand," "to appreciate," or "to study," because these are nearly impossible to measure objectively in clinical research.

Instead, use precise verbs depending on your study type:

Research TypeAppropriate Action VerbsExample
DescriptiveTo determine, to identify, to describe, to documentTo identify the primary causes of medication errors on the surgical ward
CorrelationalTo examine, to correlate, to associate, to relateTo examine the relationship between shift length and nurse burnout
ExperimentalTo evaluate, to compare, to test, to measureTo compare the wound healing rate between honey dressings and normal saline

Common Pitfalls to Avoid When Writing Research Objectives

Even experienced nursing researchers occasionally slip into common traps when drafting objectives. Recognizing these mistakes saves time during proposal defense:

  • Creating too many objectives: Having ten specific objectives usually means your study lacks focus. Keep your specific objectives to between three and five.
  • Using untestable phrasing: Writing an objective that relies on personal feelings rather than clinical data.
  • Mismatch with methodology: Stating an objective to "prove" a treatment works. Research investigates and tests; it rarely "proves" absolute truth in a single study.
  • Circular logic: Writing objectives that simply restate the title of the study using different words.
