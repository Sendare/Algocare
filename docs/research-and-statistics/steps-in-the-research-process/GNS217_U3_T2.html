Problem Identification

Introduction to Nursing Research and the Problem Identification Stage

Nursing research begins with curiosity about everyday clinical phenomena. The problem identification stage serves as the foundational step where a gap in knowledge, a recurring clinical challenge, or an inefficiency in patient care is formally recognized. Unlike routine tasks, a research problem points to a situation that requires systematic investigation to improve outcomes. In Nigerian healthcare settings, this might involve investigating high rates of patient attrition in outpatient clinics or exploring barriers to postnatal attendance in rural primary healthcare centers. Recognizing these issues moves clinical practice from guesswork to evidence-based action.

Characteristics of a Researchable Nursing Problem

Not every clinical difficulty makes a suitable research study. A truly researchable nursing problem possesses specific traits that make scientific inquiry both possible and meaningful:

  • Significance: The issue must directly impact patient outcomes, nursing education, or health service administration.
  • Ethical acceptability: The proposed investigation must not violate human rights or cause physical or psychological harm to vulnerable populations.
  • Feasibility: Adequate time, resources, cooperation, and data must be accessible to complete the study.
  • Theoretical foundation: The problem should link logically to existing nursing theories or conceptual frameworks.

Sources of Nursing Research Problems

Inspiration for clinical studies comes from multiple active zones within the healthcare ecosystem. Bedside nurses encounter daily puzzles during shift handovers and routine patient assessments. Professional literature, including journals and systematic reviews, highlights unresolved questions left by previous authors. National health priorities, such as reducing maternal mortality ratios or managing chronic non-communicable diseases in West Africa, provide macro-level direction. Furthermore, emerging healthcare technologies and interprofessional team conflicts frequently yield fertile ground for scientific inquiry.

From Clinical Observation to Research Questions

Moving from a vague clinical worry to a precise research question requires structured intellectual refinement. A nurse might observe that surgical patients on a specific ward report uncontrolled pain despite receiving prescribed analgesics. Transforming this casual observation into a formal inquiry involves breaking down the scenario into core components: the population (adult surgical patients), the variable of interest (pain management protocols), and the outcome (patient comfort levels). This structured breakdown ensures the study remains focused and measurable.

Formulating the Problem Statement and Purpose Statement

A problem statement articulates the exact nature, context, and magnitude of the issue being studied, convincing the reader why action is necessary. It typically highlights the gap in current knowledge and the negative consequences of leaving the problem unaddressed. Directly following the problem statement is the purpose statement, which clearly declares the overall objective or goal of the proposed study. While the problem statement explains the 'why,' the purpose statement outlines the 'what' the researcher intends to achieve.

Feasibility Considerations in Problem Selection

An intriguing topic remains useless if practical barriers prevent its execution. Researchers must evaluate feasibility before committing time and funds. Time constraints often derail student projects that span multiple seasons or require lengthy follow-up periods. Financial costs encompass printing questionnaires, laboratory tests, and data analysis software. Access to cooperative institutional settings and willing participants is equally vital; a brilliant study design fails if hospital management denies ethical clearance or patients refuse consent.

Refining and Narrowing Down the Research Topic

Beginners frequently commit the error of selecting topics that are overly broad, such as 'maternal health in Nigeria' or 'hospital infections.' Such expansive themes defy thorough investigation within standard academic or clinical frameworks. Narrowing a topic involves drilling down to specific populations, geographic locations, and distinct variables. For instance, 'hand hygiene compliance among intensive care nurses in a Lagos tertiary hospital' transforms an unmanageable macro-topic into a concise, focused, and highly achievable study.
