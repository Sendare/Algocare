Problem Identification

Introduction to Nursing Research and Problem Identification

Nursing research begins with a simple yet profound step: noticing that something in clinical practice is not working as well as it could. Before a nurse can collect data, review statistics, or interview patients, they must clearly identify what needs to be studied. Problem identification is the foundational phase where a broad clinical puzzle is pinned down into a specific area of concern. In everyday ward work, nurses notice gaps—such as why patients on a certain surgical ward keep falling despite standard precautions, or why families seem confused discharge instructions. Recognizing these everyday gaps transforms routine clinical duties into scientific inquiry. Without a well-defined problem, any subsequent research lacks direction, wasting time and resources that could otherwise improve patient outcomes.

Characteristics of a Researchable Nursing Problem

Not every clinical frustration makes a good research study. A truly researchable nursing problem must possess specific traits to make it worth investigating:

  • Significance: The issue must directly impact patient care, nursing practice, or health outcomes, rather than just being a matter of personal staff preference.
  • Ethical acceptability: The study must protect human subjects, ensuring no physical or psychological harm comes to vulnerable patients.
  • Feasibility: There must be adequate time, money, and access to a sufficient patient population to complete the study.
  • Empirical testability: The problem must involve variables that can be observed, measured, and analyzed using scientific methods.

Sources of Research Problems in Clinical Practice and Literature

Good research questions do not appear out of thin air; they emerge from the realities of daily healthcare environments. The bedside nurse is often the best source of ideas because they witness firsthand where protocols fail or where patient comfort drops.

Clinical observations, unusual patient reactions, and recurring shift-report complaints provide endless practical clues. Beyond the ward, professional nursing literature—such as journals, systematic reviews, and policy updates—highlights existing gaps where previous studies recommend further investigation. National health priorities, technological advancements in medical equipment, and emerging infectious diseases also supply urgent topics that demand evidence-based answers.

Translating Clinical Observations into Research Questions

A common trap for beginner nurses is jumping from a vague worry to a massive, unmanageable study. Translating a messy clinical observation into a sharp research question requires breaking down the core elements. For example, if a nurse notices that elderly patients are confused after surgery, the broad worry is postoperative confusion. To turn this into a study, the nurse must specify the population (elderly surgical patients), the intervention or exposure (such as early mobilization or reorientation protocols), and the measured outcome (incidence of delirium). Moving from a casual hallway complaint to a structured inquiry ensures the resulting study yields clear, actionable findings.

Formulating the Problem Statement and Purpose Statement

Once a topic is chosen, it must be written down clearly as a problem statement and a purpose statement. The problem statement explains the background, the nature of the difficulty, and the negative consequences of leaving it unsolved—essentially answering *why* this matter requires urgent attention. Following closely behind, the purpose statement declares the specific goal or aim of the proposed study. While the problem statement highlights the trouble or gap in knowledge, the purpose statement outlines the exact intention, such as 'to describe,' 'to compare,' or 'to evaluate' the relationship between specific variables.

Feasibility Considerations in Problem Selection

Even the most fascinating nursing problem must pass the reality check of feasibility. A student nurse might want to study rare neurological disorders across three continents, but lack the time, travel clearance, and funding to pull it off. Key practical constraints include time limits, financial costs, equipment availability, cooperation from hospital management, and the availability of willing participants. Furthermore, ethical approval boards will reject any study that places an undue burden on patients or stretches clinical staff beyond safe limits. Checking feasibility early prevents nurses from wasting months planning an impossible project.

Refining and Narrowing Down the Research Topic

Novice researchers often start with a topic that is far too broad, such as 'hospital infections' or 'patient stress.' Such topics encompass hundreds of sub-issues and cannot be studied all at once. Refining and narrowing down a topic involves drilling through layers of detail until you reach a specific, manageable question. For example, 'hospital infections' can be narrowed to 'surgical site infections in diabetic patients,' and further narrowed to 'the effect of a specific cleansing solution on surgical site infection rates in patients with type 2 diabetes.' This narrowing process ensures the study remains focused, rigorous, and capable of producing clear clinical recommendations.
