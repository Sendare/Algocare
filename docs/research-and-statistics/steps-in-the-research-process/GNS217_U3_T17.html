Research Report Writing

Introduction to Research Report Writing in Nursing

Research report writing translates scientific inquiry into actionable clinical knowledge. In Nigerian healthcare settings, evidence-based guidelines rely heavily on documented local studies to address unique challenges like tropical disease burdens, resource-limited wards, and community health practices. A well-written report bridges the gap between academic discovery and bedside nursing care. The ultimate goal is clear communication so that peers, hospital management, and policymakers can easily evaluate and implement the findings.

Standard Structure and Layout of a Research Report

Most empirical nursing studies follow the IMRAD format, which organizes content into logical compartments. Readers expect a predictable flow to locate specific details quickly without hunting through unstructured text.

  • Introduction: Establishes the background, problem statement, and study objectives.
  • Methods: Details how the study was conducted, including design, population, and tools.
  • Results: Presents the raw data findings, statistical outcomes, and descriptive tables.
  • Discussion: Interprets the results, compares them with past literature, and highlights clinical meaning.

Writing the Title, Abstract, and Introduction

The title must be concise, capturing the core variables and target population without unnecessary filler words. The abstract serves as a standalone summary, written last, condensing the entire study into roughly 150 to 250 words. Within the introduction, the author builds a funnel-shaped argument: starting from broad global or national health statistics, narrowing down to the specific gap in current nursing knowledge, and ending with clear research questions or hypotheses.

Documenting the Literature Review

A literature review is not a simple book report of past papers; it is a critical synthesis of existing evidence. Writers must group studies by thematic concepts rather than listing sources chronologically. Highlighting contradictions or gaps in previous research justifies the current study. In nursing theses, referencing recent peer-reviewed articles from both international journals and West African regional publications ensures the background is both globally informed and locally relevant.

Presenting Methodology and Research Design

Transparency is the bedrock of the methodology section. Another researcher should be able to replicate the study based solely on this text. Writers must clearly define the study design, target population, sampling technique, and data collection instruments. Ethical considerations, such as obtaining informed consent from patients and institutional review board (IRB) approval, must be explicitly stated to demonstrate respect for human subjects.

Reporting Results and Data Analysis

The results section presents objective findings without emotional commentary or broad interpretations. Quantitative data should be summarized using appropriate descriptive and inferential statistics, supported by well-labeled tables and charts. Qualitative studies require thematic organization with direct participant quotes to capture lived experiences. A common pitfall is cramming raw, unanalyzed data into the text instead of providing clear, synthesized summaries.

Drafting the Discussion and Nursing Implications

In the discussion section, authors answer the core question: 'What do these results actually mean?' The findings are compared against existing literature, explaining unexpected outcomes or contradictory data. Crucially, nurse researchers must dedicate space to clinical implications, explaining how the findings alter bedside nursing practice, patient education, or health policy. Limitations of the study, such as small sample sizes or localized sampling biases, must also be transparently acknowledged.

Formulating Conclusions and Recommendations

Conclusions provide a concise wrap-up of the study, directly answering the primary research question without introducing new data. Recommendations flow logically from the conclusions, offering concrete proposals for clinical practice, nursing education, and future research directions. Vague statements like 'more research should be done' are discouraged; instead, recommendations should specify who needs to act and what specific change should be implemented in the healthcare system.

References, Appendices, and Academic Integrity

Academic integrity requires scrupulous honesty in acknowledging all sources using approved citation styles like APA. Plagiarism—whether intentional or accidental—undermines professional credibility and violates institutional ethics. The reference list must match in-text citations perfectly. Appendices house supplementary materials that would clutter the main text, such as questionnaire templates, formal consent forms, and complex supplementary statistical tables.

Reviewing, Editing, and Disseminating the Final Report

The final stage involves rigorous proofreading to catch grammatical errors, formatting inconsistencies, and logical gaps. Peer review before formal submission helps identify blind spots that the author missed after months of writing. Dissemination transforms a shelved thesis into clinical impact through journal publication, conference presentations, or inservice training sessions for ward nurses.
