Sources of Research Problems

Introduction to Research Problems in Nursing

A research problem is a specific gap in knowledge, an unsettling clinical puzzle, or an unresolved difficulty that requires systematic investigation. In nursing, a well-chosen problem bridges the gap between everyday bedside practice and scientific evidence. Without a clear problem, a research study lacks direction and fails to contribute meaningful advancements to healthcare. Nurses encounter these gaps daily, making clinical settings the richest ground for scientific inquiry.

Clinical Practice and Patient Care Observations

The bedside is the most fertile ground for nursing research. Nurses notice patterns, recurring complications, or unexpected treatment failures during routine shifts.

  • Unexplained complications: When patients develop pressure injuries despite standard prevention protocols.
  • Protocol variations: Noticing that different shifts handle wound dressings in completely different ways.
  • Patient distress: Observing high anxiety levels among patients waiting for surgical procedures.

Existing Nursing Literature and Previous Studies

Reading published journals and systematic reviews reveals what is already known and, more importantly, what remains unknown. At the end of many research articles, authors explicitly list recommendations for future study. Researchers can replicate an old study in a new cultural setting or modify variables to test a different hypothesis. This ensures that nursing students do not waste time investigating questions that have already been definitively answered.

Professional Theories and Conceptual Frameworks

Nursing theories provide structured lenses through which patient care can be examined. When a theoretical concept does not seem to hold true in a specific clinical context, a research problem is born. Testing whether a famous nursing model applies to modern patients in resource-limited settings helps validate or update theoretical knowledge.

National and International Healthcare Priorities

Global health organizations and government ministries regularly highlight urgent health challenges, such as maternal mortality, infectious disease outbreaks, or non-communicable disease surges. Aligning nursing research with these priorities ensures funding availability and practical relevance. Studies that address national health agendas directly influence policy changes and healthcare resource allocation.

Personal Experiences and Professional Interactions

Interactions with multidisciplinary teams, hospital administrators, and patients spark informal questions that can mature into formal studies. Communication breakdowns between doctors and nurses, patient complaints about discharge instructions, or personal challenges faced during clinical rotations often reveal systemic flaws worthy of scientific inquiry.

Evaluating and Refining a Research Problem

Not every interesting question makes a feasible research study. Once a potential problem is identified, nurses must evaluate it using specific criteria:

  • Feasibility: Are there enough time, money, and participants available?
  • Ethical acceptability: Does the study protect human dignity and avoid harm?
  • Significance: Will solving this problem genuinely improve patient care or nursing knowledge?
  • Clarity: Is the research question specific and unambiguous?
