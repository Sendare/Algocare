Sources of Research Problems

Introduction to Research Problems in Nursing

A research problem serves as the foundational anchor for any scientific inquiry. In nursing, a well-defined problem addresses a gap in knowledge, resolves a clinical dilemma, or improves patient outcomes within specific healthcare environments. Without a clear starting point, investigations drift without direction, yielding findings that lack practical utility. For student nurses preparing projects in Nigerian tertiary hospitals or community health centers, identifying this core issue requires balancing theoretical curiosity with everyday realities of resource-constrained settings.

Clinical Practice and Patient Care Observations

Bedside nursing offers a rich, continuous stream of unanswered questions. Routine shifts expose patterns, recurrent complications, and unexpected patient responses that textbooks fail to address fully.

  • Unexplained treatment failures: Patients experiencing prolonged wound healing despite adhering to standard dressing protocols.
  • Adverse side effects: Frequent breakthrough pain among postoperative patients managed with standard analgesic schedules.
  • Operational friction: Delays in medication administration caused by congested ward handovers.

Noticing these recurring friction points transforms passive shift experiences into active empirical inquiries.

Existing Nursing Literature and Previous Studies

Reading published scholarly work is not merely an academic exercise; it is an active hunt for unanswered questions. Every reputable journal article concludes with a section recommending further investigation. These recommendations highlight methodological limitations, contradictory findings across different populations, or unexplored variables.

When a study conducted in a Western country shows high success with a specific tele-nursing intervention, the corresponding research problem in Nigeria might examine how infrastructural deficits like erratic power supply impact that same intervention's feasibility. Replication with contextual adaptation closes critical global knowledge loops.

Professional Theories and Conceptual Frameworks

Nursing theories provide structured lenses through which patient phenomena are interpreted. Testing these theoretical propositions in real-world clinical settings generates robust research questions. For instance, applying Dorothea Orem's Self-Care Deficit Theory to patients managing chronic hypertension in rural communities allows investigators to determine whether self-care agency directly correlates with blood pressure control. When theoretical models clash with clinical realities, research steps in to validate, modify, or refute abstract nursing concepts.

National and International Healthcare Priorities

Global health agendas and national health policies dictate where research attention is urgently required. Organizations such as the World Health Organization and national ministries of health continuously publish disease burdens, maternal mortality statistics, and health emergency targets. Aligning local research with these macro-level priorities ensures high relevance and attracts institutional backing. Investigating why maternal mortality rates remain stubbornly high in specific geopolitical zones of Nigeria directly answers a national call to action.

Personal Experiences and Professional Interactions

A nurse's career trajectory, personal encounters with illness within family networks, and interprofessional dialogues during ward rounds spark unique research queries. While subjective bias must be carefully managed to maintain scientific objectivity, personal passion sustains researchers through long, grueling data collection phases. A nurse who observed the psychological strain faced by families of stroke patients can channel that empathy into a structured qualitative inquiry on caregiver burden.

Evaluating and Refining a Research Problem

Not every interesting question makes a viable research project. Once a potential problem is identified, it must undergo rigorous scrutiny. Researchers evaluate feasibility based on time, budget, ethical constraints, and access to target populations. Furthermore, the problem must possess significance—meaning its solution will genuinely improve nursing practice, education, or administration. A poorly scoped topic is either too broad to manage or too narrow to yield meaningful generalizations.
