Sources of Literature

Introduction to Literature Sources in Nursing Research

When beginning a nursing research project or an evidence-based practice change, locating reliable information is the foundational step. Literature sources are broadly categorized into three distinct tiers based on how close the author is to the original event or study: primary, secondary, and tertiary. Understanding these tiers helps the nurse researcher build a logical, robust argument and avoid relying on weak or biased data. Nursing knowledge is constantly evolving, and anchoring clinical decisions in valid published work protects patients and improves outcomes. Every time you read a textbook, a research paper, or a clinical guideline, you are interacting with one of these source categories.

Primary Sources: Direct Evidence and Original Studies

A primary source represents the original, first-hand account of a study or event, written by the person or people who actually conducted the research. In nursing, these are typically empirical studies featuring a methodology, participant data, results, and statistical analyses.

  • Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs): Experiments where participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control groups.
  • Qualitative Studies: Exploratory research using interviews, observations, or focus groups to understand human experiences.
  • Case Studies: Detailed examinations of a single patient, unit, or unique clinical scenario.

When you read a paper that details how many milliliters of saline were used, what inclusion criteria were applied, and what the exact p-value was, you are reading a primary source.

Secondary Sources: Summaries, Reviews, and Syntheses

A secondary source is written about a primary source. Instead of collecting raw data from patients, the author of a secondary source analyzes, evaluates, or synthesizes multiple primary studies. These are invaluable for busy nurses because they condense mountains of research into readable summaries.

  • Systematic Reviews: Rigorous, methodical summaries that combine findings from numerous primary studies on a specific clinical question.
  • Literature Reviews: Broad overviews of a topic that discuss past and present research without following a strict systematic protocol.
  • Meta-Analyses: Statistical procedures that combine the numerical results of multiple independent primary studies to find an overall effect.

While secondary sources save time, remember that they rely on the accuracy of the original primary authors.

Tertiary Sources: Compilations and Indexing Tools

Tertiary sources organize, index, or compile information from primary and secondary sources. They provide broad overviews, quick facts, or directions to deeper literature rather than deep analytical discussion.

Common examples in healthcare include:

  • Textbooks and encyclopedias
  • Medical and nursing dictionaries
  • Drug handbooks and formularies
  • Bibliographies and index databases like MEDLINE or CINAHL (when used to locate citations)

Beginners often rely heavily on tertiary sources because they present established consensus facts. However, because they summarize widely accepted information, they can lag behind cutting-edge clinical research.

Peer-Reviewed Journals Versus Grey Literature

Not all written materials carry the same weight in academic circles. Peer-reviewed journals use a strict editorial process where independent experts in the same field scrutinize a manuscript for methodology, ethics, and accuracy before publication. This process ensures high academic rigor.

On the other hand, grey literature refers to research and reports produced by organizations outside of traditional commercial publishing. This includes government agency reports, conference abstracts, dissertation theses, policy documents, and white papers from health associations. While grey literature can offer unique, up-to-date local data or unpublished negative trial results (which helps reduce publication bias), it often lacks rigorous peer review. Nurses must evaluate grey literature carefully for potential organizational bias.

Digital Databases and Search Strategies for Nursing Literature

Finding relevant literature efficiently requires knowing where to look and how to ask. Nursing students and researchers rely on specialized electronic databases such as CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), MEDLINE (via PubMed), and the Cochrane Library.

To search these databases effectively, nurses use specific strategies:

  • Boolean Operators: Words like AND, OR, and NOT that narrow or broaden search results.
  • MeSH Terms: Medical Subject Headings used by MEDLINE to index articles consistently under standardized topic names.
  • Keywords and Filters: Restricting searches by publication date, language, or research design.

A well-constructed search query saves hours of frustrating scrolling and retrieves the most applicable evidence for patient care.

Evaluating the Credibility and Relevance of Sources

Gathering literature is only half the battle; nurses must critically appraise every retrieved source before applying it to clinical practice. A reliable framework for evaluation looks at authorship authority, publication date, objectivity, and methodological soundness. Always ask: Who funded this study? Is the author a recognized expert? Does the evidence directly address the clinical question at hand?

Be cautious of predatory journals that promise rapid publication for a fee without conducting genuine peer review. A shiny website and academic-sounding title do not guarantee scientific validity. Developing a sharp, skeptical eye protects the nursing profession from misinformation and ensures our interventions remain safe and effective.
