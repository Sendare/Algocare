Research Questions

Introduction and Purpose of Research Questions

A research question serves as the foundational compass for any scientific inquiry in nursing and healthcare. It narrows a broad clinical interest down into a specific, manageable query that guides study design, data collection, and analytical strategies. Without a precise question, investigations drift into unfocused data gathering, wasting resources and yielding inconclusive results. In clinical practice across Nigerian teaching hospitals and primary healthcare centers, these questions often arise directly from bedside observations, such as noticing varying rates of postpartum hemorrhage or fluctuating compliance with diabetic foot care regimens. Establishing this inquiry early ensures the entire research process remains anchored to addressing a genuine gap in current clinical knowledge.

Characteristics of a Well-Formulated Research Question

A robust research question must possess specific attributes to ensure it is scientifically sound and practically achievable. - **Feasibility**: The study must be doable within available time, financial resources, and technical expertise, keeping in mind the realities of local healthcare settings. - **Clarity**: Terms must be unambiguous so that any reader or reviewer understands exactly what is being measured. - **Significance**: The answer should contribute meaningful new knowledge to nursing practice, policy, or patient outcomes. - **Ethical Integrity**: The question must not violate human rights or cause harm to study participants. An unclear or overly broad question leads to ambiguous findings that fail to influence clinical guidelines or improve patient care.

Categorizing Types of Research Questions

Research questions take different forms depending on what the investigator wants to discover. Descriptive questions ask about the prevalence or characteristics of a phenomenon, such as measuring the exact rate of exclusive breastfeeding among mothers in a rural Nigerian community. Relational or correlational questions explore the associations between two or more variables without establishing causation, like examining whether nurses' shift lengths correlate with self-reported burnout scores. Causal or experimental questions test cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating an independent variable, such as evaluating whether a structured educational intervention reduces hospital-acquired pressure ulcers. Selecting the appropriate type depends entirely on the maturity of existing evidence on the topic.

Structuring Questions with FINER and PICOT

Frameworks provide systematic structures to build and evaluate clinical inquiries. The **FINER** criteria—Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant—help evaluate whether a broad research idea is worth pursuing. Meanwhile, the **PICOT** format is primarily used for quantitative and evidence-based practice questions, breaking down the query into specific components: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Time. For example, in a surgical ward, a PICOT question might look at adult patients (P), comparing early ambulation (I) against bed rest (C) on the incidence of deep vein thrombosis (O) within 7 days of surgery (T). Utilizing these frameworks prevents oversight of vital clinical variables.

Translating Clinical Practice Issues into Research Questions

The richest source of nursing research questions is daily clinical practice. Nurses encounter recurring challenges at the bedside, such as managing pediatric IV infiltrations, dealing with patient non-adherence to antihypertensive therapy, or addressing communication barriers in overcrowded emergency departments. Transforming these clinical frustrations into formal research requires moving from a vague grievance to a structured inquiry. For instance, observing frequent needle-stick injuries among junior nursing staff shifts into a focused question about whether the introduction of safety-engineered devices significantly reduces occupational exposure incidents. Clinical curiosity, paired with a structured questioning technique, bridges the gap between everyday nursing duties and scientific advancement.

Alignment of Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

A well-designed study maintains strict structural harmony among its research question, study objectives, and hypotheses. The research question is the core query driving the project. Objectives translate this question into operational action verbs, such as 'to determine,' 'to evaluate,' or 'to compare.' Hypotheses, typically used in quantitative research, offer a tentative prediction of the relationship between the study variables based on theoretical frameworks or previous literature. If a research question asks whether hand hygiene compliance rates differ between morning and night shifts, the objective will state 'to compare compliance rates,' and the hypothesis will predict either a difference or no difference. When these three elements align perfectly, the study maintains logical consistency from introduction to conclusion.

Common Pitfalls in Formulating Research Questions

Inexperienced researchers often stumble into common traps when drafting their inquiries. A frequent error is creating a question that is too broad, such as asking 'How can we improve nursing care in Nigeria?', which cannot possibly be answered in a single study. Another pitfall is crafting a question that can be answered with a simple 'yes' or 'no', rather than prompting deep investigation. Researchers also sometimes design questions that are economically or logistically impossible to answer given local constraints, or questions that harbor strong personal bias. Avoiding these missteps requires careful consultation of existing literature and rigorous peer review of the proposed question before data collection begins.
