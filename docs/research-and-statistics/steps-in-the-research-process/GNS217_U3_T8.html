Research Questions

Introduction to Research Questions

A research question is the central query that a study seeks to answer. It transforms a broad area of interest into a specific, testable inquiry. Without a clear research question, a nursing study lacks focus, and data collection becomes aimless. It serves as the compass for the entire research project, guiding the literature review, methodology, and data analysis. In clinical nursing, a good question often arises from a gap in practice or an unresolved patient outcome, bridging the space between bedside care and scientific investigation.

Characteristics of a Well-Formulated Research Question

A strong research question possesses specific attributes that make the proposed study feasible and valuable.

  • Clarity: The wording must be simple and unambiguous, leaving no doubt about what is being asked.
  • Specificity: It must target a distinct population, intervention, or outcome rather than a vague concept.
  • Feasibility: The study must be practical in terms of time, money, and available resources.
  • Relevance: The answer must contribute meaningfully to nursing practice, education, or policy.

Types of Research Questions: Descriptive, Relational, and Causal

Research questions take different forms depending on what the nurse researcher wants to discover.

  • Descriptive: These measure the prevalence or characteristics of a single variable, such as 'What is the average blood pressure of elderly patients admitted to the ward?'
  • Relational: These explore the association between two or more variables without proving cause, such as 'Is there a relationship between night shifts and medication errors?'
  • Causal: These test cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating an independent variable, such as 'Does a structured handoff protocol reduce communication mistakes during shift change?'

The FINER and PICOT Frameworks

To ensure research questions are robust, nurses use standardized evaluation frameworks.

The FINER criteria evaluate the overall quality of a question: Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant. If a question fails any of these, it requires revision.

For clinical and evidence-based practice questions, the PICOT format provides a structured blueprint:

  • P: Population or patient group
  • I: Intervention or indicator being tested
  • C: Comparison intervention or control group
  • O: Outcome expected
  • T: Timeframe for the result

Developing Questions from Clinical Practice

The best nursing research questions often bubble up from daily clinical challenges. When a nurse notices a recurring issue—such as frequent pressure ulcers on a specific ward or high patient anxiety before surgery—that observation forms the seed of a research question. Translating a messy bedside problem into a structured question requires stripping away emotional reactions and focusing on measurable variables. Nurses are uniquely positioned to spot these gaps because they spend the most direct time with patients.

Relationship Between Research Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

Research questions do not stand alone; they connect directly to study objectives and hypotheses.

  • Research Question: The interrogative sentence asking what needs to be discovered.
  • Objectives: Action statements that break the question down into specific goals (e.g., 'To determine the effect of...').
  • Hypothesis: A declarative prediction of the expected relationship between variables, usually tested in quantitative studies.

For example, if the question asks whether music reduces labor pain, the objective is to measure pain scores, and the hypothesis predicts that patients who listen to music will report lower pain scores than those who do not.

Common Pitfalls in Formulating Research Questions

Beginner researchers often stumble when writing research questions. A frequent error is crafting a question that is far too broad, making it impossible to answer within a reasonable timeframe. Another mistake is creating a question that can be answered with a simple 'yes' or 'no' when deeper exploration is required. Researchers should also avoid loaded or biased wording that assumes an outcome before the study even begins. Careful refinement and peer review help catch these errors early.
