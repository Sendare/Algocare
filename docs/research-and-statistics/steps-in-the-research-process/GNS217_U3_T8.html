Research Questions

Introduction to Research Questions

A research question is the central question that a study aims to answer. For nursing students in Nigeria and across the globe, formulating a clear and concise research question is the foundational step of the entire research process. It acts as a compass, guiding the researcher's methodology, data collection, and data analysis phases. Without a well-defined research question, a nursing study risks lacking focus, leading to wasted resources and inconclusive findings. Research questions stem from identified gaps in clinical knowledge, professional experiences, or existing literature. In evidence-based practice, formulating these questions bridges the gap between theoretical nursing concepts and direct patient care outcomes. As student nurses transition into professional practice, understanding how to construct and evaluate research questions enables them to critically appraise published literature, identify best practices, and contribute to the growing body of nursing knowledge relevant to the African healthcare context and beyond. Crafting this query correctly ensures that the research remains feasible, ethically sound, and practically relevant to patient populations.

Characteristics of a Well-Formulated Research Question

A well-formulated research question must possess specific characteristics to ensure the study is successful and impactful. First, it must be clear and unambiguous, using precise language so that readers and evaluators understand exactly what is being studied. Second, the question needs to be specific, narrowing down broad nursing topics into manageable inquiries. For example, instead of asking about general patient recovery, a good question focuses on a specific variable, such as post-operative pain management protocols. Third, a quality research question must be answerable and feasible, meaning the researcher has adequate time, resources, access to the target population, and appropriate ethical clearance to collect the necessary data. Fourth, it must be relevant to nursing practice, healthcare policy, or patient outcomes. In resource-constrained settings, relevance is especially critical to ensure that time and effort are directed toward solving pressing local health challenges. Finally, the question should be ethical, avoiding any harm to human subjects and respecting institutional review board guidelines. Meeting these criteria ensures that the research study yields valid, actionable insights.

Types of Research Questions

Nursing research utilizes several types of research questions depending on the study design and the nature of the phenomenon being investigated. Descriptive research questions aim to document and describe the prevalence, characteristics, or frequency of a nursing phenomenon, such as asking about the prevalence of pressure ulcers among bedridden patients in a specific ward. Relational or correlational questions explore the associations or relationships between two or more variables without establishing causation, such as investigating the relationship between nurse-to-patient ratios and medication administration errors. Causal or experimental questions examine cause-and-effect relationships, determining whether an independent variable directly alters a dependent variable, such as testing whether a new wound care protocol reduces healing time compared to standard care. Qualitative research questions are broader, open-ended, and exploratory, seeking to understand the lived experiences, perceptions, or meanings of patients or nurses in a given situation. Understanding these distinct categories helps nursing students select the appropriate research design, align their data collection methods properly, and correctly interpret their findings in clinical settings.

The FINER and PICOT Frameworks

Structured frameworks such as FINER and PICOT are invaluable tools for nursing students and researchers when developing and refining research questions. The FINER criteria help evaluate the overall quality and feasibility of a research question. It stands for Feasible (adequate patients, technical expertise, and budget), Interesting (captures the researcher's and community's interest), Novel (provides new findings, confirms, or extends previous work), Ethical (institutional review board approval and patient safety), and Relevant (advances scientific knowledge, clinical practice, or health policy). On the other hand, the PICOT framework is primarily used for quantitative, evidence-based practice questions. PICOT stands for Population or patient group of interest, Intervention or indicator, Comparison group or intervention, Outcome of interest, and Timeframe. For instance, a nursing student might use PICOT to formulate a question comparing hand hygiene education methods versus standard reminders to reduce hospital-acquired infection rates over six months. Utilizing these frameworks ensures that research inquiries are methodical, highly focused, and clinically impactful.

Developing Research Questions from Clinical Practice

Developing research questions directly from clinical practice ensures that nursing research remains grounded in real-world healthcare challenges. Clinical rotations, bedside nursing duties, community health outreaches, and interactions with patients and multidisciplinary healthcare teams offer a wealth of observations. When a nurse notices recurrent issues, such as high rates of patient falls during night shifts or poor compliance with discharge instructions among diabetic patients, these clinical puzzles serve as the starting point for inquiry. To transition from a casual observation to a formal research question, the nurse must conduct a preliminary literature review to see what is already known and where the knowledge gap lies. Transforming clinical problems into structured research questions requires moving from broad statements like 'patients are falling too much' to targeted inquiries such as 'What is the effectiveness of a multifactorial fall prevention bundle in reducing inpatient falls among elderly patients in medical wards?' This translation process empowers clinical nurses to drive quality improvement initiatives and foster evidence-based changes in their health institutions.

Relationship Between Research Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

In a well-designed nursing research study, there is a clear, logical alignment and progression from the research question to the research objectives and hypotheses. The research question sets the overarching direction and inquiry of the study. Derived directly from this question are the research objectives, which are specific, actionable statements outlining what the researcher aims to achieve or measure. For example, if the research question asks about the relationship between shift duration and nursing fatigue, the objective might be 'to measure the level of fatigue among nurses working 8-hour versus 12-hour shifts.' Following objectives, quantitative studies often formulate hypotheses, which are tentative, testable predictions about the relationship between study variables. Hypotheses can be directional (specifying the expected direction of the relationship) or non-directional. Maintaining harmony among these three components is vital; a mismatch—such as a research question exploring one variable while the hypothesis predicts something completely unrelated—compromises the validity and internal consistency of the entire research project.

Common Pitfalls in Formulating Research Questions

Formulating research questions is a meticulous task, and nursing students frequently encounter common pitfalls that hinder their study's success. One major pitfall is formulating questions that are too broad or vague, such as asking 'How does nursing care affect patients?' This lacks specific variables and cannot be effectively investigated. Another common mistake is creating questions that are too narrow or trivial, yielding findings of little clinical significance. Researchers also sometimes frame questions that cannot be answered empirically, such as questions revolving purely around subjective moral judgments rather than measurable data. Additionally, students may propose questions that are not feasible due to financial constraints, lack of access to clinical populations, or insufficient timeframes within their academic program. Ethical violations, such as questions requiring interventions that could cause patient harm, represent a severe pitfall. Being aware of these errors allows nursing students to critically review and refine their research queries before data collection begins, ensuring academic rigor and ethical integrity.
