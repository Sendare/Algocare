Criteria for Selection of Research Problems

Introduction to Nursing Research Problems

A nursing research problem is a specific gap in knowledge, a troublesome clinical situation, or an unanswered question that requires systematic investigation to improve patient care and nursing practice. Before any study begins, nurses must choose a problem worth investigating because resources are limited and clinical time is precious. A good research problem stems from daily bedside observations, professional literature, or theoretical frameworks. It should directly impact the nursing profession, whether by validating a clinical procedure, improving patient comfort, or enhancing health education. Selecting a topic is rarely an isolated academic exercise; it bridges the gap between everyday hospital routines and evidence-based practice.

Significance and Relevance to Clinical Practice

A chosen research problem must hold genuine practical value for the nursing profession. If the answer to the research question changes nothing in how nurses deliver care, manage symptoms, or educate patients, the study lacks clinical significance. High-priority topics typically address prevalent health issues, high-risk procedures, or persistent patient complications within local and global healthcare settings. When a student pitches a research title, preceptors and supervisors always ask: 'So what?' The problem must offer clear benefits that justify the effort expended by both researchers and participants.

Feasibility and Resource Availability

Even the most fascinating nursing idea is useless if it cannot be carried out in reality. Feasibility looks at the practical mechanics of conducting a study. Researchers must evaluate four critical constraints:

  • Time: Does the academic calendar or project window allow enough time to recruit patients and analyze data?
  • Finances: Are there funds for printing questionnaires, laboratory tests, or transport?
  • Cooperation: Will the hospital management and clinical staff permit access to the ward?
  • Equipment: Are the necessary tools available to measure the variables accurately?

Ethical Considerations and Patient Safety

Protecting human subjects is non-negotiable in nursing research. A research problem must never violate basic human rights or place vulnerable patients at undue risk of physical, psychological, or social harm. Researchers must ensure voluntary participation, guarantee confidentiality, and obtain informed consent. Common ethical pitfalls involve studying vulnerable groups like unconscious patients, children, or prisoners without proper legal safeguards. If a research design requires withholding standard care or exposing patients to dangerous experimental procedures without clear therapeutic benefit, the ethical review board will rightfully reject the problem.

Researcher Expertise, Interest, and Passion

Research is a long, demanding journey that tests a student's patience and endurance. Selecting a problem that aligns with the researcher's genuine clinical interest provides the internal drive needed to finish the project. However, interest alone is insufficient; the researcher must also possess the necessary skills, clinical background, and methodological competence. A nurse midwife investigating neonatal resuscitation must understand labor ward dynamics, just as a public health nurse studying community vaccine hesitancy must understand local cultural norms.

Novelty and Contribution to Nursing Knowledge

Nursing research should strive to add something fresh to the existing body of knowledge rather than endlessly repeating what is already known. Novelty means the study explores a new angle, introduces a fresh intervention, or examines a familiar problem in a completely different patient population or setting. While replication studies are occasionally useful to confirm past findings, endless duplication of old research wastes valuable time. A good problem offers new insights that push the nursing profession forward.

Measurability and Availability of Data

A research problem must be operationalized, meaning the concepts can be clearly measured and evaluated using valid tools. Vague philosophical ideas like 'good nursing karma' or 'patient spiritual peace' are difficult to quantify unless broken down into concrete, measurable indicators like blood pressure readings, pain scales, or sleep duration. Furthermore, the data required to answer the research question must actually exist and be accessible to the investigator. If patient records are missing, destroyed, or locked behind impenetrable bureaucratic walls, the problem is not viable for study.
