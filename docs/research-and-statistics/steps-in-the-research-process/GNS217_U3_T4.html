Criteria for Selection of Research Problems

Introduction to Nursing Research Problems

A research problem is an enigmatic situation, a gap in knowledge, or a clinical difficulty that warrants systematic investigation to find a solution or generate new insights. In nursing, identifying a proper research problem forms the bedrock of evidence-based practice. Without a clearly defined problem, clinical inquiries lose direction, and resulting studies fail to influence patient outcomes or healthcare policy meaningfully.

Significance and Relevance to Clinical Practice

A strong research problem must address a meaningful issue. Studies should target prevalent clinical challenges, improve patient safety, or refine nursing interventions. In resource-constrained settings like many Nigerian healthcare facilities, investigating trivial or redundant topics wastes scarce time and funding. Every chosen problem should promise actionable insights that can change bedside protocols, optimize health policies, or enhance patient recovery.

Feasibility and Resource Availability

An ambitious research idea is useless if it cannot be executed. Feasibility evaluates whether a study can realistically be carried out given existing constraints. - **Time**: Academic calendars, clinical shifts, and funding timelines restrict how long a study can run. - **Finances**: Expenses cover stationery, laboratory tests, software licenses, and research assistants. - **Access to Population**: Securing a sufficient sample size depends on patient turnout and institutional gatekeeper approval.

Ethical Considerations and Patient Safety

No matter how intriguing a research question is, it must never compromise human dignity, rights, or safety. Vulnerable populations—such as unconscious patients, children, or prisoners—require heightened protection. Researchers must obtain institutional ethical approval and informed consent. If a problem forces participants into physical danger, psychological distress, or privacy violations without overriding benefits, it must be rejected outright.

Researcher Expertise, Interest, and Commitment

Research is a demanding journey that tests perseverance. Choosing a topic aligned with the researcher's clinical background and passion sustains motivation during data analysis and report writing. A cardiac nurse investigating wound care practices might struggle with clinical nuance compared to studying heart failure self-management. Competence in methodology, statistics, and subject matter ensures the study is conducted rigorously and accurately.

Novelty and Contribution to Nursing Knowledge

Good research avoids duplicating what is already well established unless replication is specifically warranted to test previous findings in a new context. A research problem should offer novelty—introducing fresh perspectives, testing new interventions, or examining old problems in unexplored settings. Contributing to the body of nursing knowledge prevents stagnation and drives the profession forward.

Measurability and Availability of Data

A research problem must be empirical and measurable. Abstract concepts require clear operational definitions so they can be observed and quantified through valid instruments like questionnaires, physiological monitors, or observation checklists. If variables cannot be reliably measured, the research question remains unanswerable through scientific inquiry.
