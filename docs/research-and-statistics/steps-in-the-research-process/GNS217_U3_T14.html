Sample Design

Introduction to Sample Design in Nursing Research

Nursing research aims to improve patient care, test new interventions, and understand health behaviors. Because it is rarely possible to study every single person affected by a health condition, researchers rely on a sample design. A sample design is the specific plan or strategy used to select a portion of the population to participate in a study.

Good sample design ensures that the findings from the smaller group can be trusted and applied to the wider patient population. If a sample is chosen poorly, the results will not reflect reality, leading to wasted resources and potentially harmful clinical guidelines. Understanding how to select participants correctly is a foundational skill for evidence-based nursing practice.

Key Terminology: Population, Sample, and Sampling Frame

Before selecting participants, researchers must clearly define three basic terms:

  • Population: The entire group of people or items that the researcher is interested in studying, such as all diabetic patients in Lagos State.
  • Sample: The specific subset of individuals chosen from the population to actually participate in the study.
  • Sampling Frame: The actual physical list or database of all members of the population from which the sample is drawn, such as a clinic's active patient registry.

Probability Sampling Methods

Probability sampling means every single member of the target population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected. This approach reduces bias and allows researchers to make strong statistical generalizations to the wider population.

  • Simple Random Sampling: Every person is assigned a number, and a lottery or computer program picks participants completely by chance.
  • Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into subgroups, like male and female nurses, and random samples are drawn from each group.
  • Systematic Sampling: Every *k-th* person on a list is chosen, such as every tenth patient entering the clinic.
  • Cluster Sampling: Naturally occurring groups, like entire nursing schools or wards, are randomly selected, and all members within them are studied.

Non-Probability Sampling Methods in Clinical Studies

When a complete list of the population is unavailable or difficult to access, researchers use non-probability sampling. In these methods, the chance of any individual being selected is unknown. While easier and cheaper, they carry a higher risk of bias.

  • Convenience Sampling: Selecting people who are easiest to reach, such as patients currently sitting in the waiting room.
  • Purposive Sampling: Hand-picking participants because they possess specific traits relevant to the study, such as expert wound care nurses.
  • Quota Sampling: Ensuring specific subgroups are represented in exact proportions, similar to stratified sampling, but without random selection.
  • Snowball Sampling: Asking current participants to refer other suitable candidates, often used for hard-to-reach populations like intravenous drug users.

Determining Sample Size and Power

A common question in nursing research is, "How many patients do I need?" Sample size depends on the study design, how much variability exists in the data, and the desired statistical power.

Statistical power is the ability of a study to detect a true difference or effect if one exists. A sample that is too small may miss important clinical findings, leading to a false negative result. Conversely, an excessively large sample wastes time and financial resources. Researchers often use specialized software or statistical formulas to calculate the ideal number of participants before data collection begins.

Addressing Sampling Bias and Error

Sampling error happens naturally because a sample is only a subset of the population; estimates from the sample will never perfectly match population values. However, sampling bias is a systematic error introduced when the sampling process favors certain outcomes over others. For example, surveying only morning patients at a clinic misses night-shift workers and introduces bias.

Researchers minimize error and bias by using random selection, increasing sample sizes, and clearly defining inclusion and exclusion criteria. Being honest about these limitations in the research report is vital for transparent scientific communication.

Ethical Considerations in Sample Recruitment

Recruiting participants for nursing research requires strict adherence to ethical principles. Patients are often in vulnerable positions, and they must never feel coerced or pressured into joining a study by their healthcare providers.

Participation must be completely voluntary, backed by informed consent where patients understand the risks, benefits, and right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Additionally, researchers must protect patient privacy and keep all collected health data strictly confidential.
