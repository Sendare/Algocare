Sample Design

Introduction to Sample Design in Nursing Research

Nursing research relies heavily on gathering accurate data to improve patient care outcomes. Because studying an entire population of patients or nurses is rarely feasible due to time, financial, and logistical constraints, researchers select a smaller subset to study. Sample design refers to the exact plan or method used to select this subset from a larger target group. A well-designed sample ensures that findings can be generalized accurately without wasting valuable healthcare resources. In Nigerian clinical settings, where patient populations across tertiary hospitals are vast and diverse, a poor sampling plan can lead to skewed conclusions that misinform hospital policy or nursing practice.

Key Terminology: Population, Sample, and Sampling Frame

Clarity in research terminology prevents costly methodological errors during study execution.

  • Target Population: The entire aggregation of cases or individuals that meet the researcher's criteria of interest, such as all post-operative adult patients in Nigeria.
  • Accessible Population: The specific segment of the target population that is realistically available to the researcher, such as post-operative patients at Lagos University Teaching Hospital.
  • Sampling Frame: The actual physical list or database of all elements within the accessible population from which the sample is drawn, like the ward admission register.
  • Sample: The specific group of individuals selected from the sampling frame who actually participate in the study.

Probability Sampling Methods

Probability sampling relies on random selection, ensuring every member of the sampling frame has a known, non-zero chance of being included. This approach minimizes selection bias and allows researchers to calculate sampling error. Common techniques include:

  1. Simple Random Sampling: Every individual is selected purely by chance, often using a table of random numbers or computer software.
  2. Systematic Sampling: Every *k*th individual on a numbered list is chosen after a random starting point.
  3. Stratified Random Sampling: The population is divided into homogenous subgroups (strata), such as nursing rank, and random samples are drawn from each stratum.
  4. Cluster Sampling: Naturally occurring groups, like primary health care clinics across a state, are randomly selected, and all or some members within those groups are studied.

Non-Probability Sampling Methods in Clinical Studies

When a complete sampling frame is unavailable or clinical urgency makes random selection impossible, non-probability sampling is utilized. While easier and less expensive to execute, these methods carry a higher risk of bias and limit generalizability.

  • Convenience Sampling: Selecting readily available patients or staff who meet the inclusion criteria, such as interviewing patients present in an outpatient clinic today.
  • Quota Sampling: Setting specific quotas for demographic subgroups to ensure basic representation without random selection.
  • Purposive (Judgmental) Sampling: Hand-picking participants based on the researcher's expert knowledge of who can best provide valuable insights, such as expert wound-care nurses.
  • Snowball Sampling: Existing study participants recruit future subjects from among their acquaintances, often used for hard-to-reach or stigmatized populations.

Determining Sample Size and Power

A common question in nursing research proposals is, "How many participants do I need?" Sample size calculation is not a guessing game; it is a mathematical process driven by statistical power, effect size, and significance level (alpha). A sample that is too small risks failing to detect a real clinical difference, resulting in a Type II error. Conversely, an excessively large sample wastes resources. Researchers often use specialized software or statistical formulas to determine the minimum number of subjects required to achieve adequate study power, typically set at 80% or higher.

Addressing Sampling Bias and Error

Errors are inevitable in quantitative research, but understanding their source helps maintain study integrity. Sampling error is the natural variation that occurs because data is collected from a subset rather than the entire population; this decreases as sample size increases. Sampling bias, however, is a systematic flaw introduced by the selection process, where certain members of the population are systematically more likely to be included than others. For instance, surveying only day-shift nurses about burnout introduces bias by excluding night-shift staff whose workload experiences different physical and psychological stressors.

Ethical Considerations in Sample Recruitment

Selecting a sample involves interacting with human beings who deserve full protection of their rights. Researchers must obtain ethical approval from institutional review boards before approaching potential participants. Key ethical pillars during recruitment include voluntary participation, where patients understand they can decline or withdraw at any point without compromising their healthcare delivery. In vulnerable clinical groups—such as critically ill patients or pregnant teenagers in Nigerian health facilities—coercion must be actively avoided. Furthermore, protecting participant anonymity and confidentiality during data collection is mandatory.
