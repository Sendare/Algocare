Literature Review

Introduction to Literature Review in Nursing Research

A literature review is a careful, structured summary of what researchers already know about a specific clinical or health topic. In nursing research, it serves as the solid foundation for any new study, evidence-based guideline, or quality improvement project. Instead of jumping blindly into data collection, a nurse researcher must first look backward to see what has already been discovered, tested, and published.

Think of the literature review as a map of previous scholarly work. It shows you which paths have already been traveled and where the road ends. Without this step, nurses risk repeating studies that have already proven ineffective or, worse, harmful. By reading widely in nursing journals, textbooks, and clinical databases, you ground your current clinical questions in reality and proven science.

Purposes and Objectives of Literature Reviews

Literature reviews do much more than just fill space at the beginning of a research paper. They actively shape clinical practice and educational curricula.

  • Establishing theoretical frameworks: They help connect your clinical question to established nursing theories, like Florence Nightingale's environmental theory or Dorothea Orem's self-care deficit theory.
  • Uncovering gaps in knowledge: They spotlight what is missing, telling researchers where clinical data is weak or outdated.
  • Refining research questions: They help narrow a broad, vague idea into a sharp, testable hypothesis.
  • Supporting evidence-based practice: They provide the raw evidence needed to update hospital policies and bedside nursing care plans.

Primary vs. Secondary Sources

When searching for literature, nurses encounter two main categories of sources: primary and secondary. Knowing the difference protects the integrity of your research.

  • Primary Sources: These are original research reports written by the exact people who conducted the study. Examples include randomized controlled trials, qualitative interviews conducted by the author, and clinical case studies published for the first time.
  • Secondary Sources: These are summaries, reviews, or interpretations of primary sources written by someone else. Examples include systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and textbook chapters summarizing various research studies.

A common mistake among nursing students is relying too heavily on textbooks and review articles. While secondary sources are great for general background reading, rigorous research demands that you trace claims back to their original primary sources.

Developing Search Strategies and Keywords

Finding relevant literature requires a deliberate strategy rather than random typing into search engines. You begin by breaking your research question down into core concepts, often using frameworks like PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome).

Once your concepts are clear, you select exact keywords and synonyms. For instance, if your population is elderly patients with pressure ulcers, your search strings might include terms like "aged", "geriatric", "older adults", combined with "pressure injury", "bedsores", and "decubitus ulcer".

Boolean operators like AND, OR, and NOT help control your database results. Using AND narrows your search (e.g., handwashing AND infection rates), while OR broadens it by including synonyms (e.g., nurses OR nursing staff). Master these tools to prevent drowning in thousands of irrelevant articles or missing vital papers altogether.

Evaluating and Critiquing Research Studies

Finding an article is only the first step; you must then evaluate its quality. Not all published research is reliable. Critiquing involves examining whether a study was designed well, executed carefully, and interpreted honestly.

Key areas to assess include:

  • Methodology: Was the sample size large enough? Was the tool used to measure blood pressure or pain reliable and valid?
  • Bias: Did the researchers let personal wishes influence who got the experimental treatment?
  • Relevance: Even if a study is well-conducted, does it actually apply to your specific local patient population in the ward?

Using structured appraisal checklists helps guide this critical reading process step-by-step.

Synthesizing Findings and Identifying Research Gaps

Synthesis is where true scholarship happens. Instead of writing a disconnected list of article summaries—where paragraph one discusses Author A, paragraph two discusses Author B, and paragraph three discusses Author C—you must weave the literature together thematically.

Grouping studies by themes, agreements, contradictions, or clinical trends allows you to tell a coherent scientific story. For example, you might group five studies showing that music therapy lowers anxiety, while contrasting them with two studies showing no effect due to small sample sizes. Through this grouping, research gaps naturally emerge, setting the stage for your own conclusions or recommendations.

Structuring and Writing the Literature Review

A well-written literature review follows a clear logical arc, moving from broad contexts down to specific details.

  1. The Introduction: Define the clinical problem, explain its importance to nursing, and state the scope of your review.
  2. The Body: Organize the literature by identified themes, historical progression, or methodological approaches. Compare and contrast what different authors found.
  3. The Conclusion: Summarize the overall state of the knowledge, highlight the glaring gaps, and state clearly how your review or proposed study addresses those gaps.

Keep your academic voice objective, professional, and clear. Avoid excessive long quotes; instead, paraphrase authors' ideas accurately in your own words.

Ethical Considerations and Proper Citation Practices

Ethics in literature reviews center around honesty, respect for intellectual property, and academic integrity. Plagiarism—using someone else's words, ideas, or data without giving proper credit—is a severe offense in nursing scholarship and practice.

Every time you borrow an idea, statistic, or direct finding, you must cite the original author using the required referencing style, such as APA. Proper citation is not just a bureaucratic formatting chore; it gives credit where it is due and allows fellow nurses and researchers to verify your sources. Fabricating references or misrepresenting an author's findings to fit your hypothesis violates the core ethical code of the nursing profession.
