Writing a Research Report

Introduction to the Research Report

A research report is a formal written document that outlines the process, data, and findings of a systematic investigation. For nursing students and healthcare professionals, writing reports bridges the gap between clinical observation and evidence-based practice. The primary goal is to communicate new knowledge clearly so that others can evaluate the evidence and apply it to patient care. Unlike a casual essay, a research report relies heavily on empirical data, structured formatting, and strict academic honesty. Every section serves a specific purpose, guiding the reader logically from the initial research question to the final clinical implication. Mastering this document structure ensures your findings are taken seriously in clinical audits, hospital committees, and academic journals.

Structure and Organization of a Standard Report

A standard research report follows a universally recognized layout often referred to by the acronym IMRAD, along with a few necessary front and back elements. Keeping a consistent structure helps readers locate specific information quickly without searching through dense paragraphs.

  • Front Matter: Includes the title page, abstract, table of contents, and acknowledgments.
  • Introduction: States the research problem, background, and specific study objectives.
  • Methodology: Details how the study was conducted, including participant selection and data collection tools.
  • Results: Presents the raw data and statistical findings objectively without personal commentary.
  • Discussion: Interprets what the results mean in the context of existing nursing literature.
  • Back Matter: Contains reference lists, appendices, and supplementary charts.

Drafting the Preliminary Pages and Introduction

The preliminary pages set the tone for your entire document. The title must be concise yet descriptive, immediately telling the reader what the study investigates. Following the title, the abstract serves as a standalone summary, condensing the background, methods, results, and conclusion into roughly 250 words. Many busy clinicians read only the abstract to decide if the full paper fits their clinical needs.

The introduction then opens the main text by establishing the background of the health issue. You must clearly state the gap in current nursing knowledge and end with your specific research questions or hypotheses. A common pitfall is writing a history of medicine rather than focusing narrowly on the specific problem your study solves.

Presenting Literature Review and Methodology

The literature review demonstrates that your study builds upon existing scientific knowledge rather than duplicating it. Group previous studies by themes or arguments rather than listing them one by one like a book report. Show where past research fell short, directly justifying why your current study is necessary.

The methodology section follows, acting as a step-by-step recipe of your investigation. It must contain enough detail for another nurse researcher to replicate your study entirely.

  • Study Design: Whether qualitative, quantitative, or mixed.
  • Population and Setting: Who was studied and where.
  • Data Collection: Tools, questionnaires, or equipment used.
  • Data Analysis: Statistical tests applied to the raw data.

Reporting Results and Data Analysis

When writing the results section, stick strictly to the facts. Present your findings using text, tables, and graphs to make complex data digestible. Do not interpret what the numbers mean or explain why a particular hypothesis failed here; save that commentary for the discussion section.

Quantitative studies require clear presentation of statistical values, sample sizes, and response rates. Qualitative studies should feature direct, anonymized participant quotes that capture the essence of the human experience being studied. Keep your tone objective, neutral, and precise.

Writing Discussion, Conclusions, and Recommendations

The discussion section is where you bring meaning to your numbers. Start by summarizing your main findings without repeating the exact statistics from the results. Compare your outcomes with past literature you reviewed earlier, explaining why your results agree or conflict with previous studies. Acknowledge the limitations of your study openly—every research project has flaws, whether in sample size or data collection.

Following the discussion, state your definitive conclusions based directly on your findings. Finish with actionable recommendations for clinical nursing practice, hospital administration, or future research directions.

Referencing Styles and Academic Integrity

Academic integrity requires that you give proper credit whenever you use another person's ideas, words, or data. Failing to do so constitutes plagiarism, a serious offense that can ruin professional credibility and academic standing. Nursing and scientific journals typically use specific referencing styles such as APA, Harvard, or Vancouver.

Every in-text citation must have a corresponding full entry in the reference list at the end of the document. Double-check that author names, publication years, and journal titles match perfectly between your text and your bibliography. Consistent referencing allows readers to verify your sources and explore the background literature further.

Editing, Proofreading, and Disseminating Findings

Before submitting or publishing your research report, rigorous editing and proofreading are essential. Look beyond simple spelling and grammar check errors; evaluate the flow of your arguments, sentence clarity, and formatting consistency. Ask a colleague or mentor to read through the draft because writers often become blind to their own mistakes.

Once polished, disseminating the findings ensures that your hard work benefits patient care. Dissemination can take many forms, including publishing in peer-reviewed nursing journals, presenting at healthcare conferences, or sharing summaries with hospital quality improvement committees. Research that remains hidden in a filing cabinet cannot help patients.
