Writing a Research Report

Purpose and Structure of a Research Report

A research report is a formal document that details the process, findings, and implications of a systematic investigation. In nursing, these reports bridge the gap between clinical inquiry and evidence-based practice. The standard report follows a traditional structure designed to allow readers to evaluate the validity of the work efficiently. - **Preliminary Pages**: Title page, abstract, table of contents, and acknowledgments. - **Introduction**: Background, problem statement, objectives, and research questions. - **Literature Review**: Summary of existing knowledge, theoretical frameworks, and identification of gaps. - **Methodology**: Research design, population, sampling, data collection tools, and ethical considerations. - **Results**: Presentation of analyzed data using text, tables, and figures. - **Discussion**: Interpretation of findings, comparison with prior studies, limitations, and implications for practice. - **References and Appendices**: Citations and supplementary data collection instruments.

Drafting the Preliminary Pages and Introduction

The preliminary pages set the professional tone of the report. The **abstract** is particularly vital, serving as a concise summary of the entire study, usually kept under 250 words, and written last so it accurately reflects the completed document. The introduction then opens the main body by establishing the **background of the study**. It moves from a broad global or national perspective down to the specific clinical problem observed in the local setting. The **statement of the problem** clearly articulates the gap in knowledge or practice that necessitated the research. Following this, the **research questions or hypotheses** direct the focus of the investigation, ensuring every subsequent section aligns with the original intent of the study.

Presenting Literature Review and Methodology

A rigorous literature review does not simply list past studies; it critically synthesizes them. Group sources by themes or variables rather than summarizing one article after another chronologically. This demonstrates how prior work connects to the current study and highlights the specific gap your research addresses. Transitioning to the **methodology**, clarity is paramount. You must describe the **target population**, **sample size calculation**, and **sampling technique** with enough precision that another investigator could replicate the study. Detail the instruments used for data collection, addressing their **validity** and **reliability**. In Nigerian clinical research, securing ethical clearance from institutional review boards before data collection begins must be explicitly documented in this section.

Reporting Results and Data Analysis

The results section presents the processed data objectively without personal bias or interpretation. Begin with a description of the sample characteristics, often summarized as a demographic table. Follow this with findings directly addressing each research question or hypothesis. | Data Type | Presentation Method | Key Rule | |---|---|---| | Demographic Data | Tables or charts | Keep formatting clean and uncluttered | | Qualitative Themes | Narrative text with direct quotes | Use participant pseudonyms to maintain confidentiality | | Statistical Tests | Text combined with test values and p-values | Report exact values rather than just stating significance | Avoid repeating every single table value in the surrounding text; instead, highlight the most salient trends, anomalies, and key statistical outcomes.

Writing Discussion, Conclusions, and Recommendations

The discussion is where you give meaning to your results. Do not introduce new data here. Instead, interpret what your findings mean, explain unexpected outcomes, and compare your results with previous literature cited in your review. Acknowledge study **limitations**, such as small sample sizes or localized settings, which demonstrates scientific maturity. The **conclusion** flows logically from the discussion, summarizing the core takeaway of the study in concise prose. Finally, **recommendations** must be practical and actionable. In nursing reports, these should target clinical practice, nursing education, administration, and future research directions.

Referencing Styles and Academic Integrity

Academic integrity requires transparent acknowledgment of all sources used. Plagiarism—passing off another person's work or ideas as your own—is a serious ethical breach that can invalidate academic qualifications. Different academic journals and nursing institutions mandate specific **referencing styles**, such as APA, Harvard, or Vancouver. Consistency is critical. In-text citations must match the reference list at the end of the document precisely. When using direct quotes, enclose the text in quotation marks and include page numbers. Paraphrasing requires just as much citation integrity; changing a few words of an original sentence while keeping the structure without citing the author still constitutes plagiarism.

Editing, Proofreading, and Disseminating Findings

Before submission, a research report must undergo rigorous editing and proofreading. Editing focuses on sentence flow, logical transitions, clarity, and paragraph structure. Proofreading catches surface-level errors such as typos, grammatical mistakes, inconsistent formatting, and misaligned headings. Completing the written report is only half the process; **dissemination** ensures the knowledge reaches clinical areas where it can change practice. Dissemination strategies include publishing in peer-reviewed nursing journals, presenting findings at professional nursing conferences, or holding local inservice training sessions for hospital staff.
