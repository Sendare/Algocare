Presentation of Results

Introduction to Data Presentation in Nursing Research

When a nursing research study concludes, the collected data must be organized and shared clearly so other healthcare professionals can understand the findings. Data presentation is the bridge between raw numbers and clinical application. If findings are messy or confusing, life-saving insights can remain hidden, failing to improve patient care. Nursing research typically deals with two main types of data: quantitative data, which involves numbers and statistical measurements like vital signs or readmission rates, and qualitative data, which captures words, experiences, and behaviors through interviews or observations. Before presenting anything, researchers must clean their data, checking for missing values or recording errors that could distort the final picture. The primary goal of presenting results is not to show off complex mathematics, but to tell an honest, accurate story about what the data reveals. Whether the study looks at how a new wound care protocol reduces healing time or how mothers feel about postnatal depression screening, the results section must lay out the facts plainly and logically.

Choosing Between Tables, Graphs, and Textual Descriptions

Deciding how to display data depends entirely on the type of information and the volume of numbers involved. A researcher has three main formats to choose from:

  • Textual descriptions: Best used for highlighting a few key numbers or presenting simple demographic highlights without complex trends.
  • Statistical tables: Ideal for presenting large amounts of precise numerical data, allowing readers to look up exact values easily.
  • Charts and graphs: Perfect for showing trends over time, comparisons between groups, or patterns that would take too long to read in a table.

A common mistake is repeating the exact same numbers in the text that already appear in a table or graph. Instead, the narrative text should guide the reader's attention to the most important highlights of the table or graph without reciting every single cell. If a table contains fifty patient recovery scores, the text should point out the highest and lowest scores, or the overall average, rather than listing them all over again.

Effective Design and Formatting of Statistical Tables

A well-designed table allows a reader to absorb complex numerical information at a glance. Every table needs a clear, descriptive title that explains what data is included, along with the population and setting if relevant. Column and row headers must specify units of measurement, such as age in years, blood pressure in mmHg, or length of stay in days, so there is never any guesswork. Avoid using heavy vertical gridlines; simple horizontal lines at the top, bottom, and beneath the header row look cleaner and are easier to read. Numbers should be aligned consistently, usually by the decimal point, to make comparing values straightforward. Footnotes at the bottom of the table are useful for defining abbreviations, such as SD for standard deviation or CI for confidence interval, ensuring that anyone reading the document understands every symbol used.

Creating Clear and Accurate Charts and Graphs

Visual displays like bar charts, line graphs, and pie charts turn cold statistics into immediate visual understanding. However, poorly made charts can easily mislead the reader. Every chart must include labeled axes, a descriptive title, and a clear legend if multiple groups are shown. The vertical axis on bar charts should almost always start at zero to prevent exaggerating small differences between groups. Starting an axis at a higher number makes a minor improvement look massive, which can skew clinical interpretation. Line graphs work best for showing continuous data over time, such as tracking post-operative pain scores over five days. Pie charts should be used sparingly, restricted to showing parts of a whole when there are only a few categories; too many slices make a pie chart impossible to read.

Synthesizing Qualitative Findings and Narrative Data

Presenting qualitative research is very different from reporting statistical numbers. Instead of p-values and percentages, qualitative findings rely on themes, categories, and direct quotes from participants. Researchers organize these findings by grouping similar interview statements into core themes that answer the research question. To make the narrative authentic, researchers use verbatim quotes from participants, letting the patients or nurses speak in their own voices. These quotes must be properly contextualized, explaining who said it, such as a staff nurse or a chronic care patient, without revealing their real identity. The presentation should weave together the researcher's analytical interpretation with rich descriptive evidence, showing that the identified themes genuinely reflect the lived experiences of the population studied.

Common Pitfalls and Ethical Considerations in Results Presentation

Reporting research results carries a heavy ethical responsibility. One major pitfall is selective reporting, which happens when a researcher only highlights data that supports their preferred outcome while hiding results that contradict it. This practice distorts scientific literature and can lead to unsafe clinical practices. Another critical issue is protecting participant confidentiality, especially in qualitative studies where a unique quote or specific demographic detail might accidentally reveal someone's identity. Researchers must use pseudonyms and remove identifying markers from case descriptions. Additionally, researchers must separate their actual results from their personal opinions; the results section must report what the data shows, leaving the discussion and interpretation for a separate section. Misleading graph designs, manipulated axes, and altered data tables are serious ethical violations that undermine the integrity of the nursing profession.
