Presentation of Results

Introduction to Data Presentation in Nursing Research

Data presentation is the systematic organization and visual display of research findings so that readers can quickly grasp complex information. In nursing research, raw clinical data—whether gathered from patient charts, wound measurements, or qualitative interviews—remains meaningless until it is structured logically. Clear presentation transforms scattered numbers and patient narratives into actionable evidence that can improve bedside care, influence hospital policy, or guide public health interventions across Nigerian health institutions. The presentation phase bridges raw data collection and final interpretation. Researchers must decide which findings directly answer their research questions, discarding clutter while highlighting critical trends. Poorly presented data obscures valuable insights, frustrating examiners, peers, and clinical leaders who rely on the evidence. Ultimately, effective presentation honors the participants who contributed to the study by ensuring their data communicates a clear, truthful message.

Choosing Between Tables, Graphs, and Textual Descriptions

Selecting the right format depends entirely on the nature of the data and what the researcher wishes to emphasize. Using the wrong medium creates confusion and wastes valuable manuscript space. - **Textual Description**: Best for presenting simple, isolated figures, or highlighting specific qualitative nuances. Use text when data consists of fewer than three or four numbers. - **Statistical Tables**: Ideal for displaying precise numerical values, exact counts, and complex multi-variable relationships. Tables allow readers to look up specific data points easily. - **Charts and Graphs**: Designed for illustrating trends, patterns, proportions, and overall distributions at a glance. Visual formats communicate momentum, increases, or decreases much faster than dense rows of numbers.

Effective Design and Formatting of Statistical Tables

A well-designed table stands alone, meaning the reader should understand it without reading the surrounding text. Every table requires a clear, concise title that explains the variables, population, and setting. Avoid vertical gridlines entirely. Horizontal lines should be kept to a minimum—typically placed only at the very top, beneath the column headers, and at the bottom of the table. Column headers must clearly state the units of measurement, such as (in years), (mmHg), or (percentage). Footnotes are placed beneath the table to define abbreviations or explain missing data values, ensuring the main body remains uncluttered and easy to scan.

Creating Clear and Accurate Charts and Graphs

Visual graphics must convey data honestly and efficiently. Distorting scales or using unnecessary three-dimensional effects compromises scientific integrity for the sake of aesthetics. 1. **Bar Charts**: Best for comparing discrete categories or independent groups, such as infection rates across different hospital wards. 2. **Line Graphs**: Essential for tracking continuous changes or trends over time, such as post-operative pain scores recorded across several days. 3. **Pie Charts**: Useful for displaying parts of a whole, though they should be limited to five or fewer slices to maintain readability. Always ensure that axes are clearly labeled with variable names and measurement units. A distorted baseline on a bar chart can exaggerate minor clinical differences, misleading readers into believing a significant intervention effect exists where none is present.

Synthesizing Qualitative Findings and Narrative Data

Qualitative results do not rely on statistical tables or p-values. Instead, researchers present thematic structures, conceptual frameworks, and direct participant quotes to capture human experiences. Organizing qualitative findings requires grouping related codes into overarching themes that directly address the research inquiry. When incorporating verbatim quotes from patients or clinical staff, choose excerpts that truly represent the collective sentiment rather than isolated, sensational outliers. Each quote should be properly attributed using anonymized identifiers, such as [Participant 4, Rural Clinic]. Weave these quotes smoothly into the analytical narrative, ensuring the researcher's voice contextualizes the participant's lived experience without overinterpreting or altering their intended meaning.

Common Pitfalls and Ethical Considerations in Results Presentation

Integrity in results presentation requires rigorous honesty. Researchers must report all pre-planned outcomes, regardless of whether they yielded statistically significant results. Omitting negative or non-significant findings—a practice known as selective reporting—skews the global body of medical evidence and misleads clinical decision-making. Another frequent pitfall involves confusing correlation with causation in the narrative text. Just because two variables move together does not mean one caused the other. Furthermore, ethical safeguards demand that visual presentations or descriptive statistics never compromise patient anonymity, especially in smaller healthcare facilities where unique clinical cases can be easily identified by local peers.
