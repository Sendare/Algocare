Methods of Inquiry

Introduction to Methods of Inquiry in Nursing

Nursing inquiry is the systematic search for knowledge and understanding of phenomena relevant to nursing practice, education, and administration. Nurses encounter clinical questions daily that cannot be answered by tradition or routine alone. Understanding different methods of inquiry allows practitioners to critically evaluate evidence, challenge outdated practices, and adopt interventions that genuinely improve patient outcomes. Inquiry bridges the gap between clinical curiosity and rigorous scientific validation.

Ways of Knowing in Nursing

Nursing knowledge is not derived from research alone. Barbara Carper identified four fundamental patterns of knowing that shape how nurses understand their profession:

  • Empirical knowing: The science of nursing, derived from systematic research and objective facts.
  • Ethical knowing: The moral component, guiding what ought to be done in professional dilemmas.
  • Personal knowing: The therapeutic use of self, involving self-awareness and authentic connection with patients.
  • Aesthetic knowing: The art of nursing, reflecting the creative and empathetic delivery of care tailored to an individual.

Traditional and Authority-Based Inquiry

Before the widespread embrace of evidence-based practice, healthcare relied heavily on tradition, custom, and authority. Authority-based knowledge comes from experts, senior clinicians, or institutional leaders. While efficient, relying solely on authority or tradition has major limitations. Senior nurses may perpetuate practices based on personal experience rather than objective data. True inquiry requires questioning long-standing customs to verify their actual safety and effectiveness.

The Scientific Method as Systematic Inquiry

The scientific method provides a structured framework for investigating clinical problems objectively. It moves through logical phases: identifying a problem, reviewing existing literature, forming a hypothesis, collecting data, analyzing results, and drawing conclusions. Unlike personal opinion, the scientific method uses systematic controls to minimize bias and error, ensuring that findings can be replicated and trusted by other healthcare providers.

Quantitative Inquiry Approaches

Quantitative inquiry focuses on measuring variables using numbers, statistics, and objective tools. This approach answers questions about relationships, cause and effect, and the frequency of events. Common designs include descriptive, correlational, quasi-experimental, and randomized controlled trials. Quantitative research allows nurses to determine if a specific intervention, such as a new pain scale or medication protocol, produces statistically significant improvements in patient outcomes.

Qualitative Inquiry Approaches

Qualitative inquiry explores the human experience, meaning, and perceptions through narrative data rather than numbers. Methods such as phenomenology, ethnography, and grounded theory help nurses understand patient emotions, coping mechanisms, cultural beliefs, and lived realities. Instead of asking 'how much' or 'how often,' qualitative research asks 'what is the meaning of this experience?' This approach provides deep insight into patient perspectives that numbers alone cannot capture.

Mixed Methods Inquiry in Healthcare

Mixed methods research combines both quantitative and qualitative approaches within a single study. Healthcare problems are often complex, involving both measurable clinical outcomes and deep psychosocial factors. By integrating numbers with narrative feedback, researchers gain a comprehensive view of a clinical issue. For example, a study on diabetes management might track blood sugar levels quantitatively while interviewing patients about their dietary struggles qualitatively.

Ethical Considerations in Research Inquiry

All research inquiry involving human participants must adhere to strict ethical principles. These include respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. Researchers must obtain informed consent, protect patient confidentiality, and ensure that potential benefits outweigh any risks of harm. Vulnerable populations, such as children, prisoners, or unconscious patients, require special protective measures. Ethical oversight by institutional review boards ensures participant safety is never compromised for the sake of scientific discovery.
