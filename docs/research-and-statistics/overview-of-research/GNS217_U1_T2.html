Methods of Inquiry

Introduction to Methods of Inquiry in Nursing

Methods of inquiry provide the structured pathways through which nurses discover new facts, test existing theories, and validate clinical interventions. Without systematic inquiry, nursing practice risks relying solely on outdated habits and unverified assumptions. Inquiry transforms everyday clinical observations into structured questions, guiding the profession toward evidence-based care that improves patient outcomes across diverse healthcare settings, from tertiary teaching hospitals to primary rural health clinics in Nigeria.

Epistemology and Ways of Knowing

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that examines the nature, origin, and limits of human knowledge. In nursing, Barbara Carper's fundamental patterns identify four primary ways of knowing:

  • Empiricism: The science of nursing, derived from objective observation, measurement, and research.
  • Ethics: The moral component, focusing on what ought to be done and navigating complex clinical dilemmas.
  • Personal knowing: The therapeutic use of self, involving authentic engagement and self-awareness in nurse-patient relationships.
  • Aesthetics: The art of nursing, manifested in creative, highly individualized care delivery tailored to a specific patient's lived reality.

Traditional and Authority-Based Inquiry

Before the widespread adoption of empirical science, healthcare relied heavily on tradition and authority. Traditions are customs and routines passed down through generations, such as routinely changing intravenous cannulas every 48 hours regardless of clinical need. Authority-based inquiry relies on the pronouncements of recognized experts, senior clinicians, or institutional leaders.

While authority and tradition offer immediate guidance and maintain operational stability, they present significant pitfalls. Experts can be biased, and traditions often persist long after empirical evidence has proven them obsolete or ineffective.

The Scientific Method

The scientific method represents a structured, objective, and systematic approach to acquiring reliable knowledge. It moves through a logical sequence: identifying a problem, formulating a testable hypothesis, gathering empirical data through observation or experimentation, analyzing the findings, and drawing conclusions. By minimizing personal bias and controlling extraneous variables, the scientific method produces findings that can be replicated and validated by independent researchers across different global settings.

Quantitative Inquiry Approaches

Quantitative inquiry focuses on the systematic collection and analysis of numerical data. It aligns with a positivist worldview, which assumes an objective reality that can be measured and quantified. Common quantitative designs include descriptive studies, correlational research, quasi-experimental designs, and randomized controlled trials. Researchers use statistical tests to determine relationships between variables, assess cause-and-effect, and generalize findings from a smaller sample to a larger population.

Qualitative Inquiry Approaches

Qualitative inquiry explores the complex, subjective lived experiences of individuals. Grounded in naturalistic inquiry, it seeks to understand how people interpret their worlds and assign meaning to events. Common qualitative designs include phenomenology, ethnography, and grounded theory. Data collection typically involves in-depth interviews, participant observation, and textual analysis, yielding rich narrative data rather than statistical tables.

Mixed Methods Inquiry

Mixed methods inquiry combines quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis within a single research study. This approach leverages the strengths of both paradigms—offsetting the limitations of using either one alone. For instance, a researcher might survey patients to measure satisfaction levels numerically (quantitative) while conducting follow-up interviews to understand why patients felt that way (qualitative). This comprehensive strategy provides a more holistic understanding of complex healthcare phenomena.

Ethical Considerations in Research Inquiry

All forms of nursing inquiry must strictly adhere to ethical principles to protect human participants from harm. Core ethical tenets include autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. Researchers must secure informed consent, ensure voluntary participation, maintain confidentiality, and obtain approval from a recognized institutional ethics review board before commencing any study involving patients or health records.
