Uterine Fibroids

Introduction and Definition of Uterine Fibroids

Uterine fibroids, scientifically known as leiomyomas or myomas, are benign, non-cancerous smooth muscle tumors that develop within the myometrium of the uterus. They are the most common pelvic tumors affecting women of reproductive age, particularly women of African descent who often experience an earlier onset and more severe symptoms. While many women with fibroids remain completely asymptomatic and discover them incidentally during routine pelvic examinations or obstetric ultrasounds, others experience debilitating symptoms that significantly impact their quality of life. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, understanding uterine fibroids is essential because these benign growths frequently account for gynecological admissions, outpatient clinic visits, and surgical interventions such as myomectomies and hysterectomies. Although they are not malignant and rarely transform into leiomyosarcomas, their hormonal dependence means they can grow rapidly during reproductive years and typically regress following menopause when estrogen and progesterone levels decline. Nurses play a crucial role in identifying patients at risk, providing compassionate support, and demystifying the condition for women who may fear that fibroids represent a form of cancer.

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of Myomas

The anatomy and pathophysiology of uterine fibroids revolve around the myometrium, which is the middle muscular layer of the uterine wall. Each fibroid typically arises from a single mutant smooth muscle cell, meaning they are monoclonal in origin. Once initiated, their growth is heavily stimulated by the female sex hormones estrogen and progesterone, which upregulate various growth factors and promote cellular proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis. Fibroids are characterized by an excessive deposition of extracellular matrix, particularly collagen, which gives them their firm, rubbery consistency and distinguishes them structurally from the surrounding normal myometrial tissue. The blood supply to myomas is typically derived from branches of the uterine arteries, which wrap around the capsule of the tumor. As fibroids enlarge, they can outgrow their blood supply, leading to various forms of degeneration, such as hyaline, cystic, myxoid, or red (carneous) degeneration, the latter of which is especially common and painful during pregnancy. Understanding these pathophysiological mechanisms helps nursing students comprehend why fibroid size fluctuates with hormonal changes, such as during pregnancy or when using hormonal contraceptives, and why they generally shrink after the cessation of ovarian function at menopause.

Classifications and Types of Uterine Fibroids

Uterine fibroids are classified anatomically based on their exact location within the uterine walls, a factor that heavily dictates the clinical symptoms a patient will experience. Intramural fibroids are the most common type, developing within the thick muscular wall of the uterus and potentially enlarging the organ without altering its outer or inner contour initially. Submucosal fibroids lie directly beneath the endometrium and protrude into the uterine cavity; despite often being the smallest in size, they are notorious for causing severe abnormal uterine bleeding, dysmenorrhea, and reproductive challenges such as infertility or recurrent miscarriage. Subserosal fibroids develop beneath the outer peritoneal covering of the uterus and grow outward into the pelvic cavity. They can sometimes become pedunculated, meaning they are attached to the uterus by a slender stalk, which puts them at risk of twisting and causing acute surgical abdomen emergencies. Occasionally, fibroids can develop within the cervix (cervical fibroids) or between the layers of the broad ligament (intraligamentous). For nurses, recognizing these classifications is vital because the anatomical position directly correlates with the nursing care plan, surgical approach, and expected complications.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The etiology of uterine fibroids is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, hormonal influences, and environmental growth factors. Genetic mutations, particularly chromosomal abnormalities involving chromosomes 6, 7, and 12, are frequently identified in myoma tissue. Risk factors are well-documented through epidemiological studies and include age, with peak incidence occurring in women in their 30s and 40s approaching menopause. Race is a major non-modifiable risk factor, as women of African descent exhibit a significantly higher incidence, earlier age of onset, and more rapid tumor growth compared to Caucasian women. Family history also plays a prominent role; a woman with a mother or sister who has had fibroids is at a substantially higher risk of developing them herself. Modifiable and lifestyle-related risk factors include nulliparity (never having given birth), early menarche (before age 10), obesity, and diets high in red meat while low in green vegetables and citrus fruits. Hormonal exposure, such as the use of certain exogenous hormones or conditions characterized by prolonged hyperestrogenemia, further exacerbates risk. As nursing professionals, identifying these risk factors during client intake enables targeted health education, lifestyle counseling, and early screening for at-risk women in clinical and community settings.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Clinical manifestations of uterine fibroids vary widely depending on the size, number, and anatomical location of the tumors, with many women remaining completely asymptomatic. When symptoms do occur, the most classic presentation is abnormal uterine bleeding, often manifesting as heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), prolonged periods (menometrorrhagia), or intermenstrual bleeding, which can lead to chronic iron-deficiency anemia, fatigue, and pallor. Bulk-related symptoms arise as the enlarged uterus presses on adjacent pelvic structures; these include pelvic pressure, chronic lower back pain, urinary frequency and urgency due to bladder compression, constipation from rectal compression, and abdominal distension that can mistakenly make a woman look pregnant. Dysmenorrhea (painful menstruation) and dyspareunia (pain during sexual intercourse) are also frequently reported. During pregnancy, fibroids can cause complications such as preterm labor, placental abruption, fetal malpresentation, and postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony. As nurses, assessing these symptoms requires a holistic approach, inquiring sensitively about menstrual history, urinary habits, and pain levels, and recognizing that chronic blood loss significantly compromises a patient's physical well-being and daily functioning.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

The diagnostic evaluation of uterine fibroids begins with a thorough health history and physical examination, including a bimanual pelvic examination where the nurse and physician may palpate an enlarged, irregular, and mobile uterus. However, imaging studies are essential for definitive diagnosis and mapping. Pelvic ultrasound, utilizing both transabdominal and transvaginal approaches, is the primary, cost-effective, and non-invasive imaging modality used to visualize the number, size, and location of myomas. For more precise surgical planning or complex cases, saline infusion sonohysterography (SIS) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be performed to provide exceptionally detailed views of the uterine cavity and myometrium. Laboratory investigations are equally critical; a complete blood count (CBC) must be checked to evaluate for iron-deficiency anemia secondary to chronic heavy blood loss, and a serum pregnancy test is routinely performed to rule out gestation. Nursing assessment involves monitoring vital signs, assessing pain scores, evaluating the patient's emotional response to the diagnosis, and preparing the client for various diagnostic procedures by explaining what to expect, alleviating anxiety, and ensuring informed consent is obtained.

Medical and Surgical Management

The management of uterine fibroids is highly individualized, depending on the patient's age, symptom severity, desire for future fertility, and overall health status. Asymptomatic patients often require only watchful waiting with regular pelvic exams. Medical management aims to control bleeding and reduce tumor size by manipulating hormone levels; therapies include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain, oral contraceptives or progestins for bleeding, and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists to induce a temporary hypoestrogenic state that shrinks fibroids prior to surgery. For patients with severe symptoms or those wishing to preserve fertility, surgical options include hysteroscopic myomectomy (for submucosal fibroids), laparoscopic or abdominal myomectomy (removal of fibroids while leaving the uterus intact). Conversely, hysterectomy remains the definitive, permanent surgical cure for women who have completed their families or prefer definitive treatment. Minimally invasive radiological procedures, such as uterine artery embolization (UAE) and magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound surgery (MRgFUS), are also increasingly available. Nursing care in the perioperative phase involves comprehensive pre-operative education, pain management, monitoring for hemorrhage or infection, and providing emotional support as the patient navigates major reproductive surgery.

Potential Complications and Impact on Fertility

Uterine fibroids can lead to several potential complications, the most prevalent being chronic iron-deficiency anemia due to persistent, heavy menstrual blood loss. This manifests as severe fatigue, weakness, dizziness, and reduced exercise tolerance, requiring iron supplementation or blood transfusions in acute situations. Furthermore, fibroids pose significant challenges to a woman's fertility and obstetric outcomes. Depending on their size and location—particularly submucosal and large intramural fibroids—they can distort the uterine cavity, impair sperm transport, interfere with embryo implantation, or obstruct the fallopian tubes. During pregnancy, fibroids increase the risk of spontaneous abortion, intrauterine growth restriction, placental abruption, abnormal fetal presentations, obstructed labor, and postpartum hemorrhage due to the inability of the myometrium to contract effectively around the placental site. Nurses must maintain vigilance in monitoring pregnant clients with known fibroids for signs of pain (suggesting degeneration), bleeding, or preterm contractions, and provide empathetic counseling to women distressed by fertility struggles linked to their condition.

Holistic Nursing Care and Patient Education

Holistic nursing care for patients with uterine fibroids encompasses physical comfort, psychological support, cultural sensitivity, and comprehensive patient education. In many cultural contexts within Nigeria, fertility is deeply tied to womanhood and social status, making a diagnosis of fibroids—and the potential threat they pose to childbearing or the need for a hysterectomy—a source of profound emotional distress, anxiety, and stigma. Nurses must provide a safe, empathetic environment that encourages open communication and addresses these psychosocial dimensions. Patient education should focus on explaining the nature of the condition, clarifying that fibroids are benign, and reviewing prescribed medical therapies, potential side effects, and warning signs that require immediate medical attention, such as severe pelvic pain or excessive bleeding. Furthermore, nurses should counsel patients on healthy lifestyle modifications, including maintaining a balanced diet rich in iron and fiber, engaging in regular physical activity, and achieving a healthy body weight to help regulate estrogen levels. By empowering patients with accurate knowledge and holistic support, nurses bridge the gap between clinical treatment and long-term wellness.
