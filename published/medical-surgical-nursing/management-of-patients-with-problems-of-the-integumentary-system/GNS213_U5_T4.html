Burns

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Burns

Burns represent traumatic injuries to the skin and underlying tissues caused by thermal, electrical, chemical, or radiation agents. Understanding the pathophysiology of burns is vital for nursing students in Nigeria, as thermal injuries disrupt the body's largest protective barrier. Locally, open flames from kerosene cooking stoves, hot water, and electrical faults are frequent causes encountered in clinical practice. When tissue is exposed to extreme heat, cellular destruction occurs immediately, leading to protein coagulation and denaturation. The burn wound is classically divided into three distinct zones described by Jackson: the zone of coagulation, the zone of stasis, and the zone of hyperemia. The zone of coagulation is the area of irreversible tissue necrosis at the center of the injury where cellular death is complete. Surrounding this is the zone of stasis, characterized by compromised microvascular perfusion and potentially salvageable tissue if adequate resuscitation and tissue oxygenation are promptly maintained. The outermost zone of hyperemia experiences minimal injury, increased blood flow, and invariably undergoes spontaneous recovery without scarring. Nurses must recognize that poor initial management can easily convert the reversible zone of stasis into complete necrosis, thereby worsening the depth and overall severity of the burn wound.

Classification of Burn Injuries by Depth and Extent

Accurate classification of burn injuries by depth and total body surface area (TBSA) is fundamental for planning emergency nursing care and determining fluid resuscitation needs. Burn depth is traditionally categorized into first-degree (superficial), second-degree (partial-thickness), third-degree (full-thickness), and fourth-degree burns. First-degree burns involve only the epidermis, presenting with erythema and pain, similar to a mild sunburn. Second-degree burns extend into the dermis and are subdivided into superficial partial-thickness and deep partial-thickness; they present with blistering, severe pain, and weeping pink or mottled red bases. Third-degree burns destroy both the epidermis and entire dermis, often extending into subcutaneous fat, leaving the skin leathery, dry, and white or charred with absent sensation due to nerve destruction. Fourth-degree burns involve deep structures such as muscle, fascia, and bone. To estimate the extent of the injury in adults, nurses utilize the Rule of Nines, where major body areas are allocated percentages of 9 or multiples of 9. Alternatively, the palm method, where the patient's palm accounts for approximately 1% of their TBSA, is useful for scattered or smaller burns. For pediatric patients in Nigerian settings, modified charts or the Lund-Browder chart are preferred because relative body proportions differ significantly between children and adults, particularly regarding head and lower extremity sizes.

Systemic Responses and Pathophysiological Changes

Major burns exceeding 20% of the total body surface area trigger profound systemic responses affecting nearly every organ system. The primary systemic alteration is burn shock, which is a combination of hypovolemic and distributive shock. Increased capillary permeability allows plasma proteins, fluids, and electrolytes to leak rapidly from the intravascular space into the interstitial spaces, leading to severe generalized edema and intravascular volume depletion. This fluid shift occurs most intensely within the first 12 to 24 hours post-injury. Simultaneously, the cardiovascular system reacts with decreased cardiac output, increased peripheral vascular resistance, and myocardial depression mediated by inflammatory cytokines. Respiratory complications can occur due to direct thermal injury, inhalation of noxious smoke products, or systemic carbon monoxide poisoning, leading to airway edema, bronchospasm, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Metabolically, major burns induce a hypermetabolic and hypercatabolic state characterized by massive release of catecholamines, cortisol, and glucagon, leading to severe muscle wasting and accelerated energy expenditure. Renal perfusion diminishes sharply due to hypovolemia, risking acute kidney injury if aggressive fluid resuscitation is delayed. Furthermore, the loss of skin integrity and systemic immunosuppression place the patient at extreme risk for life-threatening sepsis and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).

Initial Assessment and Emergency Management

Initial assessment and emergency management of a burn patient follow the established Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocol: Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABCDE). Nurses must immediately secure the airway, especially if there are signs of facial burns, singed nasal hairs, carbonaceous sputum, or hoarseness, which indicate inhalation injury. High-flow humidified oxygen should be administered immediately via face mask to combat potential carbon monoxide poisoning. Circulation is evaluated by checking peripheral pulses, capillary refill, and blood pressure, while large-bore intravenous lines are cannulated through unburned skin whenever possible. Emergency care also requires stopping the burning process immediately by removing smoldering clothing and irrigating chemical burns with copious amounts of water, while avoiding ice as it worsens vasoconstriction and tissue damage. The patient's clothing and jewelry should be removed to prevent constriction as tissue edema develops rapidly. Nurses must perform a rapid baseline physical assessment to estimate burn depth and TBSA, obtain a detailed history of the injury mechanism, and administer tetanus prophylaxis and prescribed intravenous analgesics. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, level of consciousness, and peripheral neurovascular status in circumferentially burned limbs is essential to detect early signs of compartment syndrome.

Fluid Resuscitation and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Prompt fluid resuscitation is vital to prevent burn shock and maintain adequate organ perfusion in patients with extensive thermal injuries. The most widely used formula in clinical practice is the Parkland formula, which recommends administering 4 mL of Lactated Ringer's solution per kilogram of body weight per percentage of total body surface area (% TBSA) burned in the first 24 hours. Half of this calculated volume is administered in the first 8 hours post-burn (calculated from the time of injury, not the time of hospital arrival), and the remaining half is given over the subsequent 16 hours. In Nigerian clinical settings where delays in presentation are common, nurses must meticulously calculate the time elapsed and adjust infusion rates accordingly. Hemodynamic monitoring is critical to evaluate the efficacy of fluid resuscitation; urinary output serves as the most reliable and practical clinical indicator of systemic perfusion. An indwelling urinary catheter with a urometer should be inserted immediately, and nurses must titrate intravenous fluids to maintain a target urine output of 0.5 to 1.0 mL/kg/h in adults, and 1.0 to 1.5 mL/kg/h in children. Over-resuscitation can lead to pulmonary edema, cerebral edema, and abdominal compartment syndrome, whereas under-resuscitation leads directly to renal failure and irreversible shock.

Wound Care, Debridement, and Grafting

Comprehensive wound care is essential to prevent infection, promote re-epithelialization, and prepare the wound bed for eventual closure. Initial wound management involves gentle cleansing with mild soap and sterile water or normal saline, followed by debridement of loose, non-viable epidermis and ruptured blisters. Nurses must apply topical antimicrobial agents prescribed by the surgical team, such as silver sulfadiazine, mafenide acetate, or mupirocin, which inhibit bacterial colonization and prevent burn wound sepsis. Sterile dressings are then applied, ensuring they are non-constrictive and tailored to allow joint mobility. As the wound evolves, surgical debridement of necrotic tissue (eschar) is frequently performed to remove a major source of infection and inflammatory mediators. For full-thickness or deep partial-thickness burns that cannot heal spontaneously, skin grafting is necessary. Autografts, where the patient's own healthy skin is harvested from a donor site, represent the gold standard for permanent wound closure because they are not rejected by the body. Nurses play a crucial role in post-operative care by monitoring both the grafted site and the donor site for signs of hematoma formation, seroma accumulation, infection, and graft slippage, while ensuring proper immobilization of the grafted area.

Nutritional Support and Metabolic Management

Nutritional support and metabolic management are critical pillars in the holistic care of major burn patients due to the severe hypermetabolic response. Because resting energy expenditure can double or triple, failure to provide adequate nutritional support results in rapid lean body mass depletion, delayed wound healing, impaired immune function, and increased mortality. Early enteral nutrition via a nasogastric or feeding tube should be initiated as soon as gut motility is established, ideally within the first 24 to 48 hours post-burn, to maintain gut mucosal integrity and prevent bacterial translocation. Standardized formulas or high-protein, high-calorie diets are prescribed to meet calculated caloric and nitrogen requirements. Nurses must accurately record caloric intake, monitor gastrointestinal tolerance (checking for residual gastric volumes, abdominal distension, and diarrhea), and administer prescribed vitamin and mineral supplements such as zinc, vitamin C, and vitamin A, which are essential for collagen synthesis and tissue repair. Blood glucose levels must be monitored regularly because severe thermal injury often induces stress-induced hyperglycemia, which impairs white blood cell function and increases infection risks. Implementing strict glycemic control protocols through insulin therapy when necessary significantly improves clinical outcomes and reduces hypermetabolic stress.

Complications of Major Burn Injuries

Major burn injuries present numerous complex complications that demand vigilant nursing observation and multidisciplinary intervention. Local wound infections can easily progress to invasive burn wound sepsis, bacteremia, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), characterized by temperature instability, hemodynamic instability, leukocytosis, and altered mental status. Pulmonary complications such as pneumonia, atelectasis, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) frequently develop due to prolonged immobility, mechanical ventilation, or inhalation injury. Musculoskeletal complications include joint contractures caused by prolonged positioning in positions of comfort rather than extension, as well as heterotopic ossification, which is the abnormal formation of bone in soft tissues around burned joints. Gastrointestinal complications, particularly Curling's ulcers (stress-induced ulcerations of the gastric mucosa), pose a significant hemorrhage risk and require prophylactic antacids, H2-receptor antagonists, or proton pump inhibitors. Vascular complications such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) are common due to systemic hypercoagulability, immobility, and endothelial damage, requiring mechanical prophylaxis with sequential compression devices and pharmacological prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparins when indicated.

Rehabilitation and Long-Term Nursing Care

Rehabilitation and long-term nursing care begin on the first day of admission and extend well beyond acute hospital discharge, focusing on physical recovery, psychological adjustment, and reintegration into society. A major focus of rehabilitation is the prevention and management of hypertrophic scarring and keloids, which are common sequelae of deep dermal and full-thickness burns. Nurses educate patients and their families on the application of custom-fitted pressure garments worn 24 hours a day for up to a year or more to apply continuous pressure that flattens and softens developing scars. Physical and occupational therapy remain vital for restoring full range of motion, muscle strength, and functional independence through consistent stretching, splinting, and exercise regimens. Psychological support is equally important; burn survivors frequently experience depression, anxiety, body image disturbances, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) stemming from the trauma, disfigurement, and prolonged painful treatments. Nurses must provide empathetic counseling, encourage peer support groups, and prepare patients for social stigma, which is particularly challenging in many Nigerian communities. Discharge planning must encompass comprehensive education regarding home wound care, nutritional needs, signs of infection, and scheduled follow-up visits to ensure sustained long-term recovery and optimal quality of life.
