Assessment of Clients with Digestive Disorders

Introduction to Gastrointestinal Assessment in Nursing

Gastrointestinal (GI) assessment is a fundamental clinical skill required of nursing students in Nigeria to provide holistic care for clients experiencing digestive disorders. A thorough GI assessment enables nurses to identify acute problems, monitor chronic conditions, and evaluate the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions. The digestive system spans from the oral cavity to the anus, encompassing accessory organs like the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas. Assessment involves a systematic approach starting with subjective data collection through health history, followed by objective data gathered via physical examination techniques. Nurses must understand normal anatomical structures and physiological functions to effectively recognize abnormal findings such as abdominal distension, altered bowel sounds, or jaundice. Furthermore, cultural sensitivity and effective communication are paramount during the assessment process, as GI issues often involve sensitive body parts and bodily functions. By mastering GI assessment, nursing students lay the groundwork for safe, evidence-based clinical practice in various healthcare settings across Nigeria, from primary health clinics to tertiary teaching hospitals.

Health History

Gathering a comprehensive health history is the critical first step in evaluating a client with a digestive disorder. The nurse begins by exploring the chief complaint, utilizing standard pain assessment parameters such as PQRST (Provocation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, and Timing) if abdominal pain is present. Common gastrointestinal symptoms include nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, dyspepsia, pyrosis, changes in bowel habits, and hematemesis or melena. It is essential to inquire about the client's past medical and surgical history, including previous abdominal surgeries, inflammatory bowel disease, or peptic ulcer disease. Additionally, the nurse must obtain a detailed medication history, paying close attention to the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antibiotics, and laxatives, which frequently impact the GI tract. Family history should not be overlooked, as conditions like colorectal cancer, celiac disease, and peptic ulcers often have a genetic component. Finally, evaluating lifestyle factors such as dietary habits, alcohol consumption, smoking, recent travel history, and stress levels provides vital context for understanding the client's digestive health and guiding subsequent physical assessments.

Physical Assessment

The physical assessment of the abdomen follows a specific, altered sequence compared to other body systems to prevent the alteration of bowel motility and bowel sounds. The correct sequence is inspection, auscultation, percussion, and palpation. During inspection, the nurse observes the contour, symmetry, skin color, presence of scars, lesions, or visible peristalsis and pulsations. Auscultation is performed next using the diaphragm of the stethoscope to listen to all four abdominal quadrants for bowel sounds, noting whether they are normoactive, hypoactive, hyperactive, or absent, as well as listening over renal and iliac arteries for bruits. Percussion follows to assess the density of abdominal contents, detect fluid, gas, or solid masses, and estimate the size of the liver and spleen. Finally, palpation is conducted, starting with light palpation to identify areas of tenderness, muscle guarding, or superficial masses, followed by deep palpation to assess organomegaly and deeper structures. Nurses must ensure the client has an emptied bladder, is positioned comfortably in a supine position with knees flexed, and that the examiner's hands and stethoscope are warm to avoid tensing the abdominal musculature.

Diagnostic and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnostic and laboratory investigations provide objective data to confirm clinical suspicions, locate sites of pathology, and monitor disease progression in clients with digestive disorders. Common laboratory tests include a Complete Blood Count (CBC) to check for anemia resulting from GI bleeding or leukocytosis indicating infection or inflammation. Liver Function Tests (LFTs), such as bilirubin, ALT, AST, and albumin, assess hepatic health and biliary tract status. Serum amylase and lipase levels are crucial for diagnosing acute pancreatitis. Stool tests, including stool for occult blood (FOBT) and ova and parasites, help detect gastrointestinal bleeding and parasitic infections prevalent in tropical environments like Nigeria. Diagnostic imaging ranges from plain abdominal X-rays (KUB) to visualize gas patterns and obstructions, to advanced modalities like abdominal ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) scans, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Endoscopic procedures, such as esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy, allow direct visualization of the mucosal lining and tissue biopsy. Nurses play a vital role in preparing clients, monitoring for complications post-procedure, and ensuring informed consent is obtained.

Nutritional and Functional Status Evaluation

Evaluating the nutritional and functional status of clients with digestive disorders is essential because the GI tract is responsible for ingestion, digestion, absorption, and waste excretion. Digestive disorders frequently lead to malnutrition, electrolyte imbalances, and severe weight loss. The nursing assessment includes calculating the Body Mass Index (BMI), measuring mid-arm circumference, and noting any unintended weight changes over the past few months. Nurses should assess clinical signs of nutritional deficiencies, such as dry skin, brittle nails, glossitis, stomatitis, or muscle wasting. Functional status evaluation involves assessing the client's ability to chew, swallow, digest, and eliminate food independently, or determining if assistive devices or enteral/parenteral nutrition support is required. Diet history should detail usual daily intake, cultural food preferences, food intolerances, and hydration status. By systematically evaluating nutritional status, the nurse can collaborate effectively with clinical dietitians to formulate appropriate nutritional care plans, correct deficiencies, promote healing, and improve overall client outcomes.

Documentation and Patient Preparation for Procedures

Accurate documentation and careful patient preparation are vital responsibilities of the nurse when managing clients undergoing diagnostic or therapeutic gastrointestinal procedures. Documentation of GI assessments must be objective, concise, and timely, using standardized nursing terminology to record physical findings, vital signs, pain scores, and client statements. Clear documentation ensures continuity of care across different shifts and healthcare providers. Patient preparation varies depending on the specific procedure ordered. For endoscopic procedures like a colonoscopy or EGD, preparation typically involves bowel cleansing regimens (such as oral cathartics or enemas), fasting instructions (nil per os/NPO status for several hours prior), and checking baseline coagulation profiles. Nurses must educate clients on what to expect during and after the procedure, verify that informed consent has been properly signed, and remove any dentures or metal objects as required. Post-procedure, documentation must include monitoring recovery parameters such as return of the gag reflex following upper endoscopy, vital signs stability, monitoring for signs of bowel perforation or bleeding, and ensuring safe discharge instructions are provided.
