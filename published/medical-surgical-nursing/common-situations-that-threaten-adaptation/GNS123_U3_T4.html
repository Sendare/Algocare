Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is defined as the acute or chronic escape of blood from the vascular system, occurring when there is a compromise or rupture in the integrity of blood vessel walls, including arteries, veins, or capillaries. In the context of nursing care in Nigeria, understanding haemorrhage is vital due to its high incidence in emergency departments, surgical wards, and obstetric units, particularly concerning postpartum haemorrhage and traumatic injuries from road traffic accidents. Blood loss can be rapid and catastrophic or slow and insidious, posing an immediate threat to tissue perfusion and cellular oxygenation. When a blood vessel is injured, the body's primary hemostatic mechanisms attempt to stem the flow through vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and the activation of the blood clotting cascade. As student nurses, you must recognize that failure to manage haemorrhage promptly leads to hypovolemic shock, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome, and ultimately death. Assessment must be swift, systematic, and continuous, focusing on identifying the source of bleeding and estimating the volume of blood lost. Because blood is the primary vehicle for oxygen transport, any significant reduction in circulating blood volume drastically impairs tissue metabolism. Therefore, mastery of the definition and foundational concepts of haemorrhage serves as the bedrock for effective emergency nursing interventions, ensuring that patients receive timely life-saving care across diverse clinical settings in Nigeria.

Classification and Types of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is classified using various criteria, primarily based on the anatomical source of the bleeding and the time frame in which it occurs. Anatomically, bleeding is categorized as external, where blood escapes visibly from the body through a wound or natural orifice, or internal, where blood accumulates within body cavities such as the abdomen, thorax, or cranial vault, making it much harder to detect initially. Additionally, haemorrhage is classified by the specific type of blood vessel involved: arterial bleeding is characterized by bright red blood that spurts synchronously with the pulse due to high pressure; venous bleeding features dark red blood flowing in a steady, continuous stream; and capillary bleeding oozes slowly from minor superficial wounds. Based on the time course, haemorrhage can be acute, occurring suddenly from major trauma or a ruptured aneurysm, or chronic, involving slow, persistent blood loss over weeks or months, as seen in peptic ulcers or heavy menstrual bleeding, which frequently leads to iron-deficiency anaemia. Furthermore, temporal classifications distinguish between primary haemorrhage (occurring immediately at the time of injury or surgery), reactionary haemorrhage (occurring within 24 hours due to the slipping of a ligature or a rise in blood pressure), and secondary haemorrhage (occurring 7 to 14 days post-injury or surgery, typically caused by localized infection and tissue sloughing). Recognizing these classifications enables nursing students to anticipate the severity and urgency of care required.

Pathophysiology and Body Compensatory Mechanisms

When a significant volume of blood is lost, the human body initiates a complex cascade of physiological and compensatory mechanisms to maintain perfusion to vital organs, specifically the brain and the heart. The immediate response involves baroreceptors located in the aortic arch and carotid sinuses detecting a drop in blood pressure. These receptors signal the vasomotor center in the medulla oblongata to increase sympathetic nervous system activity. This sympathetic surge triggers widespread vasoconstriction of blood vessels in non-essential vascular beds, such as the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and skeletal muscles, effectively shunting remaining blood to the core organs. Concurrently, tachycardia (an increased heart rate) and increased myocardial contractility occur to maintain cardiac output despite the reduced stroke volume. Renal compensatory mechanisms are also activated: the kidneys respond to decreased perfusion by releasing renin, which activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to sodium and water reabsorption to expand intravascular volume, while antidiuretic hormone (ADH) is released from the posterior pituitary to promote further water conservation. Cellularly, hypoxia shifts metabolism toward anaerobic pathways, resulting in the production of lactic acid and the risk of metabolic acidosis. As nursing students, you must recognize that these compensatory mechanisms can successfully mask early blood loss, meaning vital signs may remain deceptively normal until the body's reserves are suddenly exhausted, leading to rapid clinical decompensation and profound shock.

Causes and Risk Factors

Haemorrhage arises from a diverse array of traumatic, medical, and surgical causes that compromise vascular integrity or disrupt the body's normal clotting mechanisms. Traumatic causes are among the most frequent, encompassing blunt and penetrating injuries resulting from motor vehicle accidents, falls, knife wounds, and gunshot injuries, which can directly lacerate major blood vessels or cause internal organ rupture (such as splenic or hepatic rupture). Medical and non-traumatic causes include gastrointestinal conditions like peptic ulcer disease, esophageal varices secondary to portal hypertension, and inflammatory bowel diseases that erode mucosal blood vessels. In obstetric practice, causes include postpartum haemorrhage due to uterine atony, retained placental tissue, and genital tract lacerations, which remain leading causes of maternal morbidity and mortality in Nigeria. Coagulopathies—whether inherited, such as hemophilia, or acquired due to liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, or anticoagulant therapy (such as heparin or warfarin)—significantly increase bleeding risk because the blood fails to form stable clots upon vessel injury. Surgical complications, vessel wall pathologies like aneurysms, and malignancies that invade blood vessels also contribute substantially to haemorrhage risk. Identifying these underlying causes and risk factors during nursing assessments allows for proactive preventive measures, targeted monitoring, and rapid intervention when bleeding episodes occur.

Clinical Assessment and Grading of Blood Loss

Clinical assessment of haemorrhage requires a systematic approach to evaluate the extent of blood loss and monitor the patient's hemodynamic stability. Healthcare providers utilize standardized grading systems, such as the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) classification of hypovolemic shock, which categorizes blood loss into four classes based on physiological parameters including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, urine output, and mental status. Class I haemorrhage involves a blood loss of up to 15% (approx. 750 mL in an adult), with minimal clinical symptoms and normal vital signs. Class II involves a 15% to 30% loss (750–1500 mL), characterized by tachycardia, tachypnea, and narrowed pulse pressure. Class III represents a 30% to 40% loss (1500–2000 mL), marked by pronounced hypotension, tachycardia, delayed capillary refill, and mental confusion or anxiety. Class IV is a critical, life-threatening blood loss exceeding 40% (>2000 mL), presenting with marked hypotension, severe tachycardia, negligible urine output, cold clammy skin, and lethargy or coma. As a nursing student, you must perform frequent vital sign checks, assess skin color and temperature, monitor capillary refill time, and accurately measure urine output via an indwelling catheter as a sensitive indicator of renal perfusion. Recognizing subtle early signs of deterioration—such as mild restlessness or a slight rise in heart rate—is crucial for preventing irreversible shock.

Nursing Interventions and Emergency Management

Nursing interventions for a patient experiencing acute haemorrhage demand rapid, decisive, and prioritized emergency management to preserve life and restore tissue perfusion. The primary nursing action follows the ABCDE protocol: ensure a patent Airway, support adequate Breathing with high-flow oxygen, and evaluate Circulation. To address circulation, the nurse must immediately apply direct, firm pressure to external bleeding sites using sterile dressings or bandages, elevating the bleeding limb if appropriate and practical. For internal or severe bleeding, establishing immediate intravenous access with two large-bore cannulas (14G or 16G) is critical to facilitate rapid fluid resuscitation with crystalloids (such as Normal Saline or Ringer's Lactate) and the preparation of blood products. Vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation, must be monitored continuously, and baseline blood samples should be sent urgently for full blood count, typing, and cross-matching. The nurse should position the patient appropriately—typically in a modified Trendelenburg position (flat with legs slightly elevated) unless contraindicated by head injury or respiratory distress—to enhance venous return to the heart. Accurate fluid balance charting, continuous neurological checks, and clear, urgent communication with the multidisciplinary medical team are essential competencies for nurses managing haemorrhagic emergencies in Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Medical and surgical treatments for haemorrhage aim to definitively stop bleeding, restore circulating blood volume, and correct underlying coagulopathies. Medical management centers on aggressive fluid resuscitation and the administration of blood components, including Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs) to restore oxygen-carrying capacity, Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) to replace clotting factors, platelets for thrombocytopenia, and cryoprecipitate for fibrinogen replacement. Pharmacological agents such as tranexamic acid (TXA) are frequently administered to inhibit fibrinolysis and stabilize blood clots, especially in trauma and postpartum haemorrhage. Vasopressors may be used selectively if hypotension persists despite adequate volume replacement. When medical interventions fail to control bleeding, surgical or interventional radiological treatments become necessary. Surgical management involves exploratory laparotomy or thoracotomy to directly ligate bleeding vessels, repair damaged organs, or pack surgical cavities. In obstetric emergencies, procedures range from uterine massage and bimanual compression to balloon tamponade, uterine artery ligation, or hysterectomy as a life-saving measure. Interventional radiology techniques, such as transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE), offer a minimally invasive method to occlude bleeding vessels using coils or particulate agents. As a nurse, managing these treatments requires rigorous monitoring of transfusion reactions, titration of blood products, and vigilant post-operative care.

Complications of Haemorrhage

Complications of haemorrhage can be severe, systemic, and life-threatening if bleeding is prolonged or resuscitation is delayed. The most immediate and critical complication is hypovolemic shock, characterized by inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular hypoxia, which can rapidly progress to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) affecting the kidneys, liver, and lungs. Prolonged tissue hypoperfusion frequently leads to acute kidney injury (AKI) due to renal tubular necrosis. Additionally, massive blood loss and subsequent rapid volume replacement can precipitate the lethal triad of trauma: hypothermia (caused by cold fluids and blood products), metabolic acidosis (from anaerobic cellular metabolism), and coagulopathy (resulting from the dilution and consumption of clotting factors). Transfusion-related complications are also significant concerns, including transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), hemolytic transfusion reactions due to ABO incompatibility, and the transmission of blood-borne infections such as HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C. In the recovery phase, patients are at heightened risk for deep vein thrombosis, secondary infections at wound sites, and psychological trauma such as post-traumatic stress disorder. As nursing students, you must remain vigilant in monitoring post-haemorrhage patients for these complications, ensuring early detection and rapid multidisciplinary intervention to optimize patient outcomes.
