Acute Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Acute Glomerulonephritis

Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) is a renal disease characterized by sudden inflammation of the glomeruli, which are the vital filtering units within the kidneys. In nursing education and clinical practice, understanding this condition is crucial for prompt recognition and intervention. The primary structural damage involves the proliferation of cellular elements and infiltration of inflammatory cells within the glomerular capillary loops. This leads to an abrupt impairment in the kidney's ability to filter blood effectively, retain essential proteins, and excrete nitrogenous waste products. As the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) sharply declines, patients experience a cascade of fluid and electrolyte imbalances that severely impact overall systemic homeostasis. AGN can manifest acutely across all age groups, but it is notably prevalent among school-aged children and young adults. Without timely medical intervention and skilled nursing care, the acute inflammatory response can progress to significant renal impairment, making early detection through vigilant assessment and diagnostic testing an absolute priority for student nurses in clinical rotations.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The pathophysiology of acute glomerulonephritis is fundamentally rooted in an immune-mediated mechanism, most commonly triggered by an antecedent infection elsewhere in the body. When antigen-antibody complexes form in the circulation, they become trapped within the glomerular capillary membrane. This deposition activates the complement cascade, a powerful arm of the immune system, leading to the recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes. The release of inflammatory mediators, reactive oxygen species, and lysosomal enzymes damages the delicate glomerular basement membrane and capillary endothelium. Consequently, the permeability of the glomerulus increases dramatically. This altered permeability allows blood cells and plasma proteins to escape into the urinary filtrate, manifesting clinically as hematuria and proteinuria. Furthermore, the impaired filtration capacity prevents adequate sodium and water excretion, resulting in fluid retention, circulatory congestion, and the classic signs of edema and hypertension. Grasping this immunologic cascade is essential for nursing students as it links the foundational microbiology and immunology concepts to clinical signs.

Etiology and Common Causes in the Nigerian Context

In the Nigerian healthcare context, the etiology of acute glomerulonephritis is heavily influenced by environmental and socio-economic factors. The most prevalent cause remains post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSAGN), which typically follows an infection caused by group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus. These antecedent infections frequently present as impetigo (skin sores) or streptococcal pharyngitis (throat infections), both of which are common among children living in crowded communal or rural settings. Additionally, tropical infections prevalent in Nigeria, such as malaria and various viral or bacterial illnesses, can trigger immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis. Poorly treated skin infections, often exacerbated by delayed access to healthcare facilities and the prevalence of traditional remedies before seeking formal medical care, significantly increase the risk of developing PSAGN. Student nurses must recognize these local epidemiological trends, as thorough history-taking regarding recent skin infections or sore throats is a critical competency when assessing pediatric and young adult patients presenting with symptoms of acute renal compromise.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Clinical manifestations of acute glomerulonephritis appear abruptly, typically one to three weeks following the initial streptococcal infection. One of the hallmark assessment findings is edema, which often starts characteristically as periorbital swelling (puffiness around the eyes) upon waking in the morning, later progressing to dependent edema in the lower extremities and generalized anasarca. Patients frequently present with hematuria, giving the urine a characteristic smoky, tea-colored, or cola-colored appearance due to the degradation of red blood cells passing through the acidic urinary tract. Proteinuria is another key feature, which can sometimes be in the nephrotic range, leading to frothy urine. Hypertension is commonly observed secondary to fluid overload and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation, and it can range from mild to severe, occasionally precipitating hypertensive encephalopathy. Constitutional symptoms such as fatigue, lethargy, anorexia, low-grade fever, and dull flank pain due to renal capsule distention may also be reported. Nurses must perform systematic head-to-toe physical assessments, closely monitoring fluid balance and vital signs to detect early signs of clinical deterioration.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming a diagnosis of acute glomerulonephritis requires a combination of detailed clinical history, physical examination, and targeted laboratory investigations. Urinalysis is foundational, revealing persistent microscopic or macroscopic hematuria, proteinuria, and red blood cell casts, which are pathognomonic for glomerular origin bleeding. Renal function tests typically show elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating a reduced glomerular filtration rate. Serological tests provide crucial evidence of a recent streptococcal infection; an elevated anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titer is common in post-streptococcal cases, alongside transiently depressed serum complement levels (specifically C3). Complete blood count (CBC) may demonstrate mild anemia due to hemodilution or low-grade hemolysis. In some complex or rapidly progressive cases, renal ultrasonography or a renal biopsy may be performed to visualize kidney size, assess structural integrity, and rule out other renal pathologies. Student nurses should understand how to collect these specimens correctly and interpret basic lab trends to support the multidisciplinary healthcare team.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical management of acute glomerulonephritis focuses primarily on supportive care, controlling complications, and treating any underlying active infection. If throat or skin cultures test positive for streptococcal bacteria, a full course of antibiotics, typically penicillin or erythromycin, is prescribed to eradicate any remaining organisms and prevent transmission. Pharmacological therapy for fluid overload and hypertension often includes loop diuretics, such as furosemide, to promote diuresis and reduce vascular congestion. Antihypertensive medications are administered to strictly control blood pressure and prevent end-organ damage. Dietary modifications are an essential adjunct to medical therapy; patients frequently require sodium and fluid restrictions to minimize edema and prevent hypertension, as well as protein restriction if significant azotemia and elevated BUN are present. In severe cases involving refractory anuria, severe hyperkalemia, or pulmonary edema, temporary dialysis may be necessary. Nurses play a pivotal role in administering these medications accurately, monitoring therapeutic responses, and collaborating with dietitians to ensure adherence to dietary restrictions.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Management

Developing a comprehensive nursing care plan for a patient with acute glomerulonephritis requires addressing actual and potential nursing diagnoses, such as Excess Fluid Volume, Risk for Impaired Skin Integrity, and Risk for Ineffective Renal Perfusion. The nurse must maintain meticulous intake and output (I&O) records, weighing the patient daily at the exact same time using the same scale to monitor fluid retention or diuresis. Vital signs, particularly blood pressure, must be monitored frequently to detect sudden spikes that could indicate hypertensive emergencies. Skin care is vital, especially for patients with severe edema, requiring regular repositioning and gentle hygiene to prevent skin breakdown over pressure points. Conserving patient energy is also important, as fatigue is common. Furthermore, the nurse acts as an educator and advocate, supporting the patient and family through anxiety related to sudden hospitalization. Documenting clinical observations accurately ensures continuity of care across all nursing shifts in the pediatric or adult medical ward.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Although many patients with acute glomerulonephritis experience a favorable recovery, severe complications can arise abruptly, requiring immediate emergency interventions. Hypertensive encephalopathy is a critical neurological emergency characterized by severe headache, altered mental status, confusion, vomiting, and seizures resulting from extreme blood pressure elevations. Another severe risk is acute pulmonary edema and congestive heart failure, triggered by profound fluid overload. Furthermore, severe hyperkalemia can develop due to impaired potassium excretion by the failing kidneys, posing an immediate threat of lethal cardiac arrhythmias. Nurses must remain highly vigilant, recognizing early signs of respiratory distress, chest pain, neurological changes, or peaked T waves on an ECG. Emergency interventions include the administration of rapid-acting antihypertensives, emergency diuretic therapy, and interventions for hyperkalemia such as intravenous calcium gluconate, insulin, and glucose. Quick nursing recognition and activation of the medical emergency team can be lifesaving.

Prognosis, Patient Education, and Home Care

The prognosis for acute glomerulonephritis is generally favorable, especially in pediatric patients, with the vast majority achieving complete recovery of renal function. However, patient education and home care are vital to ensure long-term health and prevent recurrence or chronic progression. Nurses must educate patients and their caregivers on the importance of completing any prescribed antibiotic regimens fully, even after acute symptoms subside. Education should also cover dietary modifications, such as maintaining low sodium intake until clearance is given by the physician, and recognizing warning signs of relapse, such as returning tea-colored urine or facial puffiness. Emphasizing the need for regular follow-up appointments and repeat urinalyses ensures that any lingering proteinuria or hematuria is monitored. In the Nigerian community setting, nurses should encourage early reporting of skin infections and sore throats to local health clinics to prevent secondary cases of streptococcal disease. Empowering families with knowledge promotes sustainable health practices and improves overall clinical outcomes.
