Endotracheal Intubation

Introduction and Indications for Endotracheal Intubation

Endotracheal intubation is an advanced airway management technique where a flexible plastic tube is inserted into the trachea through the mouth or nose. As nursing students at Algocare, understanding the precise indications for this critical procedure is essential for emergency and critical care nursing. The primary indications for endotracheal intubation include the need for mechanical ventilation due to respiratory failure, protection of the lower airway from aspiration in patients with depressed level of consciousness or impaired laryngeal reflexes, management of upper airway obstruction caused by trauma, edema, or foreign bodies, and the need to facilitate pulmonary hygiene and suctioning in compromised patients. Respiratory failure can be hypoxic, where the patient cannot maintain adequate oxygen saturation, or hypercapnic, where the patient retains excessive carbon dioxide due to ventilatory muscle fatigue. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores of 8 or less are a classic indicator that a patient can no longer protect their own airway and requires immediate intubation. Recognizing these clinical triggers allows nurses to anticipate physician orders, gather necessary emergency equipment promptly, and prepare the patient psychologically and physically for the procedure, ultimately improving patient outcomes in Nigerian clinical settings.

Anatomical Considerations of the Upper Airway

A thorough knowledge of upper airway anatomy is fundamental for successful and safe endotracheal intubation. The upper airway consists of the nasal cavity, oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx. During direct laryngoscopy, the nurse and practitioner must navigate the structures in a specific sequence to visualize the glottis. Air entering through the mouth passes the lips, teeth, and tongue. The tongue is often the primary obstacle and must be displaced to the left by the laryngoscope blade. Posterior to the tongue is the pharynx, divided into the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx. The epiglottis, a leaf-shaped flap of cartilage, acts as a valve over the entrance of the larynx. When using a curved (Macintosh) blade, the tip is placed in the vallecula (the space between the base of the tongue and the epiglottis) to indirectly lift the epiglottis and expose the vocal cords. When using a straight (Miller) blade, the tip is placed directly under the epiglottis to lift it manually. Below the epiglottis lie the vocal cords, which mark the boundary between the upper and lower airways. Passing the endotracheal tube beyond the vocal cords into the trachea is the ultimate goal. Nursing students must understand these anatomical landmarks to assist effectively, anticipate complications such as trauma to the teeth or soft tissues, and verify correct positioning.

Required Equipment and Preparation

Proper preparation of equipment and the environment is critical for minimizing patient risk during endotracheal intubation. Nurses must assemble and check all supplies using the 'SALT' or 'MSMAIDS' mnemonic to ensure nothing is missing during an emergency. Essential equipment includes a functioning suction apparatus with rigid catheters (Yankauer) to clear secretions or vomitus, a bag-valve-mask (BVM) connected to a 100% oxygen source, and an appropriately sized endotracheal (ET) tube equipped with a functional high-volume, low-pressure cuff. The cuff must be tested for leaks by inflating it with air using a syringe before insertion. A stylet is usually inserted into the ET tube to provide rigidity and shape, ensuring the tip does not protrude past the distal end of the tube. Laryngoscope handles with working batteries and a selection of both curved and straight blades must be tested for bright light emission. Additional necessities include a 10-mL syringe for cuff inflation, securement devices such as tape or commercial tube holders, a stethoscope for auscultation, and a colorimetric end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) detector. Emergency medications, including sedatives and neuromuscular blocking agents, should be readily available. Having a difficult airway cart nearby is also standard best practice in critical care units across Nigeria.

Pre-oxygenation and Pharmacological Management

Before any attempt at endotracheal intubation, optimal pre-oxygenation is vital to build an oxygen reserve in the patient's lungs and blood, preventing dangerous hypoxemia during the apneic period of intubation. Nurses administer 100% oxygen via a non-rebreather mask or bag-valve-mask device for approximately 3 to 5 minutes, or perform vital capacity breaths if time permits. Pharmacological management during rapid sequence intubation (RSI) involves the coordinated administration of induction agents and neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs). Induction agents, such as propofol, etomidate, or ketamine, are given first to induce rapid unconsciousness and amnesia. Etomidate is frequently chosen in hemodynamically unstable patients because it maintains cardiovascular stability, whereas ketamine is beneficial in patients with bronchospasm due to its bronchodilatory properties. Following sedation, a neuromuscular blocking agent such as succinylcholine (a depolarizing blocker) or rocuronium (a non-depolarizing blocker) is administered to achieve complete skeletal muscle relaxation, eliminate airway reflexes, and optimize visualization of the vocal cords. Nurses must monitor vital signs continuously throughout this phase, anticipating rapid drops in blood pressure or oxygen saturation and communicating effectively with the medical team.

Step-by-Step Intubation Procedure

The step-by-step execution of endotracheal intubation requires precision, teamwork, and adherence to sterile and safe practices. First, position the patient correctly by aligning the ear canal with the sternal notch (the sniffing position), unless a cervical spine injury is suspected, in which case in-line stabilization must be maintained. The nurse hands the laryngoscope to the practitioner, who holds the handle in their left hand. The practitioner opens the patient's mouth using a scissor technique and gently inserts the blade into the right side of the mouth, sweeping the tongue to the left. The laryngoscope is lifted upward and forward along the axis of the handle—never using the teeth as a fulcrum—to visualize the vocal cords. Once the glottis is in view, the nurse passes the prepared endotracheal tube through the vocal cords until the cuff disappears past the cords, typically noting the centimeter marking at the teeth or lips (usually 21-23 cm in adult females and males, respectively). The stylet is gently withdrawn by the nurse or practitioner. The cuff is then inflated with air to the recommended pressure (usually 20-25 cmH2O) to secure the tube and seal the airway. Finally, mechanical or manual ventilation is initiated while confirmation tests are performed.

Confirmation of Tube Placement

Immediate and accurate confirmation of endotracheal tube placement is vital to prevent accidental esophageal intubation, which can rapidly lead to fatal hypoxia and cardiac arrest. The gold standard for confirming correct tracheal placement is continuous waveform capnography or a colorimetric end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) detector, which changes color in the presence of exhaled carbon dioxide from the lungs. In addition to EtCO2 monitoring, the nurse and practitioner must perform a thorough physical assessment. This includes auscultating bilateral breath sounds over the chest wall (noting equal and bilateral air entry) and listening over the epigastrium; gurgling sounds over the stomach indicate esophageal intubation and require immediate tube removal. Other clinical signs of correct placement include observing symmetrical chest wall expansion with each ventilation breath, noting the presence of condensation inside the endotracheal tube during exhalation, and checking pulse oximetry readings for improvement in oxygen saturation. Finally, a portable chest X-ray is obtained as soon as possible to verify that the tip of the endotracheal tube is positioned approximately 3 to 5 centimeters above the carina, ensuring equal ventilation of both mainstem bronchi.

Nursing Care and Tube Maintenance

Ongoing nursing care and meticulous tube maintenance are critical responsibilities for patients with an endotracheal tube. Once tube placement is verified, the nurse must securely anchor the tube using adhesive tape or a specialized commercial device, documenting the exact centimeter marking at the lip or teeth to monitor for accidental migration. Oral care should be performed at least every 2 to 4 hours using chlorhexidine mouthwash to prevent Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP), a common and deadly hospital-acquired infection. Suctioning of the endotracheal tube must be performed only when clinically indicated—such as visible secretions, coughing, or coarse breath sounds—rather than on a routine schedule, using sterile technique and pre-oxygenation to prevent hypoxia and mucosal trauma. The nurse must regularly monitor and document endotracheal cuff pressure every 8 hours, keeping it between 20 and 25 cmH2O to prevent tracheal necrosis from over-inflation or aspiration from under-inflation. Furthermore, maintaining the head of the bed elevated between 30 and 45 degrees significantly reduces the risk of aspiration and VAP. Providing effective communication tools and sedation scoring ensures the patient's comfort and safety throughout mechanical ventilation.

Potential Complications and Emergency Troubleshooting

Endotracheal intubation carries inherent risks, and nurses must be highly vigilant to detect and troubleshoot complications swiftly. Immediate complications include dental trauma, lacerations of the lips or pharynx, esophageal intubation, aspiration of gastric contents, and severe hemodynamic instability or arrhythmias due to vagal stimulation or hypoxia. Long-term or delayed complications include tracheal stenosis, vocal cord paralysis, tracheoesophageal fistula, and ventilator-associated pneumonia. One of the most critical acute emergencies is accidental extubation or tube displacement. If a patient suddenly desaturates, becomes cyanotic, shows asymmetric chest expansion, or loses breath sounds on one side (often the left lung due to right mainstem intubation), the nurse must act rapidly. Using the 'DOPE' mnemonic helps structure troubleshooting: D for Dislodgement, Obstruction, Pneumothorax, or Equipment failure. If tube obstruction is suspected from thick mucus plugs, the tube should be suctioned immediately. If the tube has migrated into the right mainstem bronchus, the nurse notifies the physician to withdraw the tube slightly. If the tube is accidentally dislodged completely, the nurse must immediately remove the tube, apply 100% oxygen via bag-valve-mask, and call for emergency airway assistance.

Extubation Process and Post-Extubation Care

The extubation process involves removing the endotracheal tube once the patient has recovered sufficiently to maintain a patent airway, breathe independently, and protect against aspiration. Nursing students must understand the criteria for successful extubation, which include stable hemodynamic status, adequate oxygenation and ventilation parameters on minimal ventilator support, resolution of the underlying condition that necessitated intubation, and demonstrated airway reflexes such as an active cough and gag reflex. Before extubation, the nurse ensures suction equipment is ready, suctions the pharynx and endotracheal tube thoroughly, and hyperoxygenates the patient. The patient is placed in an upright or semi-Fowler's position. The cuff is deflated completely, and the securing tape or device is removed. The patient is instructed to take a deep breath or cough as the tube is smoothly withdrawn in one continuous motion. Immediately following extubation, high-flow oxygen is administered via face mask or nasal cannula. Nurses must monitor the patient closely for complications such as laryngospasm, stridor, hoarseness, and respiratory fatigue. Providing humidified oxygen and encouraging deep breathing exercises help restore normal airway function during the post-extubation recovery phase.
