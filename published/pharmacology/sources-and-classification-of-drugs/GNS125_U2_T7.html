Administration of Drugs in Various Forms

Introduction to Drug Administration and Dosage Forms

Drug administration is a core competency for nursing students and professional nurses practicing in clinical environments across Nigeria. Understanding various dosage forms is critical because the physical form of a medication directly dictates how it is administered, absorbed, metabolized, and excreted by the human body. Medications come in multiple states, including solids, liquids, semi-solids, and gases, each designed to target specific physiological systems or achieve localized versus systemic therapeutic effects. For instance, an emergency situation in an accident and emergency department often requires a rapid-onset intravenous route, whereas chronic hypertension is typically managed with sustained-release oral tablets taken at home. Nurses must comprehend the pharmacokinetics associated with each dosage form to ensure safe, effective medication delivery. Furthermore, patient factors such as age, level of consciousness, and gastrointestinal function heavily influence the selection of a specific preparation. Administering a drug in an inappropriate form can lead to therapeutic failure, toxicity, or severe adverse patient outcomes. Therefore, mastering dosage forms forms the foundation of safe pharmacological nursing practice, minimizing medication errors in accordance with global patient safety goals and the guidelines set by regulatory bodies such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria.

Oral Solid Medications: Tablets and Capsules

Oral solid medications, primarily tablets and capsules, are among the most common and convenient forms of drug administration encountered in healthcare settings. Tablets are compressed powders containing active drug substances and excipients, which may be scored for easy splitting, enteric-coated to prevent stomach irritation, or formulated as sustained-release variants for prolonged therapeutic action. Capsules consist of a gelatin shell enclosing either powder, liquid, or tiny granules. When administering oral solids, nurses must adhere to standard aseptic and safety precautions, ensuring the right patient receives the right medication. Crucially, nurses must never crush enteric-coated or sustained-release tablets, as doing so destroys their specialized delivery mechanism, leading to rapid, potentially toxic drug release or inactivation by gastric acid. If a patient has difficulty swallowing, the nurse should consult a pharmacist or physician regarding alternative liquid formulations rather than altering solid dosage forms arbitrarily. Proper documentation of administration, patient education regarding swallowing whole medications with a full glass of water, and monitoring for therapeutic or adverse effects are essential nursing duties. Mastery of these oral administration principles safeguards patients against aspiration and erratic drug absorption.

Liquid Oral and Parenteral Preparations: Solutions

Liquid oral and parenteral preparations, such as solutions, suspensions, and syrups, require precise measurement and specific administration techniques. Solutions are homogeneous mixtures where the drug is completely dissolved, whereas suspensions contain undissolved particles that settle at the bottom and must be thoroughly shaken before measurement to ensure a uniform dose. Nurses must use accurate measuring devices, such as calibrated oral syringes or medicine cups placed on a flat surface at eye level, rather than standard kitchen spoons, which vary widely in volume. For pediatric or geriatric clients, liquid medications offer ease of swallowing compared to large solid pills. Parenteral solutions, administered via subcutaneous, intramuscular, or intravenous routes, bypass the digestive tract entirely, yielding rapid onset of action. This speed makes parenteral preparations invaluable in emergency care, but it also carries heightened risk if errors occur, because once injected, the drug cannot be retrieved. Nurses preparing parenteral solutions must strictly maintain aseptic technique, verify compatibility, and check concentration calculations twice. Understanding the physical characteristics of both oral and parenteral liquids ensures accurate dosing, prevents severe adverse reactions, and promotes optimal patient recovery outcomes in clinical practice.

Topical Preparations: Lotions, Creams, and Ointments

Topical preparations including lotions, creams, and ointments are designed primarily for localized dermatological effects, though some are formulated for systemic absorption via transdermal patches. Lotions are aqueous or hydroalcoholic suspensions applied gently to soothe skin or reduce inflammation without leaving a heavy residue. Creams are semi-solid emulsions containing water and oil, spreading easily and washing off readily with water, making them ideal for weeping or acute skin lesions. Ointments are oil-heavy, semi-solid preparations that provide a greasy barrier, reducing moisture loss from the skin and proving particularly useful for chronic, dry, scaly dermatological conditions. When applying topical medications, nurses must practice meticulous hand hygiene and wear gloves to prevent self-contamination or systemic drug absorption through their own skin. The skin should be cleansed and dried gently before application to enhance drug penetration, and medications should be applied in thin, even layers following the direction of hair growth. Nurses must educate patients not to apply topical steroids or medicated ointments to broken or infected skin unless explicitly ordered by a physician, as this can increase systemic toxicity or spread infection across tissue planes.

Specialized Drug Delivery Systems and Inhalations

Specialized drug delivery systems and inhalations offer targeted therapeutic interventions for respiratory conditions like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, as well as systemic conditions requiring controlled-release transdermal patches. Metered-dose inhalers (MDIs), dry powder inhalers (DPIs), and nebulizers deliver medications directly to the bronchial tree and alveoli, achieving high local concentrations with minimal systemic side effects. When using MDIs, nurses must teach patients the correct technique, which includes shaking the device, exhaling completely, activating the canister while taking a slow, deep breath, and holding that breath for about 10 seconds to allow particulate deposition in the lungs. Using a spacer device with an MDI significantly improves drug delivery to the lower airways while reducing local oral side effects such as candidiasis, especially when using corticosteroid inhalers. Transdermal patches represent another specialized system, releasing medication slowly through the skin into the systemic circulation over hours or days. Nurses applying transdermal patches must rotate application sites to prevent skin irritation, remove old patches before applying new ones, and document placement locations clearly. Mastering these specialized delivery systems ensures therapeutic efficacy and empowers patients to manage chronic conditions safely at home.

Nursing Responsibilities and Safety in Drug Administration

Nursing responsibilities and safety in drug administration revolve around the rigorous application of the 'rights' of medication administration to prevent errors and protect patient welfare. Traditionally comprising the right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, and right documentation, these principles serve as an indispensable barrier against adverse drug events in busy clinical settings. Nurses must independently verify patient identity using at least two identifiers, such as checking the hospital identification band and asking the patient to state their name and date of birth. Calculation of drug dosages must be precise, with pediatric and critical care calculations cross-checked by a colleague when mandated by institutional policy. Furthermore, nurses bear the professional responsibility of patient education, explaining what medication is being given, why it is prescribed, and what potential side effects to report. If a medication error occurs, the nurse's immediate priority is to assess and stabilize the patient, notify the attending physician, and complete an incident report truthfully and promptly in accordance with quality improvement protocols. Upholding these high standards of safety, accountability, and clinical vigilance defines competent professional nursing practice across Nigeria and worldwide.
