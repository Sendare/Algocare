Pressure Sore

Introduction to Pressure Sores

Pressure sores, also known as pressure ulcers, decubitus ulcers, or bedsores, are localized injuries to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, resulting from sustained pressure or pressure combined with shear and friction. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding pressure sores is critical because they represent a significant challenge in hospitalized, immobile, or elderly patients. These lesions not only cause severe physical pain and psychological distress but also prolong hospital stays, increase the risk of systemic infections like sepsis, and elevate healthcare costs. The formation of a pressure sore initiates when external pressure exceeds capillary closing pressure, which typically ranges between 12 to 32 mmHg. When this pressure is sustained, blood flow is restricted, depriving tissues of vital oxygen and nutrients. This leads to local ischemia, cellular hypoxia, and eventually tissue necrosis if pressure is not relieved promptly. Furthermore, moisture, malnutrition, and sensory impairment can accelerate this destructive process. As future professional nurses, students must master the pathophysiology, risk assessment, and proactive management of pressure sores to improve patient outcomes across diverse clinical settings in the Nigerian healthcare system.

Common Pressure Points

Identifying common pressure points is essential for effective nursing assessment and prevention of pressure sores. Pressure points are anatomical areas where bony prominences lie close to the skin surface, making them highly vulnerable to compression against external surfaces such as mattresses, chairs, or medical devices. The specific pressure points depend largely on the patient's primary position. For patients in the supine position, high-risk areas include the occiput, scapulae, elbows, sacrum, heels, and toes. The sacrum and heels are particularly notorious for rapid tissue breakdown due to the heavy weight distribution and constant friction. When the patient is positioned on their side, or in a lateral position, pressure concentrates heavily over the ear, shoulder, greater trochanter of the femur, knees, and lateral malleoli. For patients sitting in chairs or wheelchairs, the ischial tuberosities, sacrum, and coccyx bear the brunt of the body weight. Nursing students must regularly inspect these high-risk anatomical sites during routine bed baths and turning schedules. Recognizing these vulnerable zones allows nurses to implement targeted offloading measures, such as using specialized cushions, pillows, and heel protectors, thereby protecting patients from preventable skin breakdown.

Causes and Risk Factors

The development of pressure sores is multifactorial, involving both primary causes and various contributing risk factors that compromise tissue tolerance. The primary mechanical forces include pressure, friction, and shear. Friction occurs when two surfaces rub together, damaging the epidermal skin layer, while shear happens when skin remains stationary while underlying bone and tissues slide, stretching and kinking blood vessels. Key risk factors that exacerbate these forces include immobility, sensory impairment, malnutrition, and moisture. Immobility leaves patients unable to shift their weight independently, while sensory loss prevents them from feeling the discomfort of sustained pressure. Nutritional deficits, particularly inadequate protein, vitamin C, and zinc intake, impair collagen synthesis and wound healing, reducing tissue tolerance to pressure. Moisture from incontinence, perspiration, or wound drainage causes maceration, softening the skin and making it highly susceptible to friction and bacterial invasion. Other contributing factors include advanced age, altered level of consciousness, vascular disease, and severe comorbidities. Nursing students must comprehensively evaluate these risk factors using validated tools like the Braden Scale to identify vulnerable patients early and institute individualized preventive interventions.

Stages and Assessment of Pressure Sores

Accurate staging and assessment of pressure sores are vital for determining the appropriate treatment plan and monitoring healing progress. The National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) classifies pressure sores into distinct stages based on the depth of tissue destruction. Stage 1 involves non-blanchable erythema of intact skin, indicating potential deep tissue damage. Stage 2 is characterized by partial-thickness skin loss with exposed dermis, presenting as a shallow open ulcer or a blister. Stage 3 involves full-thickness skin loss, where adipose tissue may be visible, but bone, tendon, or muscle are not exposed; slough or eschar may be present. Stage 4 involves full-thickness tissue loss with exposed or directly palpable bone, tendon, or muscle, often accompanied by undermining and tunneling. Additionally, Unstageable pressure injuries involve full-thickness skin and tissue loss where the extent of damage cannot be confirmed because it is obscured by slough or eschar. Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI) presents as persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration of intact or non-intact skin. During assessment, nurses must document the location, size (length, width, depth), presence of exudate, odor, and surrounding skin condition to provide comprehensive clinical handover and track therapeutic efficacy.

Treatment and Wound Management

Effective treatment and wound management of pressure sores require a multidisciplinary approach focused on removing pressure, promoting tissue healing, and preventing infection. The foundational step in management is pressure relief, achieved through frequent repositioning and the use of specialized support surfaces such as air-fluidized beds or high-density foam mattresses. Wound care involves meticulous cleansing, usually with normal saline, to remove debris and bacteria without damaging fragile granulation tissue. Debridement may be necessary to remove necrotic tissue, slough, or eschar, which can be performed mechanically, autolytically, enzymatically, or sharply by trained clinicians. Selecting the appropriate dressing depends on the wound characteristics; moisture-retentive dressings support autolytic debridement and epithelization, while absorbent dressings manage heavy exudate. Nutritional support is equally crucial, requiring high-protein diets enriched with vitamins A and C, zinc, and adequate caloric intake to drive cellular repair. Nurses must also monitor for signs of local or systemic infection, such as purulent drainage, foul odor, increasing pain, or cellulitis, and obtain wound cultures as ordered. Patient education and pain management complete the holistic care plan, ensuring optimal recovery and comfort for the patient.

Prevention Strategies in Nursing Care

Prevention remains the gold standard in nursing care, as preventing a pressure sore is far more effective and less costly than treating one. Comprehensive prevention strategies focus on risk identification, skin care, mechanical offloading, and nutritional optimization. Upon admission and routinely thereafter, nurses should assess all patients using validated risk assessment tools like the Braden Scale. Skin care protocols involve keeping the skin clean and dry, using gentle pH-balanced cleansers, applying barrier creams to protect against moisture from incontinence, and avoiding aggressive massage over bony prominences. Mechanical offloading is implemented through regular repositioning schedules—typically turning bedbound patients at least every two hours and shifting chair-bound patients every hour. Using pillows, foam wedges, and specialized pressure-redistributing mattresses or wheelchair cushions helps distribute body weight evenly. Nutritional status must be closely monitored in collaboration with dietitians, ensuring patients receive adequate hydration and nutrient-dense foods to maintain skin integrity. Furthermore, nursing students must educate patients, family members, and junior staff on the importance of mobility, reporting early signs of skin redness, and adhering to care plans. Through consistent execution of these preventive measures, nurses can safeguard vulnerable patients from the debilitating complications of pressure sores.
