Use of Masks

Introduction to Respiratory Protection in Nursing

Respiratory protection is a cornerstone of infection prevention and control in nursing practice, designed to safeguard both healthcare professionals and patients from the transmission of airborne and droplet pathogens. In environments such as Nigerian hospitals and primary healthcare clinics, where nurses frequently encounter infectious diseases like tuberculosis, Lassa fever, and seasonal respiratory viruses, understanding the appropriate use of personal protective equipment (PPE) is vital. Respiratory hygiene minimizes the spread of microorganisms generated during coughing, sneezing, or medical procedures that create aerosols. Nurses must evaluate exposure risks continuously, adhere strictly to institutional infection control policies, and integrate standard and transmission-based precautions into daily patient care. Mastery of respiratory protection reduces occupational illness and prevents nosocomial infections, ensuring a safer healthcare delivery system for vulnerable populations across diverse clinical settings.

Types of Medical and Surgical Masks

Medical and surgical masks are loose-fitting devices that create a physical barrier between the mouth and nose of the wearer and potential contaminants in the immediate environment. These masks are regulated by fluid resistance standards and are primarily designed to protect against large-particle droplets, splashes, sprays, or splatter that may contain viruses and bacteria. Standard surgical masks do not provide complete protection against aerosolized or very small airborne particles because they lack a tight facial seal, and air can pass through the edges of the mask during breathing. They are typically worn by healthcare workers during surgical procedures, routine examinations, and patient interactions to prevent the expulsion of the wearer's respiratory secretions into the surrounding environment, thereby offering source control. Nurses must distinguish between surgical masks and particulate respirators, recognizing that surgical masks primarily protect others from the wearer and offer limited filtration against fine aerosols.

Understanding Respirators (N95 and Higher)

Respirators, such as N95, FFP2, or higher-level filtering facepiece respirators, are specialized respiratory protective devices designed to achieve a very close facial seal and efficient filtration of airborne particles. Unlike simple surgical masks, N95 respirators filter out at least 95 percent of very small, airborne test particles, including infectious agents like Mycobacterium tuberculosis and aerosolized viral particles generated during medical procedures like intubation or suctioning. Achieving this level of protection requires a proper facial seal, which means that any facial hair, such as beards or thick sideburns, can compromise the integrity of the seal and allow contaminated air to leak inward. Nursing students must understand that respirators provide protection to the wearer by filtering the inhaled air, making them essential for airborne precautions in high-risk infectious disease isolation rooms.

Indications for Mask Usage in Healthcare Settings

Deciding when to use a mask or respirator depends heavily on the mode of disease transmission, the specific clinical task being performed, and current institutional guidelines. Surgical masks are indicated for routine patient care interactions, caring for patients with droplet-transmitted infections such as influenza, and during sterile procedures to protect the sterile field from the healthcare worker's respiratory flora. Conversely, respirators are indicated when caring for patients with confirmed or suspected airborne-transmitted diseases, such as pulmonary tuberculosis, or when performing aerosol-generating procedures (AGPs) like bronchoscopy, endotracheal intubation, or open suctioning of airways. Nurses must assess the clinical environment, review patient diagnosis charts, and apply transmission-based precautions accurately to select the appropriate level of respiratory protection, thereby balancing safety with resource stewardship in resource-constrained Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Proper Technique for Donning and Fit Testing

Proper donning and fit testing are critical steps to ensure that a respirator or mask provides the intended level of protection. Before using an N95 respirator, nurses must undergo initial fit testing—either qualitative or quantitative—to determine the correct model, style, and size that fits their unique facial structure. For daily use, donning must follow a strict sequence: perform thorough hand hygiene, inspect the mask or respirator for any physical defects, position it securely over the nose and mouth, secure the straps around the head or ears, and mold the metallic nosepiece to the bridge of the nose. Crucially, a user seal check must be performed every time a respirator is donned. This involves inhaling and exhaling sharply to check for air leaks around the edges. If air escapes around the nose or cheeks, the respirator must be readjusted until a proper seal is achieved.

Safe Removal and Disposal Procedures

Safe removal (doffing) and disposal procedures are just as important as donning in preventing the self-contamination of healthcare workers and the spread of pathogens in the clinical environment. Masks and respirators should be considered contaminated as soon as they are worn in patient care areas. When doffing, nurses must avoid touching the front or exterior filtering surface of the mask, as it harbors concentrated pathogens. Instead, they should handle the device exclusively by its ear loops, ties, or elastic head bands, pulling it away from the face carefully. Once removed, single-use masks and respirators must be discarded immediately into designated infectious waste bins (such as safety-colored waste receptacles used in Nigerian hospitals) rather than hanging them around the neck or placing them on clean surfaces. Following removal, immediate hand hygiene using soap and water or an alcohol-based hand rub is mandatory to eliminate any transient microorganisms transferred during the process.

Common Mistakes and Infection Control Pitfalls

Despite clear guidelines, infection control breaches involving masks and respirators occur frequently in clinical practice due to fatigue, poor habits, or lack of resources. Common mistakes include wearing a mask under the chin or hanging it around one's neck, touching and adjusting the front of the mask repeatedly while working, wearing a damp or visibly soiled mask for extended periods, and failing to perform hand hygiene before and after touching the device. Reusing single-use respirators inappropriately without following established extended-use or disinfection protocols also drastically increases contamination risks. Furthermore, wearing masks with damaged straps or compromised seals defeats the purpose of respiratory protection. Nursing students must cultivate acute situational awareness, monitor themselves and their peers for compliance errors, and champion strict adherence to infection control standards to protect the integrity of healthcare delivery.
