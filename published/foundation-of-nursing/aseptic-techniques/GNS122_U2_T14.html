Care of Drainage Tubes

Introduction and Purpose of Drainage Tubes

Drainage tubes are critical medical devices used extensively in clinical nursing practice across Nigerian healthcare facilities to remove unwanted fluids, blood, air, or purulent material from body cavities, surgical sites, and wounds. The primary purpose of utilizing a drainage tube is to prevent the dangerous accumulation of fluid or air, which can otherwise lead to hematoma formation, increased tissue pressure, delayed wound healing, and localized or systemic infection. By maintaining an unobstructed exit pathway, drains promote optimal apposition of healing tissues and allow early detection of postoperative complications such as internal hemorrhage or anastomotic leaks through the continuous observation of drained contents. Understanding the underlying physiological and mechanical principles of drainage is essential for student nurses, as timely intervention and accurate assessment directly impact patient recovery outcomes, shorten hospital stays, and minimize morbidity in surgical and trauma patients.

Types of Drainage Systems and Mechanisms

Drainage systems are broadly categorized into open and closed systems, each operating through distinct physical mechanisms. Open systems, such as a traditional Penrose drain, allow fluid to drain passively onto absorbent dressings via capillary action and gravity, which unfortunately carries a higher risk of retrograde bacterial contamination. Conversely, closed drainage systems utilize a sealed, sterile reservoir connected to the tubing, relying on gravity or negative pressure to pull fluids away from the site. Examples of closed systems include Jackson-Pratt (JP) and Hemovac drains, which utilize bulb or spring-loaded mechanisms to create gentle, continuous suction, as well as underwater seal chest drainage systems used for thoracic cavities. Understanding these mechanisms helps nursing students select the appropriate management approach, maintain proper negative pressure where required, and ensure the system remains sealed to protect the sterile internal environment of the patient.

Pre-procedural Preparation and Patient Education

Pre-procedural preparation and patient education are vital components of holistic nursing care before a drainage tube is inserted or managed. The nurse must ensure that informed consent has been obtained by the medical team and prepare the necessary equipment, including the correct drain type, sterile gloves, cleansing solutions, and securement materials. Equally important is preparing the patient psychologically and physically. The nurse should explain the purpose of the drainage tube in simple, reassuring language, detailing what sensations to expect, how to move without dislodging the tube, and how to report pain or unusual feelings immediately. Involving the patient in their care reduces anxiety, promotes cooperation during repositioning or ambulation, and enhances overall safety. Proper pre-procedural assessment also involves checking baseline vital signs, reviewing coagulation profiles, and ensuring the insertion site is clearly identified and prepared according to hospital infection control protocols.

Routine Assessment and Monitoring of Drainage Sites

Routine assessment and continuous monitoring of drainage sites and systems are core responsibilities of the nurse. Every shift, the nurse must inspect the insertion site for signs of localized infection, such as erythema, warmth, swelling, purulent discharge, or foul odor, and check the integrity of surrounding skin for breakdown or maceration. The security of the tube must be verified to prevent accidental dislodgement or tension on internal tissues; sutures and adhesive securement devices must remain intact. Additionally, the nurse must trace the entire length of the tubing to ensure there are no kinks, loops, or dependent loops that could obstruct flow. The collection container must be positioned correctly—either below the level of the wound for gravity drains or properly compressed for suction reservoirs—to ensure optimal functional capacity and prevent fluid backflow into the sterile cavity.

Maintaining Patency: Stripping, Milking, and Flushing

Maintaining the patency of drainage tubes is essential to prevent occlusion from blood clots, tissue debris, or thick secretions, which can cause fluid accumulation and subsequent complications. Techniques such as stripping, milking, and flushing are sometimes employed based on institutional policy and surgeon preference. Stripping involves compressing the tubing with one hand while sliding the other fingers down its length to push clots forward, though this creates high negative pressure and must be performed with caution. Milking involves gentle, intermittent squeezing along segments of the tubing to release blockages without generating excessive suction. Flushing, common in certain surgical or nephrostomy tubes, involves injecting sterile saline under strict aseptic technique to clear obstructions. Nursing students must remember that these interventions should only be performed when specifically ordered, as excessive or aggressive manipulation can damage delicate internal anastomoses and cause severe patient discomfort.

Measuring, Recording, and Assessing Drainage Output

Accurate measurement, recording, and clinical assessment of drainage output are fundamental nursing duties that provide vital diagnostic data regarding a patient's recovery. Output must be measured at regular intervals—typically every shift or more frequently for unstable patients—using a clean, graduated measuring container for each individual drain. The nurse must document the exact volume, color, consistency, and odor of the fluid in the fluid balance chart. For instance, serosanguineous drainage transitioning normally to serous fluid indicates healing, whereas a sudden surge in bright red blood signals active hemorrhage requiring immediate medical notification. Comparing output trends over consecutive shifts helps detect retention of fluid or sudden cessation of drainage, which may indicate a blocked tube. Precise documentation ensures effective interprofessional communication and rapid identification of physiological deterioration.

Infection Control and Dressing Changes

Infection control and proper dressing changes around drainage sites are paramount to prevent catheter-associated infections and localized surgical site complications. Nursing procedures must strictly adhere to aseptic technique. When performing a dressing change, the nurse inspects the wound, removes old soiled dressings carefully to avoid dislodging the tube, and cleans the insertion site using sterile normal saline or prescribed antiseptic solutions, working in a circular motion from the cleanest area (the insertion site) outward. The skin must be thoroughly dried before applying a sterile split gauze or drain sponge and covering it with a transparent occlusive dressing, depending on hospital policy. The nurse should also date, time, and initial all dressings. Frequent monitoring for signs of infection, maintaining closed system integrity, and adhering to hand hygiene before and after handling the drainage system drastically reduce the risk of nosocomial infections.

Troubleshooting Common Complications

Troubleshooting common complications associated with drainage tubes requires quick thinking and sound clinical judgment from the nurse. Common issues include accidental dislodgement, tube occlusion, system disconnection, and fluid leakage around the insertion site. If a closed drain becomes disconnected, the nurse must immediately cleanse the ends with an antiseptic wipe using aseptic technique and reconnect them, or apply a sterile cap if contamination occurred, notifying the physician promptly. In the event of complete accidental removal of a surgical drain, the nurse should place a sterile occlusive dressing over the site, monitor the patient's vital signs for signs of hemorrhage or distress, and notify the surgeon immediately. If fluid leaks around the site, reinforcing the dressing and assessing for internal tube blockage is necessary. Quick identification and management of these complications prevent patient harm and stabilize clinical status.

Removal of Drainage Tubes and Post-Removal Care

The removal of drainage tubes is a significant clinical milestone indicating that the patient's condition has improved and fluid output has decreased to minimal levels as prescribed by the medical team. Post-removal care involves preparing the patient emotionally, gathering necessary supplies such as suture removal kits, sterile dressings, and biohazard waste bags, and premedicating the patient for pain if ordered. During removal, the nurse instructs the patient to take a deep breath and hold it (or perform the Valsalva maneuver), which increases intrathoracic or intra-abdominal pressure and minimizes discomfort as the tube is smoothly withdrawn. The site is immediately covered with a sterile occlusive dressing to prevent air or bacteria entry and to absorb any residual drainage. Post-removal nursing monitoring includes assessing the site frequently for bleeding or leakage, observing for signs of infection, and ensuring patient comfort.
