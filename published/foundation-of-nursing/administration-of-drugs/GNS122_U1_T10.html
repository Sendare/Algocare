Instillation of Drops

Introduction and Purpose of Medication Instillation

Medication instillation is a fundamental nursing skill involving the introduction of liquid medications into body cavities or orifices, most commonly the eyes (ophthalmic), ears (otic), and nose (nasal). The primary purpose of instilling drops is to achieve a localized therapeutic effect, such as reducing intraocular pressure in glaucoma, treating external ear infections, or relieving nasal congestion. Unlike systemic medications that travel through the entire bloodstream, topical drops applied via instillation act directly on the affected tissues, minimizing unwanted systemic side effects and maximizing local bioavailability. For nursing students in Nigeria, mastering this procedure ensures that patients receive accurate, targeted pharmacotherapy while minimizing discomfort and secondary complications. Nurses must understand that while these routes are localized, systemic absorption can still occur, particularly if the medication passes through the nasolacrimal duct into the mucous membranes of the throat. Therefore, precise technique and thorough knowledge of pharmacokinetics are essential for safe administration across diverse clinical settings.

Anatomical Considerations for Eye, Ear, and Nose Drops

Anatomical structures dictate the unique approach required for instilling drops into the eyes, ears, and nose. For the eye, the conjunctival sac is the target cavity because it safely holds medication and prevents direct trauma to the sensitive cornea. The nasolacrimal duct drains fluid from the eye into the nasal cavity, which is why gentle pressure should be applied to prevent unwanted systemic absorption. For the ear (otic canal), the anatomical pathway differs based on age. In adults and children over three years old, the pinna must be pulled upward and backward to straighten the external auditory canal. In infants and children under three, the pinna is pulled downward and straight back due to a shorter, more horizontally aligned canal. For the nasal cavity, drops must be directed toward the olfactory region or mucous membranes while avoiding aspiration into the lower respiratory tract. Understanding these anatomical variations prevents procedural errors, ensures medication reaches the intended target site, and protects delicate structures from mechanical injury during the administration process.

General Nursing Principles and Safety Measures

General nursing principles and safety measures form the bedrock of safe medication administration. Nurses must rigorously adhere to the "rights" of medication administration, including the right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Hand hygiene must be performed before and after the procedure, and clean gloves must be worn to prevent cross-contamination, especially when treating infectious conditions like conjunctivitis or otitis externa. Aseptic technique is critical, meaning the dropper tip must never touch any part of the patient's anatomy or surrounding surfaces to avoid contaminating the remaining medication bottle. Patient identification using at least two unique identifiers is mandatory prior to administration. Furthermore, the nurse must check for any known drug allergies and assess the client's baseline status regarding the target organ. If any adverse reactions or unexpected findings occur during or immediately after the instillation, the nurse must know how to respond promptly, stop the procedure if necessary, and report the event to the prescribing healthcare provider.

Equipment Preparation and Patient Assessment

Before initiating drop instillation, thorough equipment preparation and patient assessment must take place. The nurse must gather the prescribed medication, clean cotton balls or gauze, disposable gloves, and tissues. Checking the medication label against the medication administration record (MAR) at least three times is essential to verify accuracy. The nurse must assess the target area for any signs of infection, inflammation, discharge, structural damage, or pain. For instance, inspecting the tympanic membrane with an otoscope before giving ear drops is vital if a ruptured eardrum is suspected, as instilling fluid into a compromised ear can cause severe inner ear damage or introduce infection. Patient assessment also includes evaluating the client's level of consciousness, ability to follow instructions, and willingness to cooperate. Positioning the patient correctly according to the specific route is planned during this phase. Ensuring the room is well-lit and that the patient is comfortable establishes a cooperative environment that facilitates a smooth and safe procedure.

Procedure for Ophthalmic (Eye) Drop Instillation

The procedure for ophthalmic (eye) drop instillation requires precision and gentle technique to avoid triggering the blink reflex or causing injury. The patient should be placed in a supine or sitting position with the head tilted back slightly and looking up at the ceiling. The nurse uses a tissue or clean cotton ball to gently pull down the lower lid of the affected eye, exposing the conjunctival sac. Holding the dropper approximately one to two centimeters above the sac, the nurse instills the prescribed number of drops into the center of the pocket. The patient is then instructed to gently close their eyes without squeezing them tightly, which helps distribute the medication evenly. To prevent systemic drainage through the nasolacrimal duct, the nurse applies gentle pressure with a clean tissue or gloved finger over the inner canthus for 30 to 60 seconds. If the patient has been prescribed multiple eye drops, a wait time of at least five minutes between medications is strictly observed to prevent the second drop from washing away the first.

Procedure for Otic (Ear) Drop Instillation

Otic (ear) drop instillation requires careful attention to solution temperature and anatomical positioning. Otic solutions should ideally be warmed to room temperature; administering cold ear drops can stimulate the vestibular system in the inner ear, triggering severe vertigo, dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. The patient should be positioned on their side with the affected ear facing upward, or sitting with the head tilted sideways. After ensuring the external auditory canal is clear of excessive cerumen or drainage, the nurse straightens the canal by pulling the pinna upward and backward for adults, or downward and back for young children. The dropper is held just above the ear canal opening without touching the structures. The prescribed number of drops is instilled along the side of the canal rather than directly onto the tympanic membrane to minimize discomfort. Afterward, the patient should remain in the side-lying position for about five to ten minutes, and the nurse can gently press on the tragus of the ear a few times to help work the medication down the canal.

Procedure for Nasal Drop Instillation

Nasal drop instillation is commonly used to treat localized congestion, inflammation, or infection within the nasal passages and sinuses. The patient should be placed in a supine position with the head tilted back, or sitting up with the head hyperextended or tilted backward over a pillow. This specific positioning ensures that the medication flows deeply into the posterior nasal cavity rather than immediately swallowing down the throat or running out of the nose. The nurse instructs the patient to blow their nose gently before the procedure if passages are blocked with mucus. The dropper is inserted approximately one centimeter into the nares, pointing toward the midline of the nose and the superior meatus. After instilling the prescribed drops, the patient must maintain the head-back position for several minutes to allow optimal absorption by the mucosal tissues. The nurse should advise the patient to breathe through their mouth during and immediately after the instillation to prevent sneezing or inhaling the drops directly into the lower respiratory tract.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

While medication instillation is generally safe, potential complications can arise if proper techniques are neglected. Localized irritation, allergic reactions, burning, or stinging can occur upon drug contact with sensitive mucous membranes. In ophthalmic procedures, severe squeezing of the eyelids can expel the medication or cause trauma, while contamination of the dropper bottle can lead to secondary bacterial or viral eye infections such as conjunctivitis. For otic instillations, using cold solutions can induce severe vertigo and nausea, and instilling fluid into an undiagnosed ruptured tympanic membrane can cause agonizing pain and inner ear infections. In nasal drop administration, overuse of decongestant drops can lead to a rebound effect known as rhinitis medicamentosa, where chronic nasal congestion worsens upon cessation of the drug. Nurses must be vigilant in monitoring patients for these complications, stopping administration if adverse effects appear, and providing prompt nursing interventions such as flushing the area with normal saline if ordered and reporting the incident to the physician.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate documentation and comprehensive patient education are the final critical steps in the medication instillation process. Documentation in the patient's medical record or MAR must include the exact date and time of administration, the name of the drug, dosage, specific route (e.g., right eye, left ear, or bilateral nares), and the patient's immediate response or any adverse reactions observed. If the procedure was withheld or modified, the rationale must be clearly recorded. Patient education empowers clients, especially in resource-limited settings where home self-care is vital, to manage their health safely. The nurse must instruct the patient and caregivers on proper hand hygiene, correct head positioning, how to avoid touching dropper tips, and the importance of completing the full course of prescribed therapy, such as antibiotic ear or eye drops. Furthermore, patients should be informed about potential side effects to watch for and when to contact a healthcare provider if symptoms do not improve or worsen.
