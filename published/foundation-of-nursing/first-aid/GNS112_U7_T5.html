Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning and Toxicological Principles

Poisoning is a critical medical emergency defined by the physiological harm caused by the ingestion, inhalation, injection, or absorption of toxic substances. In nursing practice, understanding basic toxicological principles is essential for predicting patient outcomes and guiding rapid interventions. Toxicology evaluates the dose-response relationship, famously summarized by Paracelsus: 'the dose makes the poison.' This principle means that virtually any substance, including therapeutic medications and even water, can act as a poison if the quantity is excessive. Toxicokinetics describes what the body does to a poison, encompassing absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), while toxicodynamics describes what the poison does to the body at the cellular and receptor levels. Nurses must recognize that systemic toxicity depends heavily on the route of exposure, the chemical properties of the toxin, and individual patient factors such as age, underlying organ function, and metabolic rate. Recognizing these foundational concepts allows nursing students to anticipate delayed toxic effects, especially with sustained-release formulations or pro-drugs that require metabolic activation. Furthermore, rapid identification of toxic exposure changes clinical trajectories, making prompt assessment a core competency for all healthcare providers operating in emergency care settings across Nigeria.

Classification and Common Routes of Poisoning

Poisons can be classified in multiple ways, including by their source, chemical structure, or intended use, such as pharmaceuticals, agricultural chemicals, household cleaners, and industrial agents. The route of exposure significantly influences how fast a toxin enters the systemic circulation and manifests clinical symptoms. Common routes include ingestion (oral), inhalation, dermal absorption, ocular contact, and parenteral injection. Oral ingestion is frequently encountered in both accidental pediatric exposures and intentional self-harm among adolescents and adults, with absorption occurring primarily in the stomach and small intestine. Inhalation exposures, such as carbon monoxide or chemical fumes, rapidly cross the alveolar-capillary membrane, leading to swift systemic distribution and immediate central nervous system or respiratory compromise. Dermal and ocular exposures typically result from occupational hazards or household accidents, causing local irritation, chemical burns, or systemic toxicity if the agent is lipophilic enough to cross the skin barrier. Parenteral routes, including intravenous drug abuse or animal envenomation from snakebites, bypass typical absorption barriers, resulting in almost immediate bioavailability and rapid onset of severe systemic manifestations. Understanding these classifications and routes helps nursing professionals anticipate the speed of symptom onset and prepare appropriate personal protective equipment and decontamination strategies.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

The etiology and epidemiology of poisoning vary significantly across global regions due to socioeconomic, occupational, and cultural factors. In the Nigerian context, poisoning cases present unique public health challenges driven by widespread agricultural practices, unregulated local markets, and traditional healthcare practices. Organophosphate and carbamate pesticide poisonings are notably prevalent in rural farming communities where insecticides and herbicides are frequently stored in unlabelled beverage bottles, leading to accidental ingestion by young children or occupational toxicity among farm workers. Another critical local concern is the ingestion of caustic substances, particularly liquid caustic soda or local cleansing agents ('Gogo wash'), often stored inappropriately within households, resulting in severe corrosive esophageal injuries. Additionally, traditional remedies, herbal concoctions, and heavy metal-contaminated preparations contribute significantly to toxicity presentations. Socioeconomic stressors also play a role in intentional self-poisoning incidents, where pharmaceuticals like paracetamol and prescription analgesics or rat poisons containing zinc phosphide are readily accessible over-the-counter. Recognizing these region-specific etiologies empowers Nigerian nursing students to perform targeted patient histories, identify high-risk domestic practices, and advocate for safer storage and handling of toxic substances in their communities.

Pathophysiology and Systemic Effects of Toxins

Toxins exert their harmful effects through diverse pathophysiological mechanisms at the cellular, tissue, and systemic levels. Understanding these mechanisms helps nurses anticipate multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and prioritize supportive interventions. Some poisons act as cellular asphyxiants, such as cyanide and carbon monoxide, which disrupt cellular respiration and prevent oxygen utilization despite adequate arterial oxygen saturation. Other agents, like organophosphests, inhibit acetylcholinesterase, leading to an accumulation of acetylcholine and continuous stimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors, manifesting as excessive secretions, bronchoconstriction, and muscle fasciculations. Corrosive agents cause direct chemical destruction of tissue proteins through coagulation or liquefactive necrosis, threatening airway patency and gastrointestinal perforation. Furthermore, many pharmaceutical agents cause metabolic acidosis, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, or direct myocardial depression, resulting in life-threatening dysrhythmias and hemodynamic instability. Hepatotoxicity, commonly seen in paracetamol overdose, occurs when toxic metabolites deplete hepatic glutathione stores, leading to centrilobular hepatic necrosis. Recognizing these distinct pathways enables nurses to monitor specific laboratory parameters, such as liver function tests, arterial blood gases, and electrolyte panels, ensuring early detection of failing physiological systems.

Clinical Assessment and Toxidromes

Clinical assessment of the poisoned patient begins with a rapid primary survey focusing on the airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure (ABCDE) framework. When a specific toxin cannot be readily identified, nurses must look for 'toxidromes'—constellations of signs and symptoms that strongly suggest a specific class of poison. Recognizing a toxidrome allows for presumptive treatment before definitive laboratory screening results are available. Common toxidromes include the cholinergic toxidrome (characterized by Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, Gastrointestinal distress, and Emesis—SLUDGE, alongside miosis and bradycardia), the anticholinergic toxidrome (manifested by dry skin, blurred vision, delirium, tachycardia, and mydriasis, often remembered as 'blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone'), sympathomimetic toxidromes (hypertension, tachycardia, agitation, and mydriasis), and sedative-hypnotic toxidromes (central nervous system depression, hypoventilation, and decreased reflexes). A thorough history from bystanders, family members, or emergency responders is crucial, alongside examining pockets, bags, or pill bottles brought with the patient. Nurses must also conduct serial neurological evaluations, monitor vital signs continuously, and assess for hidden traumatic injuries that may have occurred during the poisoning incident.

Emergency Management and Decontamination

Emergency management of poisoning prioritizes immediate stabilization of vital functions followed by interventions to limit further absorption and enhance elimination of the toxin. The resuscitation phase supersedes all decontamination efforts; establishing a patent airway, providing supplemental oxygen, supporting ventilation, and maintaining hemodynamic stability are paramount. Once the patient is stabilized, gastrointestinal or external decontamination may be considered based on the time elapsed since exposure and the nature of the poison. Activated charcoal is frequently administered orally or via a nasogastric tube to adsorb various toxins within the gastrointestinal tract, though it is ineffective against heavy metals, alcohols, and corrosive agents, and is contraindicated if the airway is unprotected or if a corrosive ingestion has occurred. Whole bowel irrigation using polyethylene glycol electrolyte solutions may be utilized for body packers or sustained-release ingestions. Gastric lavage is now performed less frequently due to high risks of aspiration and esophageal perforation, reserved only for life-threatening ingestions presenting within a very narrow timeframe. For dermal or ocular exposures, copious irrigation with water or normal saline for at least 15 to 30 minutes is the primary decontamination method. Nurses play a vital role during these procedures by monitoring airway reflexes, preparing suction equipment, and ensuring patient safety.

Pharmacological Antidotes and Supportive Care

While supportive care remains the cornerstone of toxicology management, specific pharmacological antidotes can dramatically alter the clinical course by directly neutralizing toxins, blocking their receptor sites, or enhancing their elimination. Nurses must be highly knowledgeable about common antidotes, their indications, dosages, and potential adverse effects. For instance, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is the definitive antidote for paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose, working by replenishing hepatic glutathione stores to prevent severe hepatotoxicity, ideally administered within eight hours of ingestion. Naloxone is a competitive opioid receptor antagonist used to reverse life-threatening respiratory depression caused by opioid toxicity, requiring careful titration to prevent acute withdrawal syndromes. Atropine acts as a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, reversing the excessive secretions and bronchoconstriction seen in organophosphate poisoning, often paired with pralidoxime to reactivate acetylcholinesterase. Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepine-induced sedation, though it must be used cautiously in chronic users due to the risk of precipitating status epilepticus. Supportive care runs concurrently with antidote administration, involving aggressive fluid resuscitation, temperature regulation, correction of electrolyte and acid-base disturbances, and seizure management using benzodiazepines. Nurses monitor hemodynamic responses and document serial clinical changes following antidote administration.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care for the poisoned patient extends beyond emergency stabilization into continuous, rigorous monitoring and psychosocial support. A structured nursing care plan begins with nursing diagnoses such as 'Ineffective Breathing Pattern related to central nervous system depression', 'Risk for Aspiration', 'Impaired Oral Mucous Membrane' (in caustic ingestions), and 'Risk for Self-Directed Violence' (in intentional poisoning). Continuous physiological monitoring is mandatory, encompassing cardiac telemetry for dysrhythmias, pulse oximetry, frequent neurological assessments using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), strict fluid balance charting, and regular temperature checks to identify malignant hyperthermia or hypothermia. For patients with altered mental status or corrosive injuries, nurses must implement meticulous oral hygiene, positioning adjustments to prevent pressure injuries, and frequent airway suctioning. In cases of intentional self-harm, safety precautions and constant observation are vital to prevent secondary attempts while in the healthcare facility. Furthermore, nurses must coordinate multidisciplinary psychiatric evaluations, social services, and discharge counseling before the patient leaves the hospital, ensuring that underlying stressors and support systems are adequately addressed.

Prevention, Education, and Public Health Strategies

Prevention, education, and public health strategies are the most effective long-term measures for reducing the burden of morbidity and mortality related to poisoning. Nursing professionals play a pivotal role in community advocacy and health education, targeting common household, agricultural, and occupational hazards. Education campaigns should emphasize safe storage practices, such as keeping medications and cleaning agents in locked cabinets out of the reach of children, and strongly discouraging the transfer of chemicals or pesticides into empty food or beverage bottles. Public health outreach should also address safe disposal of expired or unused household pharmaceuticals rather than keeping them in home medicine chests. In agricultural settings, nurses can collaborate with local leaders and cooperatives to promote the proper use of personal protective equipment (PPE) during pesticide mixing and application, as well as establishing clear re-entry intervals for treated farmlands. Furthermore, healthcare facilities must maintain accessible poison information resources and emergency contact numbers for regional poison control centers. By engaging in community awareness programs, school health education, and policy advocacy, nurses help build resilient communities capable of preventing toxic exposures before they occur.
